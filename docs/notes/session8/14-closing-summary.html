Session 8. Quantum Magnetism, Spinons and Kondo Physics

8.14 Closing summary

We started from what we built before. These were the mean-field method of Section 1.12, the Heisenberg model (6.5) from the Hubbard dimer of Section 6.8, the magnons and the Holstein-Primakoff transformation of Section 6.14, and the singlet (7.3) of Session 7. In this session we saw the following.

  • Magnetism, in all the forms that we saw in Session 1, Session 6 and in this session, comes from strong repulsive interactions between electrons.

  • Frustrated magnetic models, such as those on triangular and kagome lattices of Section 8.4, can show quantum spin liquid behavior. This is a highly entangled ground state with zero local spin expectation value on every site, as already for the singlet (8.3) and the valence bond solid (8.5). The RVB state (8.6) to (8.7) goes beyond both, because its singlets resonate over every pairing of the frustrated lattice. Conventional mean-field theory cannot describe it by construction.

  • The zero magnetization of a QSL is the opposite regime from the one that Holstein-Primakoff needs (8.9). For this reason, its fundamental excitations are not magnons but spinons, with \(S=1/2\). These are emergent fermions without charge that we obtain from the Abrikosov or parton construction, (8.10) to (8.15). This construction is the mean-field method for electrons of Session 1 and Session 6, now applied to the spin sector. Spinon bands can be gapless, gapped, or of Dirac type, as in Section 8.9.

  • The parton construction has an emergent gauge redundancy (8.16). The mean-field bond variable is a link field, and its topological defects are gauge excitations called visons, which are in general coupled to the spinons, as in Section 8.10.

  • Quantum entanglement that mixes spin and fermion degrees of freedom also appears in Kondo systems. The same parton method, applied to a lattice of local moments inside a metal, (8.17) to (8.21), turns the Kondo exchange coupling into a mean-field hybridization \(\gamma_K\). This hybridization opens a gap in the conduction band at the anti-crossing and turns a metal into a Kondo insulator, also called a heavy-fermion insulator. The gap grows monotonically with the strength of the Kondo coupling, as in Section 8.13.

Question

Which excitation carries spin \(1/2\), no charge, and appears in the parton construction of a quantum spin liquid?

  1. The spinon.
  2. The magnon.
  3. The vison.
Show the answers
  • A. ✓ True

    This is true. The spinon is the fermion of the parton construction, which carries the spin of the electron. The magnon and the triplon have spin \(1\), and the vison is a flux of the gauge field with no spin.

  • B. ✗ False

    This is false. Flipping one spin of a ferromagnet changes \(S^z\) by \(1\). Its spin is \(1\) and not \(1/2\).

  • C. ✗ False

    This is false. The vison is a plaquette with flux \(\Phi=-1\) and it is a spin singlet. It carries no spin at all.

Where this comes back
  • In Section 9.9 the Abrikosov fermions and spinons of this session are used again for a single magnetic impurity in a quantum magnet, with the same mathematics as the Friedel oscillations of Section 9.6.
  • In Section 9.3 the Kondo effect is a case where linear response fails, since the Kondo temperature is non-perturbative in the coupling.
  • In Section 10.6b a gauge theory with spins on the links comes back, with the plaquette product as the quantity that a local gauge flip does not change.
