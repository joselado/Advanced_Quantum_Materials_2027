6.1 Two classes of magnets and their excitations

Which kinds of magnets exist, and what is the elementary excitation of each kind? We will see that there are two big classes of magnetic systems. The magnets that break time-reversal symmetry have neutral excitations of spin 1. The quantum spin liquids, which are among the quantum magnets, have neutral excitations of spin \(1/2\). This section first previews material of Session 8, and only then the session moves to its own topic, which is the first class.

The first class contains the magnets that break time-reversal symmetry. These materials have a net magnetization on every site, or a staggered one. A finite spin angular momentum makes a system look different when we play its movie backwards, which is the definition of broken time-reversal symmetry. Ferromagnets, antiferromagnets and noncollinear spin spirals are in this class. Iron and cobalt are concrete examples of this family. In this session we spend all our time on this class.

The second class contains the magnets that do not break time-reversal symmetry, at least not classically. These are quantum magnets, and quantum spin liquids are among them. They have a local moment on every site, but the spins are in a quantum superposition and do not form a static classical arrangement, so time-reversal symmetry does not need to be broken overall. A related case, which we only mention briefly here, is the heavy-fermion Kondo system of Section 8.11. It is a combination of local moments in a quantum superposition with a sea of itinerant metallic electrons. Typical materials of the three families are iron for the family of ferromagnets and antiferromagnets, herbertsmithite for quantum spin liquids and samarium hexaboride SmB\(_6\) for heavy-fermion insulators.

Each class has its own emergent excitation. Magnets that break time-reversal symmetry have magnons, which are spin-1 and charge-neutral fluctuations of the order parameter, and they are the direct magnetic analogue of a phonon. Magnons have no charge, so an electric field does not push them as it pushes an electron, and they can propagate in materials that are electronic insulators. A ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic Mott insulator does not let any current through, but a magnon can still carry spin and heat across it.

Quantum magnets have instead spinons, which are spin-\(1/2\) and charge-neutral excitations. They are sometimes called half an electron, since the spin and the charge degrees of freedom of the electron have in practice separated. In the rest of this session we will construct magnons in detail. Spinons and the Kondo lattice model only appear here as a name and a spin quantum number. Their full construction is in Session 8, in Section 8.9 for spinons and in Section 8.11 for the Kondo lattice.

Question

A Mott insulator carries no charge current. Which statement about magnons in it is correct?

  1. Magnons cannot exist, since they need free charge carriers.
  2. Magnons are neutral spin-1 excitations, so they carry spin and heat but no charge.
  3. Magnons carry spin \(1/2\) and charge \(e\).
Show the answers
  • A. ✗ False

    This is false. Take the Hubbard dimer at half filling and large \(U\). Its triplet lies above the singlet by \(J=4t^2/U\), and it is a magnon-like excitation that moves no charge, since every site keeps one electron.

  • B. ✓ True

    This is true. A magnon is a fluctuation of the order parameter, created by \(S^-\), so the number of electrons does not change. It carries one unit of spin and the energy of the excitation.

  • C. ✗ False

    This is false. Applying \(S^-\) to one site changes \(S^z\) by \(1\) and leaves the number of electrons the same. The excitation is therefore neutral with spin \(1\), and a charge \(e\) would need to add or remove an electron.

Where this comes back
  • In Section 8.1 the two classes of magnets come back, and the spinon of a quantum spin liquid is built as half a magnon.
  • In Section 8.4 frustration is the route to a quantum spin liquid, with herbertsmithite on the kagome lattice as the best-known candidate material.
  • In Section 8.13 the Kondo lattice gives the heavy-fermion Kondo insulator, with SmB\(_6\) as the example.
