# Superconductivity, Nambu Representation and Majorana Physics {#session-5}

At the end of Session 4 we kept in mind the kind of fractionalization that we had built there, and we pointed forward to a second example of it, which looks quite different. In a partially filled topological flat band, correlations split the charge of the electron and make its exchange statistics anyonic. In a superconductor, as we said there, pairing correlations instead split the electron into two Majorana halves. In this session we will work out this second case in full. As we will state carefully below, superconductors and fractional quantum Hall states are two correlated states that both host fractional excitations, but through opposite mechanisms. The first one has a mean-field description and needs an attractive interaction, and the second one has no mean-field description and needs a strong repulsive interaction. The idea behind both is the same, which is a correlated state with excitations that are not the electron itself, but the two routes to it are really different. In this session we will build all the tools for the superconducting route.

These tools also complete a topic that we left open before. In session 1 we built mean-field theory as a general tool and then applied it quickly to three symmetry-broken states, a charge density wave, a magnet and, as our third and shortest example, a superconductor. There we started from an attractive Hubbard interaction and took an expectation value of two annihilation operators instead of one creation and one annihilation operator. With this we defined the pairing order $\Delta =
\langle c_i c_j\rangle$ (session 1, eq. [1.9](#eq-1-9)), and we showed that this term gets a phase $e^{2i\varphi}$ under a gauge transformation, while every ordinary bilinear term stays invariant. This means that a superconductor spontaneously breaks gauge symmetry, and with it the fixed electron number. That first treatment was short on purpose. At that time we said that magnetism and superconductivity would each get \"a full, much deeper treatment in their own dedicated sessions later in the course,\" and in this session it is the turn of superconductivity. Magnetism is moved once more to the next recording, which is session 6 in the plan of this course.

So in this session we take up this topic again and build the full set of tools around it. First, we need a new representation, the Nambu spinor, since without it we cannot even diagonalize a Hamiltonian that mixes creation and annihilation operators in the way a pairing term does. This representation gives us the Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian, which is the central object of the whole session. We will use it to classify superconducting order by symmetry, into singlet and triplet and into s-, p- and d-wave. Then we will show that two superconductors can have the same spectrum and still have a completely different topology, and to distinguish them we will use the same Chern-number tools as in the topological-band-structure and quantum-Hall sessions. We will follow this topological difference down to its strongest consequence, which is the Kitaev chain. We will see that this model is the SSH model from the topological-band-structure session written in terms of Majorana operators, and that its topological zero modes are their own antiparticles. At the end we will ask what these Majorana modes can be used for, which is topological quantum computing. We will also point to session 6, which does for magnetism what we are about to do for superconductivity, and this is the one point of the course where the lecture explicitly leaves a question open for the next recording. The same interacting Hamiltonian can give, at the mean-field level, either a superconductor or a magnet, and magnetism is the topic that we moved to the next recording.
