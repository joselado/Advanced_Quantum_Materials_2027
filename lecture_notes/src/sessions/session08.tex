\chapter{Quantum Magnetism, Spinons and Kondo Physics}

In Session 6 we only studied magnets that break time-reversal symmetry, which are ferromagnets and
antiferromagnets. In the last part of that session we used the Holstein-Primakoff transformation to
write these ordered states in terms of bosons, the magnons, and we went as far as a topological
magnon band structure with its own chiral edge modes. That session stopped suddenly, at
``topological magnons,'' without a final recap. It also left one open point. There was a short
remark about Kitaev-type interactions, which were called ``important for quantum spin liquids'' at
that time, but we did not continue with them. In this session we come back to that point, as a lecture
of its own, with its own introduction and its own plan. First, we will see minimal models of quantum magnetism. Then we will see the excitations that
quantum spin liquids (QSLs) have, which are spinons and gauge excitations. At the end we will study
Kondo systems and Kondo lattice models, which are a very different physical system that can be
solved with the same method. The idea that connects the whole session is the same idea that we
have used in this course since Session 1. We take a many-body Hamiltonian that mean-field theory
cannot treat, we write it with the right auxiliary particles, and we do mean-field theory on
\emph{that} problem.

In this session we use $\hbar = 1$ for the spin operators, which is the same convention that we
already used without saying it in Session 6.

\section{Two classes of magnets and where we find them}

In the language of this lecture, every magnetic material belongs to one of two classes.
\textbf{Classical magnets} break time-reversal symmetry, and examples are conventional
ferromagnets, antiferromagnets and spin spirals. For the electrons, these magnets can be described
with mean-field theory, in the same way as we did in Sessions 1 and 6. Most magnetic materials in
nature are of this type, for example iron, copper oxide, or any material with a well-defined
N\'eel temperature.

\textbf{Quantum magnets} are the opposite case. They cannot be described with mean-field theory in
terms of electrons, and their ground states have long-range entanglement. This entanglement is not
a small detail, since it gives rise to excitations that do not exist in a symmetry-broken magnet.
In this session we will study two physical realizations of quantum magnetism, which are quite
different from each other.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Quantum spin liquids (QSLs)}, which are the first class that we build in detail. The
  best-known candidate material is herbertsmithite, a compound where the magnetic ions form a
  kagome lattice of spin-$1/2$ sites.
\item \textbf{Heavy-fermion Kondo insulators}, which are often rare-earth compounds, and the
  standard example is samarium hexaboride (SmB$_6$). We build the theory for this class in the
  second half of the session.
\end{itemize}

\section{Two emergent excitations, magnons and spinons}

The strong many-body interactions between electrons are the origin of the emergent collective
excitations of magnetic materials. In a symmetry-broken magnet, this collective excitation is the
\textbf{magnon}, which has spin $1$ and no charge. The reason is that the strong repulsion between
electrons freezes the charge degree of freedom completely, so only the spin can fluctuate. In a
quantum magnet, the excitation is of a different kind. It is the \textbf{spinon}, which has spin
$1/2$ and also no charge. It is useful to think of a spinon as the spin half of an electron. If we
take this picture one step further, we obtain the standard language of fractionalization for a QSL,
where the electron splits into a \textbf{chargon}, which has the charge, and a \textbf{spinon},
which has the spin.

There is a simple argument with pictures that shows why the spin of the two excitations differs by
exactly a factor of two. Let us take a ferromagnet, where the ground state has all spins up,
$|{\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow\cdots}\rangle$. If we flip one spin to build an excited state, the
total spin of the excited state differs from the one of the ground state by $\Delta S = 1$, and
this is a local magnon. Let us now take a quantum spin liquid, where the ground state is a
collection of singlets everywhere and no spin points in a specific direction. If we build an
excited state by leaving one spin without a partner, instead of putting it in a singlet, the
difference in total spin is $\Delta S = 1/2$, and in this picture this is a spinon. This picture is
too simple on purpose, since real spinons are more subtle than ``an unpaired spin,'' but it
is the right first intuition for why symmetry-broken magnets and quantum spin liquids have
fundamentally different excitations. Figure~\ref{fig:s08:magnonspinon} shows the two counting
arguments next to each other.

\section{Two minimal dimers, a classical order parameter and a quantum singlet}

The simplest way to start with quantum magnetism is to look first at the smallest possible magnet,
which has two spins, and to compare two different Hamiltonians for it.

The first one is the \textbf{Ising dimer},

\begin{equation}
\mathcal H_\text{Ising} = S_0^z S_1^z,
\end{equation}

which couples only the $z$-components of two spin-$1/2$'s. This Hamiltonian has two degenerate
ground states, which are related by time reversal.

\begin{equation*}
|GS_1\rangle = |{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle, \qquad |GS_2\rangle = |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle.
\end{equation*}

Both are eigenstates with the same energy, but each one of them \emph{alone} breaks time-reversal
symmetry, since time reversal flips every spin and so it turns $|GS_1\rangle$ into $|GS_2\rangle$
and the other way around. Only the degenerate pair of the two states together respects the
symmetry. A macroscopic symmetry-broken antiferromagnet, of the same kind as the one we built in
Session 6, is the version of this same problem with an infinite number of sites, and the same
argument also works for ferromagnets and spin spirals.

Let us now replace the Ising coupling by the full \textbf{quantum Heisenberg dimer}, which has
rotational symmetry,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal H_\text{Heis} = \mathbf S_0 \cdot \mathbf S_1.
\end{equation}

This is the model that we obtain from a half-filled Hubbard model with strong interactions in the
large-$U$ limit, as we derived in Session 1. When we solve it with $\mathbf S_0, \mathbf S_1$ as
real spin-$1/2$ operators, the two spin-$1/2$'s combine into a singlet ($S=0$) and a triplet
($S=1$). The value is $\mathbf S_0\cdot\mathbf S_1 = -3/4$ for the singlet and $+1/4$ for the
triplet, so the unique ground state is the \textbf{singlet},

\begin{equation}
|GS\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle -
|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\Big), \qquad \langle \mathbf S_i \rangle = 0.
\end{equation}

This ground state is different from the Ising case in three ways, and these three points are
important for the rest of the session. First, it is the \emph{unique} ground state, so there is no
degeneracy and no spontaneous choice between states. Second, it is \emph{time-reversal symmetric},
since its net magnetization is exactly zero and time reversal maps $|GS\rangle$ to itself. Third,
it is \emph{maximally entangled}, since the two terms in (8.3) have the same weight, which is the
largest entanglement that two qubits can have. Can we have a macroscopic version of this ground
state? This question is the origin of quantum spin liquids.

\section{Frustration as the route to a quantum spin liquid}

Before we build a macroscopic version of (8.3), we need to know when the ground state of a
Heisenberg model stays time-reversal symmetric in this way, instead of ordering spontaneously as
the magnets of Session 1 and Session 6 did. The general rule is \textbf{frustration}. The standard
example is a triangular plaquette with antiferromagnetic coupling on all three bonds. Any two spins
can point in opposite directions, but then the third bond is between two spins that are already
antiparallel to each other, so no spin configuration can satisfy all three bonds at the same time.
This geometric frustration gives many classical ground states that are degenerate, which means that
they have the same energy and different spin configurations. When we add quantum fluctuations, they
mix these degenerate configurations into one quantum superposition, which in general does not break
time-reversal symmetry.

This is the reason why the \textbf{triangular} and \textbf{kagome} lattices, which are both built
only from triangles, are the standard place to look for quantum spin liquid physics. An
antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model on either lattice is a real candidate for a QSL ground state, in
the right range of couplings. This is also the reason why herbertsmithite, a compound with
spin-$1/2$ sites on a kagome lattice, is the best-known real candidate material.
Figure~\ref{fig:s08:frustration} shows the geometry behind the whole argument, which is a plaquette
without frustration, a frustrated triangle with its degenerate configurations, and the kagome
lattice that is built only from such triangles.

\section{The dimerized Heisenberg model, a first step before a spin liquid}

Before we study a frustrated Hamiltonian, it is useful to build the simplest possible
\emph{macroscopic} quantum magnet and to check explicitly that its ground state does what (8.3)
told us. Let us take a \textbf{fully dimerized Heisenberg model}, which is made of dimers that are
completely decoupled from each other, placed on a chain or on a two-dimensional lattice,

\begin{equation}
H = \sum_n J\, \mathbf S_{2n}\cdot \mathbf S_{2n+1}, \qquad J>0.
\end{equation}

Since every dimer is decoupled from its neighbors, we can write the many-body ground state
exactly, as a product of singlets over the pairs of the dimers,

\begin{equation}
|\Psi\rangle \sim \bigotimes_n \Big(|{\uparrow_{2n}\downarrow_{2n+1}}\rangle -
|{\downarrow_{2n}\uparrow_{2n+1}}\rangle\Big), \qquad \langle \mathbf S_i\rangle = 0 \text{ on every
site}.
\end{equation}

In one sense this is a trivial Hamiltonian, a collection of independent zero-dimensional
problems. But it already shows something important. A macroscopic Heisenberg
Hamiltonian \emph{can} have a ground state that does not break time-reversal symmetry, and this
state is the direct generalization of the two-site singlet (8.3) to an extensive system. If we add
small couplings between the dimers, the state changes smoothly and it still does not break
time-reversal symmetry.

There is, however, an important difference to make here, because this state is \emph{not yet} a
quantum spin liquid. Its fundamental excitations still have spin $1$. If we turn one singlet into
its triplet partner, we obtain a \textbf{triplon}, which is a local transition from singlet to
triplet, and it has the same structure as a magnon, with only a different name. A state that is
built in this way, from a fixed and static pattern of singlets, is called a \textbf{valence bond
solid (VBS)}. To obtain a real quantum spin liquid, with spin-$1/2$ excitations instead of spin-$1$
triplons, we need more than a static dimer covering. We need the singlets to fluctuate and to
resonate over the whole lattice, and this is the frustrated case that we study in the next section.
Figure~\ref{fig:s08:vbsrvb} shows this difference explicitly.

\section{The resonating valence bond wavefunction as the QSL ground state}

For a frustrated Heisenberg Hamiltonian $\mathcal H = \sum_{ij} J_{ij}\, \mathbf
S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$, we do not expect the ground state to be one fixed dimer covering as in
(8.5). Instead, we expect a \textbf{linear combination of singlets over every possible pairing of
sites}, where each pairing has its own amplitude, which is a complicated many-body wavefunction. We
could try to treat this directly, for example by writing it as a tensor network as in Session 7 for
the general strongly correlated case. There is, however, a way that is easier to understand
physically, which is a variational ansatz based on an analogy with superconductivity.

The idea is that the singlets in a QSL are similar to the Cooper pairs in an s-wave
superconductor, since both are singlet correlations between pairs. A BCS-type wavefunction is easy
to write at the mean-field level in the right basis, which is the Nambu space as in Session 5. So we
start from a BCS-paired state built from the same auxiliary fermions $f_{i\sigma}$ that we will
justify in detail in Section 8.8,

\begin{equation}
|\Psi_\text{BCS}\rangle = \prod_{\mathbf k}\Big(u_{\mathbf k} + v_{\mathbf k}\,
f^\dagger_{\mathbf k\uparrow} f^\dagger_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}\Big)|0\rangle,
\end{equation}

where $u_{\mathbf k}, v_{\mathbf k}$ are variational parameters, as in the BCS state of Session 5.
By itself, (8.6) is a superposition of states with every possible number of fermions per site,
which can be empty, singly occupied or doubly occupied. This is not a physical spin state at all.
A QSL has strong local interactions, so we need single occupation on every site, and no charge
fluctuations between sites are allowed. We impose this condition with a \textbf{Gutzwiller
projector} onto the singly-occupied subspace,

\begin{equation}
|\Psi_\text{RVB}\rangle = P_G\,|\Psi_\text{BCS}\rangle, \qquad P_G = \prod_i n_i(2-n_i), \qquad
n_i = \sum_\sigma f^\dagger_{i\sigma}f_{i\sigma},
\end{equation}

which gives $1$ for states with exactly one fermion per site and $0$ for all other states. What
remains is a real many-body spin wavefunction. It is a superposition over the different ways to
pair sites into singlets, in the same spirit as the singlet of a single dimer (8.3), but now over a
whole frustrated lattice. The name of this construction describes what it is, and it is called the
\textbf{resonating valence bond (RVB)} variational wavefunction. It turns out to be a good
description of the physics of quantum spin liquids. Figure~\ref{fig:s08:vbsrvb} shows what makes
this resonating state, and the excitations that it has, different from the frozen dimer covering of
Section 8.5.

\section{Why the Holstein-Primakoff magnon approach fails for QSL excitations}

It is hard to write the exact QSL ground state, so the natural next step, as in Session 6, is to
ask about the excitations \emph{on top of} the ground state, and not about the ground state itself.
Up to now, the only tool that we have for this is the \textbf{Holstein-Primakoff transformation},
which writes

\begin{equation}
S_i^z = S - a_i^\dagger a_i, \qquad S_i^+ \approx \sqrt{2S}\,a_i, \qquad S_i^- \approx
\sqrt{2S}\,a_i^\dagger,
\end{equation}

where we linearized under the assumption that the number of bosonic magnons is small,
$\langle a_i^\dagger a_i\rangle \ll S$. This is a very good approximation close to a fully or
nearly polarized magnet, which is the regime where we used it in Session 6.

A QSL is in the opposite limit. Its defining property, $\langle \mathbf S_i\rangle = 0$ on every
site, gives through (8.8)

\begin{equation}
\langle a_i^\dagger a_i \rangle = S,
\end{equation}

which is the regime where the condition of a small boson density, the basis of the linearized
Holstein-Primakoff mapping, fails completely. For a QSL there is no ordered
reference state that it is ``close to'' and around which we can expand. As a result, the magnon
method that worked for the ordered magnets of Session 6 is not the right tool here, and we need a
different approximation for the excitations of a quantum spin liquid.

\section{The parton construction with Abrikosov fermions and the single-occupancy constraint}

The construction that allows us to treat QSL excitations does not write each spin as a bosonic
fluctuation around an ordered reference state. Instead, it writes each spin with an auxiliary
\textbf{fermion} field $f_{i\sigma}$ ($\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow$) on every site. This is a
particle with spin $1/2$ and no electric charge, which is the spinon of Section 8.2, now written in
a formal way. These particles are called \textbf{Abrikosov fermions}, and the operator identity
that defines the parton transformation is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf S_i = \frac12 \sum_{\alpha\beta} f_{i\alpha}^\dagger\, \boldsymbol\sigma_{\alpha\beta}\,
f_{i\beta},
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is the vector of Pauli matrices. One can check directly, from the
anticommutation relations of the fermions, that (8.10) gives the correct $SU(2)$ spin algebra
$[S_i^a,S_i^b] = i\varepsilon^{abc}S_i^c$ on the full Fock space of
$f_{i\uparrow},f_{i\downarrow}$, which has four states, namely empty, singly occupied with spin up,
singly occupied with spin down and doubly occupied. No extra input is needed for this. The problem
is that this transformation makes the local Hilbert space \emph{artificially larger}, since a
physical spin-$1/2$ has only two states, while $f_{i\uparrow},f_{i\downarrow}$ give four. We remove
the two extra unphysical states, which are the empty and the doubly occupied ones and which (8.10)
maps to $\mathbf S_i=0$ in any case. We do this by imposing by hand the
\textbf{single-occupancy constraint}

\begin{equation}
\sum_\sigma f_{i\sigma}^\dagger f_{i\sigma} = 1 \qquad \text{at every site } i.
\end{equation}

This is the fermionic analogue of the Gutzwiller projection (8.7) that we applied before to the
many-body wavefunction. To project the many-body state onto the singly-occupied subspace and to fix
the fermion filling to one per site are the same statement, the first one seen from the side of
the wavefunction and the second one from the side of the operators.
Figure~\ref{fig:s08:parton}a shows which two states the constraint has to remove, and why they were
never spin states in the first place.

When (8.11) holds, (8.10) is an \emph{exact} way to write a spin-$1/2$ operator in the language of
fermions, and we can put it directly into the Heisenberg Hamiltonian $\mathcal H=\sum_{\langle
ij\rangle}J_{ij}\,\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$. With the Fierz identity for Pauli matrices
$\sum_a \sigma^a_{\alpha\beta}\sigma^a_{\gamma\delta} = 2\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma} -
\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\gamma\delta}$ and the constraint (8.11) on both sites, we obtain the
simple identity

\begin{equation}
\mathbf S_i \cdot \mathbf S_j = \frac14 - \frac12\, \chi_{ij}^\dagger \chi_{ij}, \qquad \chi_{ij}
\equiv \sum_\sigma f_{i\sigma}^\dagger f_{j\sigma},
\end{equation}

where $\chi_{ij}$ is the spinon bond operator, or hopping operator, between sites $i$ and $j$. When
we insert (8.12), the original Heisenberg Hamiltonian, which is bilinear in the spins, becomes a
Hamiltonian that is \emph{quartic} in the auxiliary fermions,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal H = \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} J_{ij}\left(\frac14 - \frac12\,\chi_{ij}^\dagger\chi_{ij}\right)
= \text{const} - \frac12\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} J_{ij}\, \chi_{ij}^\dagger \chi_{ij}.
\end{equation}

This is the same problem that we had in Sessions 1 and 6 with the Hubbard interaction,
which is a quartic term that we cannot diagonalize directly. We treat
it in the same way, with a \textbf{mean-field decoupling},

\begin{equation}
\chi_{ij}^\dagger\chi_{ij} \;\approx\; \langle\chi_{ij}^\dagger\rangle\,\chi_{ij} +
\chi_{ij}^\dagger\,\langle\chi_{ij}\rangle - |\langle\chi_{ij}\rangle|^2.
\end{equation}

\section{The mean-field spinon Hamiltonian and its dispersion classes}

We put the couplings and the expectation value of (8.14) together into one mean-field parameter
$\chi_{ij}$ per bond, and if needed we impose the constraint (8.11) on average with a Lagrange
multiplier. In this way, the mean-field decoupling of Section 8.8 turns the original spin problem
into a real Hamiltonian for the auxiliary fermions, which has the form of a tight-binding model and
which we can diagonalize,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal H_\text{MF} = \sum_{ij,s} \chi_{ij}\, f^\dagger_{i,s} f_{j,s}.
\end{equation}

This mean-field step is different from all the other mean-field decouplings of this course up
to now, since it does not need to break time-reversal symmetry. If we take the same $\chi_{ij}$ for spin up and spin down, we keep
$\langle \mathbf S_i\rangle = 0$ exactly, so the ground state that we obtain is time-reversal
symmetric by construction, as (8.5) and (8.9) require. Equation (8.15) is a tight-binding model for
the auxiliary fermions, with hoppings $\chi_{ij}$ that are fixed self-consistently by the geometry
of the lattice and by the exchange couplings $J_{ij}$. Its excitations are the \textbf{spinons},
which are emergent fermionic quasiparticles with spin $1/2$ and no charge. We obtain them with the
same procedure that we used for electrons in the whole course, which is to take a quartic
interaction, decouple it at the mean-field level and diagonalize a bilinear Hamiltonian, and the
only difference is that now we apply it to the spin sector. Figure~\ref{fig:s08:parton}b shows the
resulting picture, with spinons that hop on the lattice with amplitude $\chi_{ij}$, at half filling
because of (8.11).

\input{figures/s08f04_parton}

When we diagonalize (8.15) on a given lattice, we obtain a real spinon band structure. Depending on
the mean-field parameters that we obtain, there are three different possible outcomes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gapless spinons}, with excitations at energies as close to zero as we want.
\item \textbf{Gapped spinons}, where it costs a finite energy to create a spinon.
\item \textbf{Dirac spinons}, with a linear relation between energy and momentum at isolated points
  in the Brillouin zone.
\end{itemize}

\section{Emergent gauge symmetry and vison excitations}

There is one more essential part of the QSL picture, and it comes directly from the fact that we
introduced fermions to describe a spin. Let us take a local phase rotation of the auxiliary
fermions that depends on the site,

\begin{equation}
f_{i\sigma} \;\to\; e^{i\theta_i}\, f_{i\sigma}, \qquad f^\dagger_{i\sigma} \to e^{-i\theta_i}
f^\dagger_{i\sigma}.
\end{equation}

Both the physical spin operator (8.10) and the single-occupancy constraint (8.11) do not change at
all under (8.16). The phase $e^{i\theta_i}$ cancels between $f^\dagger_{i\alpha}$ and
$f_{i\beta}$ in every term, so the Hamiltonian and the physical spin operators are exactly
invariant. The mean-field bond variable $\chi_{ij}$, however, is \emph{not} invariant, since it
gets a relative phase, $\chi_{ij} \to e^{i(\theta_i - \theta_j)}\chi_{ij}$. This is the structure
of a lattice gauge theory. The variable $\chi_{ij}$ behaves as a link variable, and (8.16) is a
local transformation, which is called a gauge transformation. The physical spin theory is invariant
under it, while the mean-field saddle point of Section 8.9 is not. Any two choices of $\chi_{ij}$
that are related by (8.16) describe the same physical quantum spin liquid, so the mean-field
decoupling only fixes a class of gauge-equivalent solutions and not a unique set of hoppings. This
redundancy is the origin of an \textbf{emergent gauge theory} for quantum spin liquids.

Once we see this gauge structure, $\chi_{ij}$ becomes a dynamical field on the links of the
lattice, and it can have its own topological defects. This is in direct analogy with the superconducting
order parameter, whose gauge degree of freedom can have vortices. Let us write the
mean-field Hamiltonian as $\mathcal H = \sum_{ij,s}|\chi_{ij}|e^{i\theta_{ij}}f^\dagger_{i,s}f_{j,s}$,
and let us define the flux through a plaquette as the product of the phases that we pick up when we
go around its boundary, $\Phi = \prod e^{i\theta_{ij}}$. For concreteness, let us take a square
lattice where the hoppings around every elementary loop pick up the phase $\Phi=+1$, which is a
ground state, except for one loop, where the phase is $\Phi=-1$. This single flipped plaquette is an
excited state. It is a topological defect in the emergent gauge field, and it is called a
\textbf{vison}. The vison is the excitation of the gauge sector, and in general it is coupled to the
spinon excitations of the matter sector that we built in Section 8.9. In this language, a quantum
spin liquid is described not only by its spinon dispersion but also by an emergent gauge theory and
by the vison excitations of that theory. This is a new organizing principle, and nothing like it
exists in the symmetry-broken magnets of Session 6. There is no notebook exercise for this
construction, which is a purely conceptual and formal part of the QSL picture.
Figure~\ref{fig:s08:gauge} shows both parts of it, why the flux and not the link variable is the
physical quantity, and what a single flipped flux looks like on the lattice.

\section{From a single Kondo impurity to the Kondo lattice}

Now that we have the method for quantum spin liquids, the lecture moves to a second physical
system, which is quite different but which can be solved with the same method. This is the
\textbf{Kondo lattice model}, which appears whenever a metal and a correlated system of local
moments exist together.

Let us start with the simplest case, which is a single spin-$1/2$ magnetic impurity in a
non-interacting two-dimensional electron gas. The total Hamiltonian is a free kinetic term with a
dispersion for the conduction electrons, plus a Heisenberg-like coupling between the spin of the
impurity and the local spin density of the conduction electrons at the impurity site,

\begin{equation}
H = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\Big(c^\dagger_{i\sigma}c_{j\sigma} + \text{h.c.}\Big) +
H_K, \qquad H_K = \sum_{\alpha\beta}\Big(c^\dagger_{0\beta}\,\boldsymbol\sigma_{\beta\alpha}\,
c_{0\alpha}\Big)\cdot \mathbf S,
\end{equation}

where $H_K$ is the \textbf{Kondo coupling}. To solve this problem exactly is hard. However, we
can describe the ground state in a simple way, even without the full solution. The spin of the impurity forms a singlet, which has the same structure
as (8.3), with an effective conduction electron close to it,

\begin{equation*}
|GS\rangle \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(|{\Uparrow\downarrow}\rangle -
|{\Downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\Big),
\end{equation*}

and the rest of the electron gas behaves as a Fermi sea without interactions. This is a good
approximation to the many-body ground state. The resulting state has total spin zero and does
not break time-reversal symmetry, so even if it has a real local moment, it is already a quantum
magnet in the sense of Section 8.1.

Let us now generalize this to a whole \textbf{lattice} of magnetic impurities, with one impurity per
site, where each impurity is coupled to the same bath of conduction electrons,

\begin{equation}
H = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\Big(c^\dagger_{i\sigma}c_{j\sigma} + \text{h.c.}\Big) +
J\sum_{j,\alpha\beta}\Big(c^\dagger_{j\beta}\,\boldsymbol\sigma_{\beta\alpha}\,c_{j\alpha}\Big)\cdot
\mathbf S_j.
\end{equation}

Every impurity again tends to form a singlet with a conduction electron close to it. Since many
impurities compete for the same sea of electrons, the electrons that screen the impurities are
shared, in a fractional way, between the singlets of different impurities. The result is a state
with a large entanglement over the whole lattice, between all the magnetic sites and the electron
gas, while the total spin stays exactly zero.

\section{Solving the Kondo lattice with the parton method and a hybridization}

The lecture makes the parallel with Section 8.8 explicit, since we solve this problem in the same
way as we solved the QSL. We replace every impurity spin by an Abrikosov fermion,
$\mathbf S_j = \tfrac12
f_{j\alpha}^\dagger \boldsymbol\sigma_{\alpha\beta} f_{j\beta}$, with the same single-occupancy
constraint (8.11), which we write here in the equivalent traceless form

\begin{equation}
S_{\alpha\beta}(j) = f^\dagger_{j\alpha}f_{j\beta} - \frac{n_f(j)}{N}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
\end{equation}

When we insert this into (8.18), the Kondo coupling becomes a real quartic interaction. Now it does
not couple the auxiliary $f$ fermions to the $f$ fermion of another site, as in (8.13), but to the
\emph{real} conduction electrons $c_{j\sigma}$ on the same site,

\begin{equation}
H \sim \sum_{\mathbf k\alpha} \varepsilon_{\mathbf k}\, c^\dagger_{\mathbf k\alpha}c_{\mathbf
k\alpha} \;-\; J\sum_{j,\alpha\beta}\Big(c^\dagger_{j\beta}f_{j\beta}\Big)\Big(f^\dagger_{j\alpha}
c_{j\alpha}\Big).
\end{equation}

As in (8.14), we decouple this quartic term at the mean-field level, with the expectation value
$\langle c^\dagger_j f_j\rangle$, and we do a Fourier transform to momentum space. This gives a
bilinear Hamiltonian that mixes real electrons and auxiliary fermions,

\begin{equation}
H \sim \sum_{\mathbf k\alpha} \varepsilon_{\mathbf k}\, c^\dagger_{\mathbf k\alpha} c_{\mathbf
k\alpha} \;-\; \gamma_K \sum_{\mathbf k,\alpha} f^\dagger_{\mathbf k\alpha}c_{\mathbf k\alpha} +
\text{h.c.},
\end{equation}

where $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}$ is the \textbf{conduction band dispersion} and $\gamma_K$ is the
\textbf{Kondo hybridization}. The hybridization is the only mean-field parameter, it is determined
self-consistently, and it contains all the information about the strength of the original Kondo
coupling $J$.

\section{Electronic structure of the Kondo lattice and the Kondo insulator}

For each spin, Equation (8.21) is an ordinary two-band Bloch Hamiltonian in the basis $(c_{\mathbf
k\alpha}, f_{\mathbf k\alpha})$. When $\gamma_K = 0$, there is no mixing at all. The spectrum is
then the ordinary dispersive conduction band $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}$, together with a completely
\textbf{flat} band of $f$-fermion levels at zero energy. This band is flat because the auxiliary
fermions have no hopping of their own when the Kondo coupling is zero. When we switch the Kondo
coupling on again, $\gamma_K$ becomes finite, and the flat band and the dispersive band have an
\textbf{anti-crossing}. In every place where the flat spinon band would cross the conduction band,
a \textbf{gap} opens instead, and the gap is larger when the Kondo coupling is larger. We see this
directly when we compare the spectra for increasing $J_K$. At $J_K=0$ the bands cross. At
$J_K=0.25$ there is already a visible gap at the crossing. At $J_K=0.5$ the gap is much larger.
Figure~\ref{fig:s08:kondobands} shows the two ends of this comparison, and where the Fermi level is
in each case.

\input{figures/s08f06_kondo_bands}

Physically, the ground state of this hybridized system is non-magnetic by construction. It has
total spin zero and it is time-reversal symmetric, in the same way as for the single impurity
(8.17) and for the quantum spin liquid of Section 8.9. The consequence for the electronic
structure, however, is new. A lattice of Kondo impurities turns a system that would otherwise be an
ordinary metal into an \textbf{insulator}, because every electron at the Fermi surface forms a
bound state with a magnetic impurity. This is the \textbf{Kondo insulator}, which is also called a
heavy-fermion insulator, and SmB$_6$, which we already introduced in Section 8.1, is the standard
real material example.

Note that there is a similarity with a quite different construction of Session 5. Superconductors
also open a gap in their quasiparticle spectrum through a hybridization, but there it is between
electrons and holes and not between electrons and auxiliary fermions. The two systems are
physically very different, but the \emph{mechanism} that opens a gap in the single-particle
spectrum is very similar. The way the effect appears is also similar. The temperature
scale at which the gap of the Kondo insulator becomes visible depends on the Kondo coupling in a
strongly non-linear way, which is the same kind of non-linear dependence that we know from the
dependence of the superconducting gap on the pairing interaction.

\section{Closing summary}

This is the final recap of the lecture, with the equations that it refers to.

\begin{itemize}
\item Magnetism, in all the forms that we saw in Sessions 1 and 6 and in this session, comes from
  strong repulsive interactions between electrons.
\item Frustrated magnetic models, such as those on triangular and kagome lattices (Section 8.4),
  can show \textbf{quantum spin liquid} behavior. This is a maximally entangled ground state with
  zero local spin expectation value on every site (8.5). It generalizes the singlet of the
  Heisenberg dimer (8.3), through the valence bond solid state (8.5), to a resonating and
  frustrated ground state (8.6)-(8.7), and conventional mean-field theory cannot describe it by
  construction.
\item The zero magnetization of a QSL is the opposite regime from the one that Holstein-Primakoff
  needs (8.9). For this reason, its fundamental excitations are not magnons but \textbf{spinons},
  with $S=1/2$. These are emergent fermions without charge that we obtain from the Abrikosov or
  parton construction, (8.10)-(8.15), which is the mean-field method for electrons of Sessions 1
  and 6 applied to the spin sector. Spinon bands can be gapless, gapped, or of Dirac type
  (Section 8.9).
\item The parton construction has an emergent gauge redundancy (8.16). The mean-field bond variable
  is a link field, and its topological defects are gauge excitations called \textbf{visons}, which
  are in general coupled to the spinons (Section 8.10).
\item Quantum entanglement that mixes spin and fermion degrees of freedom also appears in
  \textbf{Kondo systems}. The same parton method, applied to a lattice of local moments inside a
  metal, (8.17)-(8.21), turns the Kondo exchange coupling into a mean-field
  \textbf{hybridization} $\gamma_K$. This hybridization opens a gap in the conduction band at the
  anti-crossing and turns a metal into a \textbf{Kondo insulator}, also called a heavy-fermion
  insulator, and the gap grows monotonically with the strength of the Kondo coupling
  (Section 8.13).
\end{itemize}

Session 9 continues directly from this construction. The method of Abrikosov fermions and spinons
that we built here is used again, now for a single magnetic impurity in a metal that is otherwise
ordinary, with an explicit reference to a result on Friedel oscillations that is derived earlier in
that same session.
