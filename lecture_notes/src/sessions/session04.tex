\chapter{Electrons in a Magnetic Field, Quantum Hall Effect and Landau Levels}

In the last session we built the general tools of topological band structure. These were the Kubo
formula for the Hall conductivity, the way to write it as an integral of the Berry curvature, the
Chern number as the topological invariant that comes out of it, and the bulk-boundary
correspondence, which says that a nonzero Chern number gives a protected chiral edge state. We
applied these tools to two concrete examples, the SSH chain and a gapped Dirac cone on the
honeycomb lattice, which is the Haldane construction, and we did this on purpose without any
magnetic field. This choice left one case open. In session 2 we saw that the dispersion of a Bloch
Hamiltonian comes in three basic types, parabolic, Dirac and flat, and the flat-band case, which is
the one where a real magnetic field gives its own topological story, was left for this session. So
let us continue from the point where we stopped. In this session we apply the Chern-number tools of
session 3 to the flat-band case in full. First we will see the integer quantum Hall effect and
Landau levels as topological flat bands. Then we will let these flat bands become real many-body
objects, and we will see the Laughlin wavefunction, fractional charge and anyonic statistics, which
are the simplest example of fractionalization that we will meet in this course. At the end we will
go back to the case without a field. The Haldane model gives a quantized Hall conductance with no
magnetic field at all, and we will see explicitly that it and the ordinary quantum Hall state are
two forms of the same topological phase.

\section{Where the quantum Hall state shows up}

The quantum Hall state is not the property of one specific material. It is a state of matter that a whole family of two-dimensional electron systems
can realize, and each of these systems has its own energy scale and temperature scale.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Semiconductor quantum wells}, historically gallium arsenide (GaAs), were the first
  platform. In these systems the electrons are essentially parabolic electrons, with the ordinary
  quadratic dispersion, and the relevant energy scale grows linearly with the magnetic field. For
  the fields that we can reach in a laboratory this gives roughly one millielectronvolt, and this is
  the reason why quantum Hall physics in GaAs needs temperatures of the order of one kelvin.
\item \textbf{Two-dimensional materials}, and graphene above all, realize the same physics but
  with Dirac electrons instead of parabolic ones. As we will derive explicitly below, the energy
  scale then grows as the square root of the field and not linearly. The slides give the concrete
  numbers, which are roughly two orders of magnitude larger, around 100 millielectronvolts. This is
  high enough that the quantum Hall plateaus of graphene survive up to about 100 K, and in the best
  samples up to almost room temperature.
\end{itemize}

For all these platforms the common point is the one we already saw in the last session. An
observable, the Hall conductance, is exactly quantized, and this quantization comes from topology
and not from the chemistry of a specific material.

\section{Measuring the Hall conductance}

The experiment itself is simple to describe. We take a two-dimensional system, apply a voltage
along one in-plane direction, which we call $y$, and measure the current that flows along the
perpendicular in-plane direction, $x$. The ratio of this transverse current to the applied voltage
is the Hall conductance, and this is the quantity that becomes quantized in the quantum Hall
regime.

A real device for this measurement is a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) with several finger
gates in contact with it. A voltage is applied between a top gate and a bottom gate, and the
current is measured between two gates placed along $x$, with all the connections made through
metallic leads. The geometry of the device does not add new physics beyond what we need for the
concepts, so we will not discuss it further. The device figures in the slides are useful to see
how a real experiment looks, but from here on the notes work with the ideal two-dimensional
system.

\section{From the Kubo formula to the Chern number, briefly}

We derived this result in full in the last session, so we will not repeat fifteen lines of algebra
that would look almost the same. Instead, let us recall the logic and check that it applies here
without changes. The Kubo formula is linear response, so it is first order in the applied field,
and its sum over states has the form of a second-order perturbation expression. It writes the
transverse conductivity as a sum over all pairs of occupied and empty bands, where each term has
matrix elements of $\partial H/\partial k_x$ and $\partial H/\partial k_y$ divided by energy
differences. There is an identity that relates $\partial H/\partial k$ to the derivative of the
eigenstates, which we obtain when we differentiate the eigenvalue equation
$H(\mathbf{k})|u_\mathbf{k}\rangle = \varepsilon(\mathbf{k})|u_\mathbf{k}\rangle$ with respect to
$\mathbf{k}$. With this identity every explicit energy denominator cancels, and the result depends
only on the wavefunctions, through the Berry connection $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{k}) = i\langle
u_\mathbf{k}|\nabla_\mathbf{k}|u_\mathbf{k}\rangle$ and its curl, the Berry curvature
$\Omega(\mathbf{k}) = \nabla_\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{k})$. The Brillouin zone is a
closed, compact manifold, which is a torus, and when we sum over occupied bands and integrate over
it we obtain an integer,

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{xy} = \frac{e^2}{h}\, C, \qquad C = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\,\text{occ.}}\int_{\text{BZ}} \Omega_n(\mathbf{k})\, d^2k \in \mathbb{Z},
\end{equation}

which is the Chern number of the occupied bands. This is the same statement as in session 3. The
Hall conductance is not only quantized, it is also a topological invariant, so it does not change
under any smooth deformation of the Hamiltonian that does not close the bulk gap. What is new in
this session is not this derivation. It is the specific band structure that we will use in it,
which is the set of Landau levels of a magnetic field, and everything that follows when we ask how
the many-body ground state of a \emph{partially filled} Landau level looks.

\section{Landau levels as Chern-number-one flat bands}

A two-dimensional electron gas in a perpendicular magnetic field develops a set of completely flat
bands, the Landau levels, with index $n=0,1,2,\dots$. In this context ``flat'' means without
dispersion, so the energy does not depend on momentum at all, and we will derive the reason for
this in Section 4.9. Even if they have no dispersion, each Landau level has a finite Chern number,
which is also the smallest nonzero value, $C=1$. If the chemical potential is between the $N$-th
and the $(N+1)$-th Landau level, the total Chern number of the occupied bands is the sum of $N$
contributions equal to $1$,

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} C_n = N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_{xy} = N\,\frac{e^2}{h},
\end{equation}

which is an integer that counts the number of filled Landau levels. This is the integer quantum
Hall effect. It is a state built only from flat bands without dispersion, and still its Hall
conductance is an exact integer multiple of $e^2/h$, with a sharp quantization.

\section{Edge states and why an insulating bulk still conducts}

There is an obvious puzzle. If the chemical potential is in a gap between two Landau levels, the
bulk of the sample is by definition an insulator, since there is no partially filled band and
there are no bulk states at the Fermi energy. So how can such a system carry a finite, quantized
current?

The answer is the same as in the general bulk-boundary argument of session 3. A nonzero Chern
number cannot end without consequences. At any interface where the Chern number changes, which here
is the boundary between a sample with Chern number $N$ and the vacuum, which has $C=0$, there must
be $N$ protected, gapless and chiral edge modes. A chiral mode propagates in only one direction,
and the number of edge modes at an interface is equal to the difference in Chern number across it.
Every real sample is finite, so it always has an edge, and the edge states are always there, even
when we do not think about them. When we apply a voltage between the top edge and the bottom edge
of the sample, we create an imbalance between the edge electrons that move to the right and the
ones that move to the left, and this imbalance is the quantized Hall current.

The band structure of a ribbon, which is a sample that is infinite in $y$ and finite in $x$, shows
this picture in a completely explicit way. Next to the flat bulk Landau levels without dispersion,
we find a set of dispersive states localized at the left edge, which all propagate in one
direction, and a mirror set localized at the right edge, which propagate in the opposite direction.
Each propagation direction is tied to a \emph{location}, the left edge or the right edge, so an
impurity on the left edge cannot scatter a left-moving electron into a right-moving one, because
the right edge is at a macroscopic distance. There is no available final state, so there is no
backscattering, and the edge transport has no dissipation. Figure~\ref{fig:s04:ribbon} shows the
ribbon spectrum next to its real-space picture, together with the chiral modes that cross the
chemical potential.

\section{Quantized Hall plateaus in experiment}

When we sweep the magnetic field on a real 2DEG, we see this transition directly. At low field,
before the quantum regime starts, the Hall resistance grows linearly with $B$, which is the
classical Hall effect of ordinary electromagnetism. When the field reaches the tesla range, a
staircase of sharp plateaus appears in the Hall resistance instead. The precise field scale
depends on the material and follows the spacing of the Landau levels that we derive below. When
the field increases further, the cyclotron energy $\hbar\omega_c$ grows, so fewer Landau levels
stay below a fixed chemical potential. Each time the chemical potential goes past a Landau level,
the number of filled levels $N$ in (4.2) decreases by one, and the Hall resistance jumps to the
next quantized plateau.

\section{Coupling electrons to a magnetic field with minimal coupling and the Peierls substitution}

To go beyond this phenomenology and derive the spectrum of the Landau levels, we first need to
know how to include a magnetic field in a Hamiltonian. In classical physics, a magnetic field
$\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ enters the equations of motion through the canonical
momentum,

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\pi} = \mathbf{p} - e\mathbf{A},
\end{equation}

which replaces the ordinary momentum $\mathbf{p}$ everywhere it appears, and when we compute the
equations of motion that follow from it we recover the Lorentz force law. In this session we set
the magnitude of the electron charge and the speed of light to one, as the slides do without
saying it, and we keep only $\hbar$ explicit where the spacing of the Landau levels needs it. The
same replacement (4.3) works without changes in quantum mechanics, with
$\mathbf{p}\to-i\hbar\nabla$. Minimal coupling turns the free Hamiltonian $p^2/2m$ into
$\pi^2/2m$, and in the same way it changes the Dirac Hamiltonian below.

There is one subtle point that we cannot avoid, which is that $\mathbf{A}$ is never unique. Any
change $\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi$ with a scalar function $\chi$ gives the same physical
field $\mathbf{B}$, so we always have to choose a gauge. We will need two gauges, the Landau gauge
and the symmetric gauge, and we will see both in Section 4.8. A related point is a bit against
intuition. A uniform out-of-plane field does not break the in-plane translational symmetry of the
physical system, but \emph{every} choice of $\mathbf{A}$ for this field breaks translational
symmetry in a formal way, in one direction or in both. Which symmetry survives, and how much of it,
is a choice of gauge and not a physical choice.

The minimal-coupling replacement (4.3) is the natural method for continuum models, which are
Schrödinger or Dirac electrons, where we write and solve a differential equation directly. For a
tight-binding model, which is written in real-space orbitals, the more natural method puts the
dependence on the field into the wavefunctions and not into the Hamiltonian, with a local gauge
transformation. In concrete terms, the hopping amplitude $t_{ij}$ is by definition an overlap
between the orbital wavefunctions at sites $i$ and $j$. When we include the vector potential in
these wavefunctions, every hopping gets multiplied by a complex phase, which is equal to the line
integral of $\mathbf{A}$ between the two sites,

\begin{equation}
t_{ij} \;\longrightarrow\; t_{ij}\, \exp\!\left(i\int_{\mathbf{R}_i}^{\mathbf{R}_j} \mathbf{A}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}\right),
\end{equation}

and this is what is called the \textbf{Peierls substitution}. It is the tight-binding version of
minimal coupling, and the two give the same physics, since at low energies the expansion of a
tight-binding model with the Peierls substitution gives directly the continuum Hamiltonian with
minimal coupling.

\section{The Landau gauge and the symmetric gauge}

For a uniform field $\mathbf{B} = B\hat{z}$, there are two gauges that will be important for us.

\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{Landau gauge}, $\mathbf{A} = (0, Bx, 0)$, puts all the spatial dependence into
  one coordinate, $x$. Since $\mathbf{A}$ does not depend on $y$, the Hamiltonian built from it
  still commutes with $p_y$, so translations along $y$ are still a good symmetry, but it does not
  commute with $p_x$. This gauge keeps \emph{one} translational symmetry, which is what we need to
  have a band structure at all, and it is the gauge that we use in every ribbon calculation in this
  session.
\item The \textbf{symmetric gauge}, $\mathbf{A} = \tfrac{B}{2}(-y, x, 0)$, breaks both
  translational symmetries but keeps the rotational symmetry of the system instead. It is the
  natural gauge to write many-body wavefunctions with a good angular momentum, which is what we
  need when we arrive at the fractional quantum Hall state, from Section 4.13 on.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:s04:gauge} shows the difference in a concrete way, by writing both gauges as
Peierls phases (4.4) on a square lattice. The phases of the single bonds are completely different
in the two gauges, but the sum around any plaquette is the same flux in both. This sum is the only
gauge-invariant combination, and it is the only thing that the electrons respond to.

\section{Landau levels of Schrödinger electrons}

Let us now solve for the spectrum, and let us start with the simplest case. These are free
electrons with no lattice and no potential apart from the field itself, and with dispersion
$p^2/2m$ before we switch on the field. With minimal coupling and the Landau gauge
$\mathbf{A}=(0,Bx,0)$ we have

\begin{equation}
H = \frac{\pi_x^2+\pi_y^2}{2m} = \frac{p_x^2}{2m} + \frac{(p_y-Bx)^2}{2m}.
\end{equation}

We take the charge of the carriers to be $+e$ in (4.3) and we set $e=1$ in all this session, while we keep $\hbar$ explicit.
This is a choice of convention, but it has consequences, since it fixes
$[\pi_x,\pi_y]=+i\hbar^2/\ell_B^2$. The ladder operators of Section 4.10 and the holomorphic
wavefunctions of Section 4.13 are built on this convention. For an electron, which has charge
$-e$, every cyclotron orbit and every edge current below goes around in the other direction, and
nothing else changes.

Since $\mathbf{A}$ does not depend on $y$, we have $[H,p_y]=0$, so we can look for eigenstates of
the form $\psi(x,y) = e^{ik_y y}\phi(x)$. With the replacement $p_y\to\hbar k_y$, the Hamiltonian
(4.5) becomes a one-dimensional problem in $x$,

\begin{equation*}
H\phi(x) = \left[\frac{p_x^2}{2m} + \frac{B^2}{2m}\Big(x-\frac{\hbar k_y}{B}\Big)^2\right]\phi(x),
\end{equation*}

which is exactly the Hamiltonian of a quantum harmonic oscillator centered at $x_0 = \hbar
k_y/B$, with frequency $\omega_c = B/m$, the cyclotron frequency. The shift of the center is
removed with a change of variable, as it is described in the lecture. When we solve it with the
same ladder operators as the ordinary harmonic oscillator we obtain

\begin{equation}
E_n = \hbar\omega_c\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right), \qquad n=0,1,2,\dots,
\end{equation}

which does not depend on $k_y$ at all. Every value of $k_y$, or in other words every choice of the
guiding center $x_0$, gives the same energy. This is the reason why the Landau levels are flat
bands, and why each of them has a macroscopic degeneracy that is proportional to the area of the
sample. Figure~\ref{fig:s04:guidingcenter} shows this mechanism directly, with the same well placed
at different positions, and with the degeneracy counted as one orbital for each flux quantum
through the sample. When we put in the real physical constants for a field typical of GaAs, we
obtain the millielectronvolt scale that we gave in Section 4.1.

\section{Landau levels of Dirac electrons}

Now we repeat the same calculation for a Dirac Hamiltonian, $H_0 = v_F(p_x\sigma_x+p_y\sigma_y)$,
which is the low-energy effective description of graphene near the $K$ point. With the same
minimal coupling and the same Landau gauge, $\pi_x=p_x$, $\pi_y=p_y-Bx$, the Hamiltonian is now an
\emph{off-diagonal} $2\times2$ matrix,

\begin{equation*}
H = v_F\begin{pmatrix}0 & \pi_x-i\pi_y\\ \pi_x+i\pi_y & 0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}

The matrix $H$ is not diagonal and we cannot solve it by inspection, but its square is. We
introduce the magnetic length $\ell_B=\sqrt{\hbar/B}$ and the ladder operators $a =
\frac{\ell_B}{\sqrt{2}\hbar}(\pi_x+i\pi_y)$ and
$a^\dagger=\frac{\ell_B}{\sqrt{2}\hbar}(\pi_x-i\pi_y)$. Since $[\pi_x,\pi_y]=i\hbar^2/\ell_B^2$,
these operators satisfy the ordinary bosonic commutation relation $[a,a^\dagger]=1$. In terms of
$a,a^\dagger$ we have

\begin{equation*}
H = \frac{\sqrt{2}\hbar v_F}{\ell_B}\begin{pmatrix}0 & a^\dagger\\ a & 0\end{pmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
H^2 = \frac{2\hbar^2v_F^2}{\ell_B^2}\begin{pmatrix}a^\dagger a & 0\\ 0 & aa^\dagger\end{pmatrix}
= \frac{2\hbar^2v_F^2}{\ell_B^2}\begin{pmatrix}N & 0\\ 0 & N+1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}

where $N=a^\dagger a$ is the number operator of the harmonic oscillator. This is the statement in
the transcript that $H^2$ has the form ``$p^2+B^2x^2+\text{const}$, which looks like a harmonic
oscillator''. Since $H^2$ is diagonal and has the form of a harmonic oscillator, its eigenvalues
are $2\hbar^2v_F^2 n/\ell_B^2$ for $n=0,1,2,\dots$, and the energy itself is the square root,

\begin{equation}
E_n = \text{sgn}(n)\, v_F\sqrt{2\hbar B|n|}, \qquad n = 0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots,
\end{equation}

with a real mode at exactly zero energy for $n=0$. For this mode the lower spinor component
vanishes identically, so there is no partner state to pair it with. This is the origin of the
$\sqrt{B}$ scaling in graphene. The spectrum is the \emph{square root} of a harmonic-oscillator
spectrum and not the spectrum itself, so both the dependence on the field and the level spacing
change their behavior. For Schrödinger electrons the levels are equally spaced and grow linearly,
while for Dirac electrons the levels are spaced as $\sqrt{n}$ and grow as $\sqrt{B}$, and this is
the reason why, at the same field, the Landau levels of graphene are roughly two orders of
magnitude higher in energy than the ones of a GaAs 2DEG. Figure~\ref{fig:s04:ladders} shows the two
ladders next to each other, including the zero mode, which has no counterpart at all in the
Schrödinger case.

\section{Numerical check of the Landau levels and edge states on the lattice}

Let us say explicitly how the continuum results of Sections 4.9 and 4.10 are connected to
tight-binding lattices, since this is where the edge states of Section 4.5 first appear together
with the bulk Landau levels in a single calculation. We take a ribbon of a square lattice with a
finite width. The band bottom of this lattice is parabolic, so it plays the role of the
Schrödinger 2DEG. We add a uniform orbital field with the Peierls substitution (4.4), and we put
the chemical potential near the bottom of the band, so that the lattice model effectively describes
a dilute electron gas that is almost free. The band structure that we obtain shows what the
continuum calculation predicted. There is a set of flat bulk bands, the Landau levels, together with
dispersive edge bands, with one branch localized at each edge of the ribbon and propagating in
opposite directions, which is the chiral pair described in Section 4.5. When we repeat the same
construction on an armchair ribbon of the honeycomb lattice, where the low-energy physics is a real
Dirac equation at the $K$ and $K'$ points, we obtain the $\sqrt{B}$ Landau levels of Section 4.10
together with their own chiral edge states. The band structures of both ribbons, shown for a width

\section{Ladder operators and the many-body Landau Hamiltonian}

In Sections 4.9 and 4.10 we found the structure of a harmonic oscillator by working in a specific
gauge. We can show this structure in a way that does not depend on the gauge, if we define the
ladder operators directly from the canonical momentum,

\begin{equation*}
a = \frac{\ell_B}{\sqrt{2}\hbar}(\pi_x + i\pi_y), \qquad a^\dagger = \frac{\ell_B}{\sqrt{2}\hbar}(\pi_x-i\pi_y),
\end{equation*}

which satisfy the canonical bosonic commutation relation $[a,a^\dagger]=1$. For this we only use
$[\pi_x,\pi_y]=i\hbar^2/\ell_B^2$, which depends on the field and not on the gauge. In terms of these operators the \emph{full} quantum Hall Hamiltonian for a
Schrödinger electron becomes

\begin{equation}
H = \hbar\omega_c\left(a^\dagger a + \frac{1}{2}\right),
\end{equation}

which is clearly a harmonic oscillator, with eigenvalues that give (4.6) directly, without any
reference to a gauge. This is the clearest way to see that the statement ``Landau levels behave
like a harmonic oscillator'' is not an artifact of the Landau gauge. It is a structural property
of any electron in a uniform field.

\section{The symmetric gauge and the lowest-Landau-level wavefunctions}

To go from single-particle Landau levels to a real many-body quantum Hall wavefunction, we change
to the symmetric gauge, $\mathbf{A}=\tfrac{B}{2}(-y,x,0)$, and to the complex coordinate $z=x+iy$.
We write $\partial_z = \tfrac12(\partial_x-i\partial_y)$ and $\partial_{\bar z} =
\tfrac12(\partial_x+i\partial_y)$, and then the ladder operator of Section 4.12 in this gauge is
proportional to $\partial_{\bar z} + z/4\ell_B^2$. The condition for the lowest Landau level (LLL),
$a\psi=0$, is therefore the first-order differential equation

\begin{equation}
\left(\partial_{\bar z} + \frac{z}{4\ell_B^2}\right)\psi(z,\bar z) = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\psi(z,\bar z) = f(z)\, e^{-|z|^2/4\ell_B^2},
\end{equation}

where $f(z)$ is an \emph{arbitrary holomorphic function}, meaning any function built only from
powers of $z$ and never from $\bar z$. This is the precise form of the statement ``any power $z^m$
times a Gaussian is a zeroth-Landau-level wavefunction''. Holomorphicity is not an extra
assumption, since it follows directly when we solve the LLL condition in the symmetric gauge. The
single-particle LLL orbitals are then

\begin{equation}
\psi_m(z) = z^m\, e^{-|z|^2/4\ell_B^2}, \qquad m=0,1,2,\dots,
\end{equation}

and each of them has an angular momentum $m$, which is the eigenvalue of $L_z$ in this gauge. All
of them have the same Gaussian envelope, but the polynomial prefactor $z^m$ moves the probability
density of the orbital further from the origin when $m$ grows. The reason is that $|\psi_m|^2$ has
its maximum where $\tfrac{d}{d|z|}\big(|z|^{2m}e^{-|z|^2/2\ell_B^2}\big)=0$, which is at the radius
$r_m = \sqrt{2m}\,\ell_B$.

\section{The many-body wavefunction of the filled lowest Landau level}

Now we look for the many-body ground state of $N$ electrons in which \emph{every} LLL orbital is
occupied. Two conditions fix this state completely. It must be built only from LLL orbitals (4.10),
and it must be antisymmetric under the exchange of any two electrons, as fermion statistics
requires. Both conditions are satisfied by

\begin{equation}
\Psi_1(z_1,\dots,z_N) = \prod_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)\; \exp\!\left(-\sum_i \frac{|z_i|^2}{4\ell_B^2}\right).
\end{equation}

The Vandermonde product $\prod_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)$ does two things at the same time. First, it is
antisymmetric by construction, since the exchange of two coordinates $z_i\leftrightarrow z_j$
changes the sign of exactly one factor $(z_i-z_j)$, and this gives the overall minus sign that the
Pauli principle requires. Second, it is exactly the Slater determinant of the single-particle
orbitals $1,z,z^2,\dots,z^{N-1}$, because of the standard identity for the Vandermonde determinant,
$\det\!\big(z_i^{\,j-1}\big) = \prod_{i<j}(z_j-z_i)$. So (4.11) is the Slater determinant of the
$N$ lowest LLL orbitals, times their common Gaussian envelope, where the Gaussians of the single
electrons combine into the collective exponential shown above. This is the exact many-body ground
state of $N$ electrons that fill the lowest Landau level completely, which is the integer quantum
Hall state at filling $\nu=1$.

\section{Angular momentum, enclosed flux, and the uniform electron gas}

In Section 4.13 we saw that a larger angular momentum $m$ moves the maximum of an orbital further
from the origin, to the radius $r_m=\sqrt{2m}\,\ell_B$. In other words, this orbital encloses a
larger area, and so a larger magnetic flux. We will use this link between angular momentum and
enclosed flux in Section 4.17 to find how much charge a quasiparticle has. For now, the important
consequence is about (4.11) itself. When we sum over the full Slater determinant, we find a density
profile that is essentially uniform, or flat, up to a radius set by the highest occupied angular
momentum $m=N-1$, and that drops sharply to zero beyond this radius. In other words, the filled
lowest Landau level is a uniform two-dimensional electron gas without any structure, and this is the
property that we will now ask also from a Landau level with \emph{fractional} filling.
Figure~\ref{fig:s04:droplet} shows both parts of this statement, with the single orbitals as
nested rings of growing radius, and the flat droplet that they give when we add them.

\section{Fractional filling and the Laughlin wavefunction}

Let us now say that we want to occupy only a fraction of the available LLL orbitals, for example
two out of every three, but in a way that keeps the electron gas uniform. If we only leave some
orbitals empty, we obtain a density that is clearly not uniform and has lumps, since each empty
orbital is at its own fixed radius. We look for a many-body wavefunction with three properties.
First, it still lives only in the LLL, so it is built only from the coordinates $z_i$ and never
from $\bar z_i$. Second, it is still antisymmetric, since the particles are still electrons.
Third, in a heuristic sense, it keeps the electrons as far from each other as possible, since this
is what a repulsive Coulomb interaction favors when we minimize its energy.

The construction that satisfies the three conditions at the same time is very simple. We take the
wavefunction of the filled LLL (4.11) and we raise the Vandermonde factor to a power,

\begin{equation}
\Psi_m(z_1,\dots,z_N) = \prod_{i<j}(z_i-z_j)^m\; \exp\!\left(-\sum_i \frac{|z_i|^2}{4\ell_B^2}\right),
\end{equation}

which is called the \textbf{Laughlin wavefunction}. The exponent $m$ must be an odd integer, since
an even power would make the wavefunction \emph{symmetric} and not antisymmetric under exchange,
and this is not allowed for fermions. A larger exponent makes the electrons avoid each other more,
since the wavefunction now vanishes to $m$-th order, and not only linearly, when two electrons are
at the same point, and this is the correlation effect that we wanted. When we compute the electron
density that follows from it, we obtain the filling factor

\begin{equation}
\nu = \frac{1}{m},
\end{equation}

which we can also see directly by counting. The maximum power of any single coordinate in the
fully expanded polynomial (4.12) is $m(N-1)$, so the wavefunction uses the angular-momentum
orbitals up to $m(N-1)$. This means that $N$ electrons are spread over $\approx m(N-1)+1 \approx
mN$ available LLL orbitals, which gives a filling fraction of $N/(mN)=1/m$, in agreement with
(4.13). So we start only from the single-particle LLL
orbitals, the Pauli exclusion principle and a heuristic argument about correlations, and we arrive
at the exact ground state of a strongly interacting many-body problem, which will give us
fractional charge in the next section.

\section{Quasiholes and quasielectrons}

\emph{From this point to the end of Section 4.18, the material has no counterpart in
\texttt{session4.ipynb}. The part of the notebook for this session stops at the integer quantum
Hall state, its Landau levels, and its edge and Chern-insulator physics, which are Sections 4.10,
4.11 and 4.19 below. The Laughlin wavefunction, the quasiholes and quasielectrons, and the
Berry-phase argument for fractional charge and anyonic statistics come from the transcript and the
slides, and there is no numerical exercise built around them.}

The excitations above the Laughlin ground state (4.12) also have a simple construction in these
coordinates. A \textbf{quasihole} at the origin is obtained when we multiply the wavefunction by an
extra overall factor $z_i$ for every electron. A quasihole at an arbitrary position $w$ is obtained
instead when we insert a factor $(z_i-w)$ for each electron,

\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\text{qh}}(w) = \prod_i (z_i-w) \; \Psi_m(z_1,\dots,z_N).
\end{equation}

This extra factor raises by one the effective power of every orbital. With the relation between
angular momentum and radius of Section 4.15, this moves the charge density outward, away from $w$,
and leaves a region with less charge there, which is the quasihole. When we repeat this
construction $m$ times, at $m$ different positions, we remove in total exactly the charge of one
electron, which is consistent with each quasihole having only a fraction $1/m$ of the electron
charge. We will confirm this fraction directly with the Berry phase in Section 4.18. In the same
language, a \textbf{quasielectron} is the inverse operation. Instead of raising the power, which
we do when we multiply by $z_i-w$, we lower it, and we do this with the derivative $\partial/\partial z_i$
acting on the polynomial part instead of a multiplication. This moves charge inward instead of outward, and it gives an
excitation with the opposite, negative, fractional charge.

\section{Berry phase, fractional charge, and anyonic statistics}

We now use the Berry phase, which is the same object that we used in session 3 to build the Chern
number, to show directly two things. First, the quasihole excitation of Section 4.17 has a fraction
of the electron charge. Second, a pair of them follow neither fermionic nor bosonic exchange
statistics.

\textbf{Fractional charge.} We take the wavefunction with one quasihole (4.14) and move $w$
adiabatically around a closed loop. We write $w=|w|e^{i\theta}$ and compute the Berry phase that
we obtain when $\theta$ goes from $0$ to $2\pi$. The result is an Aharonov-Bohm phase,
proportional to the flux enclosed by the loop, but with an extra prefactor $1/m$ with respect to
the phase that an ordinary particle with charge $e$ would get around the same loop. The
Aharonov-Bohm phase for a particle with charge $q$ around an enclosed flux $\Phi$ is $2\pi
q\Phi/\Phi_0$, where $\Phi_0$ is the flux quantum, so the extra factor $1/m$ that we find is a
direct and clear statement about the \emph{charge} of the excitation,

\begin{equation}
q_{\text{qh}} = \frac{e}{m}.
\end{equation}

For these excitations the electron is no longer the basic unit of charge, and the unit is a real
fraction of it.

\textbf{Anyonic statistics.} Now we take a wavefunction with \emph{two} quasiholes, one fixed at
the origin and one at $w$, and we move the second one in a loop around the first, again with
$w=|w|e^{i\theta}$. The Berry phase that we obtain has two parts. The first part is the same
Aharonov-Bohm contribution as before, given by the charge times the enclosed flux. The second part
is an additional \textbf{statistical phase}, which comes only from the fact that one quasiparticle
goes around the other,

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{stat}} = \frac{2\pi}{m}.
\end{equation}

To compare with the familiar cases, note that to move one
quasiparticle all the way around another is \emph{two} exchanges, one after the other, so the
exchange phase is half of (4.16), which is $\pi/m$. For ordinary fermions the exchange of two
particles gives a phase $\pi$, and for bosons it gives a phase $0$. The exchange phase $\pi/m$ is
neither of the two, and it goes from one familiar case to the other as $m$ grows, as shown in
figure~\ref{fig:s04:quasihole}. These quasiparticles are what are called \textbf{anyons}, which
are excitations with exchange statistics that go continuously from the fermionic to the bosonic
case, and they are labeled by the same integer $m$ that fixed the filling fraction. We started from
a many-body wavefunction built only from ordinary electrons, which are real fermions, and from the
correlations of this wavefunction we obtained excitations that are neither fermions nor bosons.
This is the simplest example of fractionalization in a quantum many-body system that we will meet
in this course.

\section{The Haldane model and the quantum Hall effect without Landau levels}

This last section goes back on purpose to a model that we already know, and it is not new
material. In session 3 we already built the Haldane model, as an example of a Chern insulator
obtained by opening a gap in a Dirac cone without any external field. We come back to it here
because it closes the argument that we started in this session. Everything above needed a real
magnetic field to give Landau levels, so we want to confirm explicitly that the quantized Hall
conductance itself never needed one.

Let us recall the construction. We start from the honeycomb lattice with first-neighbor hopping
only, which has a Dirac cone without a gap at $K$ and $K'$, and we add a second-neighbor hopping
that is purely imaginary, which breaks time-reversal symmetry. In the Bloch Hamiltonian this term
appears as a mass at $K$ and $K'$. An ordinary staggered onsite potential also gives a mass at
both valleys, but the two masses enter with opposite relative sign between the valleys. For the
staggered potential the Berry curvatures of the two valleys have opposite sign and cancel, which
gives $C=0$. For the Haldane term, the imaginary second-neighbor hopping, the Berry curvatures of
the two valleys have the \emph{same} sign, so when we integrate over the Brillouin zone the two
valley contributions add and do not cancel, which gives $C=\pm1$. The result is a net nonzero
Chern number, an insulating bulk gap and a chiral edge state, all with $B=0$.

What is really new in our version of this story is the explicit \textbf{adiabatic connection}
between the Haldane model and the ordinary quantum Hall state of this session, which is driven by
the field. We start in the Haldane model, with imaginary second-neighbor hopping and no field.
Then we switch on a real magnetic field while we keep the Haldane coupling, and Landau levels
develop from the bands that were dispersive before. Finally we switch off the Haldane coupling,
and the system is left in the ordinary quantum Hall state built only from Landau levels. At every
step of this path the bulk gap stays open and the chiral edge state never disappears. Since we can
connect the two end points without closing the gap, the Chern insulator of the Haldane model and
the quantum Hall state of the Landau levels are \emph{topologically equivalent}. They are the same
phase of matter, reached by two completely different physical routes, one that needs a magnetic
field and one that does not.

\section{Real materials realizing Chern and fractional Chern physics}

\emph{This survey, like Sections 4.17-4.18, comes from the transcript and the slides and has no
counterpart in the notebook. There is no numerical exercise in \texttt{session4.ipynb} built around
any of the specific compounds below.}

The Chern insulator at zero field is not only a theoretical idea. Chromium-doped
$(\text{Bi,Sb})_2\text{Te}_3$, which is a magnetically doped topological insulator, breaks
time-reversal symmetry through real magnetism and realizes an integer quantized Hall conductance
with no applied field. Twisted graphene bilayers aligned with a boron nitride substrate realize the
same physics with a different mechanism, which is based on orbital currents and not on magnetic
moments. On the fractional side, twisted two-dimensional materials can host topological flat bands
with a mathematical structure similar to the one of an artificial pseudo-magnetic field, which
comes from the elastic reconstruction of the lattice and not from a real field. Twisted bilayers of
molybdenum ditelluride (MoTe$_2$) are a recent and striking example, and they show real fractional
quantum Hall physics at zero magnetic field.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item The Hall conductivity is a topological invariant, the Chern number of the occupied bands, as
  we derived in more detail in session 3. Equation (4.1) is the same result, which we confirm here
  and do not derive again from the beginning.
\item A magnetic field turns a two-dimensional electron gas into a set of flat Landau levels, each
  with Chern number one, so the Hall conductivity is quantized to $N e^2/h$ for $N$ filled Landau
  levels, equation (4.2).
\item An insulating bulk still conducts because a nonzero Chern number gives chiral edge states
  without backscattering at the boundary of the sample (Section 4.5), which we can see directly in
  the band structures of ribbons.
\item Landau levels come from minimal coupling (4.3) or, on a lattice, from the Peierls
  substitution (4.4). Schrödinger electrons give equally spaced levels
  $E_n=\hbar\omega_c(n+\tfrac12)$, equation (4.6), while Dirac electrons give $E_n\propto\sqrt{nB}$,
  equation (4.7), and this explains the much higher temperature scale of the quantum Hall effect in
  graphene.
\item In the symmetric gauge, the wavefunctions of the lowest Landau level are holomorphic,
  $\psi_m(z)\propto z^m e^{-|z|^2/4\ell_B^2}$, equation (4.10). The many-body state of the filled
  LLL is the Vandermonde determinant times a collective Gaussian, equation (4.11).
\item When we raise the Vandermonde factor to an odd power $m$ we obtain the Laughlin wavefunction,
  equation (4.12), with filling factor $\nu=1/m$, equation (4.13). Its quasihole excitations,
  equation (4.14), have fractional charge $e/m$, equation (4.15), and follow anyonic statistics.
  They get a phase $2\pi/m$ for a full braid, equation (4.16), which is an exchange phase $\pi/m$
  strictly between the bosonic $0$ and the fermionic $\pi$, and this is the simplest case of
  fractionalization that we will meet.
\item The Haldane model shows that the quantized Hall conductance never strictly needed a magnetic
  field, and that it is adiabatically and topologically equivalent to the quantum Hall state driven
  by the field. This is a return to session 3 and not new theory.
\end{itemize}

In this session the order was driven only by a magnetic field, which is an external
symmetry-breaking parameter that we switched on by hand. In the next session we will see a
completely different route to order, which is the attractive interaction between the electrons
themselves. This interaction drives the system to become a superconductor without any external
field, and we will see that the same classification into trivial and chiral topological states,
which we built here and in session 3, comes back to sort superconducting order parameters into
conventional and topological classes. It is also useful to keep in mind the form of
fractionalization that we just built, since we will soon meet a second example of it, which looks
quite different. Here, the correlations inside a partially filled topological flat band split the
charge of the electron and made its exchange statistics anyonic. In the next session, pairing
correlations in a superconductor will split the electron into two Majorana halves instead. The
underlying idea is the same, which is a correlated topological state with excitations that are not
the electron itself, but it is reached by two really different mechanisms.
