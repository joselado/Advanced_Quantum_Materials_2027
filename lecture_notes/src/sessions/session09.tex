\chapter{Excitations and Defects in Quantum Materials}

In Session 8 we built a small set of new ground states. These were quantum spin liquids described
by mean-field spinons, and Kondo lattices, where the local moments are screened by the conduction
electrons with the same Abrikosov-fermion construction, so that a metal becomes a heavy-fermion
insulator. Up to now every session has followed the same procedure. We take a many-body
Hamiltonian, we write it in a controlled or approximate way, and we read the resulting ground state. In this session we use all of these methods in a different way. Instead of building
one more ground state, we will \emph{probe} the ground states that we already have. The idea is easy
to state. We take a quantum material, which can be a metal, a superconductor, a topological insulator
or a quantum magnet, we disturb it a little, and we look at how it responds. The response itself is
computed only from the undisturbed ground state, and it tells us a lot about what that ground state
is. We will use this one idea, linear response, as a probe of almost every class of systems that
we have built in the course so far. These are ordinary metals, the superconductors of session 5,
the topological matter of sessions 3 and 4, and the quantum magnets of session 8. In each case we
will see that the response function works as a diagnostic, and it gives us Fermi surfaces,
superconducting pairing symmetries, topological invariants and spin gaps, all from the same formalism.
We will also see that the Stoner criterion for itinerant magnetism follows directly from the same
language of response functions. This connects again to the magnetism of session 1 and session 6, but
now we see magnetism as an \emph{instability} of the paramagnetic response and not as a mean-field
ansatz for the ground state that we impose by hand. In this sense, this is the diagnostic and synthesis
session of the course, which gives a new way to look at everything that we have built.

\section{Response functions as a probe of the ground state}

Let us first set up the general experiment, since it will come back in every example of this
session. We take a quantum material with a ground state that we understand,
or that we think we understand, and we make a small perturbation at position $x$ and time $t$. Then we
measure some observable at a different position $x'$ and at a different time $t'$, which is
typically later. The relation between what we did at $(x,t)$ and what we observe at $(x',t')$ is
contained in a \textbf{response
function}. Once we know that function completely, we know how the material reacts to a
perturbation applied at any place and at any time.

This point of view does not depend on the observable that we choose, and the same formalism
describes the following cases.

\begin{itemize}
\item A perturbation in the local electrostatic potential, measured as a change in the local charge
  density, which gives the \textbf{dielectric function}.
\item A local magnetic moment or field, measured as a change in the local magnetization at another
  place, which gives the \textbf{magnetic susceptibility}, also called the spin-fluctuation response.
\item A local change of temperature, measured as a change of temperature at another place, which gives
  the \textbf{thermal conductance}.
\end{itemize}

There are also two quite different ways to do this experiment in the lab, and we will use both in
this session. The first way is a real
physical perturbation. If we replace one atom of a metal by an atom of a different element, we have
created a real, localized impurity. As we will see in detail in Section 9.6, the \textbf{Friedel
oscillations} that appear in the electronic density show us directly the Fermi surface of the
material. In the second way, the measurement itself is the perturbation. We send a neutron, whose
magnetic moment couples to the local moments in a magnetic material, and the scattering
cross-section that we record gives directly the magnetic fluctuations of the material, which is
called inelastic neutron scattering. These two experiments look very different, but the formalism
behind them, and the kind of information that we obtain, is the same in both cases.

Finally, a simplification makes almost everything below possible to compute. When the
unperturbed Hamiltonian has translational symmetry in space and does not depend on time, the response
function can only depend on $x'-x$ and $t'-t$, and not on $x,t,x',t'$ separately. In this situation
the natural language is to Fourier transform to reciprocal space and frequency, $(q,\omega)$, and
this is the language that we will use for almost all the rest of this session.

\section{The response of a classical magnet as a first example}

Before we use any quantum many-body method, let us see how the response predicts the ground state
in a setting that we can solve exactly. This
setting is a classical Ising magnet at finite temperature, treated in mean-field theory, and
Section 1.12 gives the mean-field method itself. At a temperature well above any ordering transition, is there a measurement that we can do
\emph{at that temperature}, in the disordered phase, which tells us what kind of order the system will
develop when we cool it down?

The relevant response is the \textbf{magnetic susceptibility}, $\chi = \partial M/\partial H$, which is
the linear-response coefficient that relates an applied field $H$ to the magnetization $M$ that it
induces. If we solve the mean-field Ising model at high temperature, we find three different
behaviors, which depend on the sign and the strength of the exchange coupling.

\begin{equation}
\chi(T) \;\propto\;
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{T} & \text{paramagnet (no exchange coupling), diverges only at } T=0, \\[4pt]
\dfrac{1}{T - T_c} & \text{ferromagnet, diverges at a positive } T_c, \\[4pt]
\dfrac{1}{T + T_c} & \text{antiferromagnet, apparent divergence at a negative temperature.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The physical picture behind (9.1) is important, because we will find it again in a fully quantum
form in Section 9.5. In the paramagnetic regime the thermal fluctuations dominate, and the
susceptibility stays finite at any $T>0$, since nothing in the system tends to order. In the
ferromagnetic case the fluctuations \emph{reinforce} the applied field, so as $T\to T_c^+$ the
system becomes polarized more and more easily, and $\chi$ really diverges at the transition. In the
antiferromagnetic case the fluctuations \emph{oppose} the applied field, since they favor staggered
order and not uniform order, and this appears as the opposite sign in the denominator. The
susceptibility then seems to go towards a divergence at a temperature below absolute zero. Of course
it never reaches it, but the sign alone already tells us that the system tends to antiferromagnetic
order and not to ferromagnetic order. In experiments this is a practical diagnostic. We plot
$\chi^{-1}(T)$, and if the straight line extrapolates to a positive intercept, the system is close to
a ferromagnet, while a negative intercept means that the system is close to an antiferromagnet.
All of this is measured in the disordered phase at high temperature. This is the model for the whole
session, which is that \textbf{the response of the disordered/uniform state,
computed without ever solving for the ordered state itself, already contains the information about
which ordered state is coming.}

\section{Linear response and perturbation theory in detail}

Let us now build the quantum version of this idea, since everything that follows is based on it. We split the full Hamiltonian into an unperturbed part $H_0$, which can be a metal, a
superconductor, a quantum magnet or any system that we study, and a perturbation $V$. We also add a
coupling constant $\lambda$ that helps us keep track of the orders, and at the end we will set it
to one.

\begin{equation}
H(\lambda) = H_0 + \lambda V, \qquad \lambda = 0 \;\text{(pristine)}, \quad \lambda = 1\;\text{(full perturbation)}.
\end{equation}

Every observable of the system, such as energies, densities, magnetizations and the wavefunctions
themselves, becomes a function of $\lambda$ once we fix $H(\lambda)$. If this function is smooth
enough to make a Taylor expansion around $\lambda=0$, and later we will see when this assumption
fails, then for any observable $A$ we have

\begin{equation*}
A(\lambda) = A^{(0)} + \lambda\, \chi_A + O(\lambda^2), \qquad
\chi_A \equiv \left.\frac{\partial A}{\partial \lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0},
\end{equation*}

and by definition $\chi_A$, the first-order coefficient, \emph{is} the response function that we have
been talking about in this session. To compute $\chi_A$ for a generic observable, we first need the
perturbed wavefunctions. We expand an unperturbed eigenstate $|n\rangle$ of $H_0$, with energy
$E_n^{(0)}$, in the full basis $\{|m\rangle\}$ of $H_0$. Standard Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation
theory then gives, to first order in $\lambda$ and away from any degeneracy,

\begin{equation}
|n(\lambda)\rangle = |n\rangle + \lambda \sum_{m \neq n} \frac{\langle m|V|n\rangle}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}} \, |m\rangle + O(\lambda^2).
\end{equation}

The structure of (9.3) is the basis of every derivation in the rest of this session. A perturbation mixes a state $|n\rangle$ with every other state $|m\rangle$.
The weight of the mixing is the matrix element of $V$ between the two states, and it is suppressed by
the inverse of the energy gap between them. States that are close in energy mix strongly, and states
that are far apart in energy almost do not mix. Once we have (9.3), we can write any observable, in
particular any density or magnetization, to first order in $\lambda$ by using the perturbed states.

\textbf{When does this break down?} The construction in (9.3) assumes that $A(\lambda)$ is an
analytic function of $\lambda$ close to $\lambda=0$, and this assumption is not always true. There
are three paradigmatic quantum states that are known to be intrinsically non-perturbative in this
sense, and we mention them explicitly since they mark the limit of everything that we derive below.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An impurity in a metal, the orthogonality catastrophe.} In the thermodynamic limit, the full
  many-body wavefunction of a metal with the impurity switched on becomes exactly orthogonal to the
  wavefunction with the impurity switched off, for any strength of the impurity potential, even a
  very weak one.
\item \textbf{A quantum spin in a metal, the Kondo effect.} The emergent Kondo temperature is a
  non-perturbative function of the Kondo coupling, since it contains an essential singularity in the
  coupling constant and not a power series.
\item \textbf{Superconductivity in the BCS limit.} In the same way, the superconducting gap is
  non-perturbative in the attractive coupling constant.
\end{itemize}

In the rest of this session we stay in the regime where perturbation theory \emph{does} work, which
is the regime where (9.2) and (9.3) give a controlled and systematic way to compute responses.

\section{The Fermi sea and the Lindhard function}

To make (9.3) concrete, let us take the case that we will use for most of this session, which is a
non-interacting electronic Hamiltonian in diagonal form, $H_0 = \sum_k \varepsilon(k)\, c_k^\dagger
c_k$. Its many-body ground state is the Fermi sea, where every single-particle state with
$\varepsilon(k)$ below the Fermi energy is filled and every state above it is empty. In the same way,
$\langle c_k^\dagger c_k\rangle = n_F(\varepsilon(k))$, which is equal to $1$ for occupied states and
$0$ for unoccupied states at $T=0$, and more generally is the Fermi-Dirac function. The set of
$k$-points that are exactly at the Fermi energy defines the \textbf{Fermi surface}. This surface will
be the most important geometric object in everything that follows, because a small perturbation can
only change the states that are close to it. To move an electron from deep inside the Fermi sea to
far above it costs too much energy to be relevant at first order.

Now we use (9.3) with a specific perturbation that has a clear physical meaning, which is a local
shift of the onsite energy. This is a local change of the chemical potential at one point of the
material. The observable that we follow is the change in the local electronic density,
$n(x) - n_0(x)$. We use translational symmetry, we Fourier transform to $q$-space, and we insert the
perturbed wavefunctions (9.3) into the density operator. After some algebra, where each term comes
from a matrix element $\langle k+q|V|k\rangle$ divided by the energy denominator
$\varepsilon(k+q)-\varepsilon(k)$, with the same structure as (9.3), we obtain the \textbf{static
Lindhard function},

\begin{equation}
\chi_0(q) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_k \frac{n_F(\varepsilon(k)) - n_F(\varepsilon(k+q))}{\varepsilon(k+q) - \varepsilon(k)}.
\end{equation}

Let us first check (9.4). As $q\to 0$, we expand both the
numerator and the denominator to first order in $q$ and we obtain
$\chi_0(q\to 0) \to -\sum_k n_F'(\varepsilon(k))/N$. At $T=0$ this is exactly $D(E_F)$, the density
of states per site at the Fermi level, which is a positive and finite number, as expected. Equation
(9.4) is different from zero only when $k$ is occupied and $k+q$ is unoccupied, or the opposite. If
both are occupied or both are empty, the numerator vanishes. So the response is built only from
states on the two sides of the Fermi surface, as the general argument above predicted.

\textbf{Instabilities and nesting.} Equation (9.4) has a strong feature in one dimension. If there is
a wavevector $q^\star$ that connects an occupied state at $k$ to an empty state at $k+q^\star$ with
the \emph{same} energy, the denominator in (9.4) vanishes for that term, and $\chi_0(q^\star)$
diverges. For a generic 1D band the Fermi surface is only the two points $\pm k_F$, and the single
nesting vector that connects them is $q^\star = 2k_F$ (figure~\ref{fig:s09:nesting}a,b). When we sum
(9.4) over the whole band we obtain a logarithmic divergence, $\chi_0(2k_F) \sim \ln(W/T)$, where $W$
is a cutoff given by the bandwidth. This divergence is the Peierls instability, which drives
one-dimensional metals to dimerize into a charge density wave. If a response function diverges, an arbitrarily weak perturbation at that wavevector gives an
arbitrarily large change of the ground state, meaning that the ground state that we started from is
unstable to that specific fluctuation. This is the central strategy of this session. To predict a new phase of matter, we look for the wavevector where the response function of the old
phase diverges.

Everything above can be generalized directly to finite frequency. If we repeat the derivation with a
perturbation that depends on time as $\propto e^{-i\omega t}$, we obtain the retarded
\textbf{dynamical Lindhard function},

\begin{equation}
\chi_0(q,\omega) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_k \frac{n_F(\varepsilon(k)) - n_F(\varepsilon(k+q))}{\varepsilon(k+q) - \varepsilon(k) - \hbar\omega - i0^+},
\end{equation}

which becomes (9.4) at $\omega = 0$. For a one-dimensional chain, if we plot
$\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_0(q,\omega)$ over the whole $(q,\omega)$ plane, we obtain a characteristic surface
of divergences that follows the particle-hole continuum of the band structure. This $\omega$-$q$ plot
is only on the slides. In the spoken lecture its shape is described in words, as ``a whole collection
of $\omega$ versus $q$ values where there are divergences'', but it is not drawn, so it is useful to
open the slides to see the figure.

The same logic extends directly to two dimensions. For a square lattice with dispersion
$\varepsilon(k) = -2t(\cos k_x + \cos k_y)$, a low filling gives a small Fermi surface with a shape
close to a circle. Then $\chi_0(q,0)$ is large for $q$ inside a circle whose size is set by the
diameter of the Fermi surface, and small outside it, so the response function is a direct image of
the Fermi surface. This is the physical basis of \textbf{quasiparticle interference (QPI)}. In real STM
experiments, impurities are put into a 2D metal, the modulation of the local density of states that
they create is imaged, and the observed wavelength is inverted to obtain the Fermi surface. At half
filling, however, the specific dispersion of the square lattice gives $\varepsilon(k+Q) =
-\varepsilon(k)$ for $Q=(\pi,\pi)$. This is a \emph{perfect} nesting condition, which connects very
large parts of the Fermi surface with the same wavevector at the same time, and at half filling the
Fermi surface itself is a square made of straight lines and not a circle. Equation (9.4) then has a
very large divergence at $q=(\pi,\pi)$, which signals a strong instability there.
Figure~\ref{fig:s09:nesting} shows the whole argument in one place. It shows the single 1D nesting
vector, the logarithmic divergence that it gives in $\chi_0(q)$, and the 2D Fermi surface with
straight edges that $Q=(\pi,\pi)$ nests all at once. The
shape of the Fermi surface at half filling and the explicit nesting vector are shown on the slides in
a more concrete way than in the spoken lecture. The figure shows the straight parallel edges that make the $(\pi,\pi)$ nesting so
effective.

\section{Interactions as a perturbation, with RPA, plasmons and the Stoner instability}

So far the perturbation has been an external impurity. However, we can also treat the
electron-electron interaction \emph{itself} as the perturbation. We use $\chi_0$, the
non-interacting response that we just built, as input, and we ask for the response $\chi$ of the full
interacting system. For this we need two things. The first is a rule that connects $\chi$ to $\chi_0$,
and the second is a criterion to find instabilities from $\chi$.

\textbf{From $\chi_0$ to $\chi$ with the random phase approximation.} Let us take a contact interaction,
of Hubbard type, with strength $U$, which couples to the density. The physical picture is a
diagrammatic one. The response to a probe is the bare response $\chi_0$, plus the bare response
followed by one interaction event and another bare response, plus two interaction events, and so on,
which is an infinite ladder of terms. The most efficient way to include all of them is not to sum the
series term by term, but to write the self-consistent equation that the full response must satisfy,

\begin{equation*}
\chi(q,\omega) = \chi_0(q,\omega) - \chi_0(q,\omega)\, U\, \chi(q,\omega),
\end{equation*}

and its algebraic solution gives the \textbf{random phase approximation (RPA)},

\begin{equation}
\chi(q,\omega) = \frac{\chi_0(q,\omega)}{1 + U\,\chi_0(q,\omega)}.
\end{equation}

Equation (9.6) has a mixed character. It is derived order by order in $U$, so it is perturbative,
but since we sum the whole geometric series it becomes non-perturbative. In this way it includes
physics that no finite order in $U$ can give, which is a real pole, meaning a real phase transition.

\textbf{Two different sources of divergence.} The interacting response (9.6) can diverge for two quite
different reasons. It is useful to separate them, because they correspond to two different emergent
quasiparticles.

\emph{From $\chi_0$ itself}, as in Section 9.4. This is an electron-hole excitation, which is a
single-particle process that moves one electron from an occupied state to an empty state.

\emph{From the RPA denominator}, $1+U\chi_0=0$, which is a new collective phenomenon that comes from
the interaction. For a long-range Coulomb interaction, $U \to v(q)$, which depends strongly on $q$
and diverges as $q\to0$. The pole of (9.6) as a function of $\omega$ at fixed $q$ then defines a new
quasiparticle, the \textbf{plasmon}, which is a collective charge oscillation of the whole electron
gas. Its dispersion depends on the screening and on the dimensionality of the system. In the standard
RPA treatment of the three-dimensional electron gas, which is the Bohm-Pines treatment, at long
wavelength the dispersion has the form

\begin{equation}
\omega_{\rm pl}(q)^2 = \omega_p^2 + \tfrac{3}{5} v_F^2\, q^2 + O(q^4),
\end{equation}

where $\omega_p$ is the classical plasma frequency. The plasmon exists even at $q=0$, which is
different from the particle-hole continuum, because it is a collective mode of the whole charge
density and not a single-particle excitation. Figure~\ref{fig:s09:plasmon} shows both kinds of
divergence in the same $(q,\omega)$ plane, and it shows explicitly the window at small $q$ where the
pole exists and no single-particle excitation exists.

\input{figures/s09f02_plasmon}

\textbf{The Stoner criterion as an instability of the response function.} Now we use the same RPA logic
in the \emph{spin} channel of an interaction of Hubbard type, so that $\chi_0$ in (9.6) becomes the
non-interacting spin susceptibility. The sign is the opposite of the density channel, because
$U n_\uparrow n_\downarrow = \tfrac{U}{4}(n^2 - m^2)$ is repulsive for the density and attractive for the
magnetization, so the same $U$ screens the charge response and enhances the spin response, and the
denominator of (9.6) becomes $1-U\chi_0$. The interacting spin response diverges when

\begin{equation}
U\, \chi_0(q,0) = 1.
\end{equation}

At $q=0$ we use the limit $q\to0$ of (9.4), $\chi_0(0,0) = D(E_F)$, so (9.8) becomes
$U\,D(E_F) = 1$. This is exactly the \textbf{Stoner criterion} for itinerant ferromagnetism, which
says that an interacting metal becomes magnetic once the product of the density of states at the
Fermi level and the interaction strength is larger than one. However, (9.8) is more general than the
special case $q=0$. For a Fermi surface with strong nesting, such as the half-filled square lattice
of Section 9.4, $\chi_0(q,0)$ has its \emph{maximum} not at $q=0$ but at the nesting vector
$Q=(\pi,\pi)$, where it is already logarithmically enhanced. The instability threshold in (9.8) is
then reached first at $Q=(\pi,\pi)$ and not at $q=0$. As a result, the system develops a
\emph{staggered} antiferromagnetic instability and not a uniform ferromagnetic one, and this happens
at a smaller value of $U$ than the simple Stoner estimate predicts.

This is the connection that the course has been preparing. In Sessions 1 and 6 we obtained magnetism by writing the Hubbard model, applying mean-field
theory directly to the \emph{ordered} state, and solving self-consistently for a non-zero
magnetization $m$, as in Equation 1.7 of Section 1.14. In this session we have obtained the
\emph{same physics}, with the same threshold $U\,D(E_F)=1$ and the same preference for
antiferromagnetism at half filling, without writing any ordered mean-field ansatz. Instead, we asked
when the \emph{paramagnetic}, disordered response function develops a divergence, which is the same
logic as the classical susceptibility of Section 9.2. Mean-field theory and the response function
are two ways to reach the same instability. The first one computes it from the side where the
symmetry is broken, and the second one computes it only from the symmetric side, by following how
this state becomes unstable.

The same method, applied to different response channels and to different signs of the interaction,
classifies a whole family of instabilities, which we write here explicitly. On the slides this
classification into three cases is presented as a clear summary table, in a more systematic way than
in the spoken lecture, which mainly worked out the magnetic case in detail.

\begin{center}

\hline
Instability & Response channel & Interaction sign \\
\hline
Magnetism & spin response & repulsive \\
Dimerization / charge density wave & charge-phonon response & repulsive \\
Superconductivity & electron-electron scattering response & attractive \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Impurities as diagnostics I, Friedel oscillations in a metal}

We now go to the most physical realization of a perturbation, which is a real local impurity. Four
case studies follow, one for each broad class of ground state that we have built in the course. These
are metals, superconductors, topological matter and quantum magnets. In each case the diagnostic
logic is the same. We perturb the system locally, and we obtain the nature of the ground state from
how the effect of the perturbation decays in space.

Let us start with a metal. An impurity is a local shift of the onsite energy. If we add one to a
Hamiltonian with translational symmetry, this symmetry is necessarily broken. Note, however, that
perturbation theory itself never needed translational symmetry, since equations (9.2) and (9.3) are
valid for any $H_0$ and any $V$. If we apply them directly to the local density of states, we obtain
a response that is proportional to matrix elements between states at the Fermi surface. For a
generic band, close to a parabolic one, the oscillation that results is set only by the Fermi
wavevector.

To see this in an exact way, note that the static Lindhard function of a one-dimensional
free-electron gas has the closed form

\begin{equation*}
\chi_0(q) = \frac{m}{\pi\hbar^2 q}\ln\left|\frac{2k_F + q}{2k_F - q}\right|,
\end{equation*}

which is smooth everywhere except for a logarithmic non-analyticity exactly at $q = 2k_F$. This is
the same nesting singularity that we found in Section 9.4. A standard result of Fourier analysis is
that a non-analyticity of a function $\chi_0(q)$ at a wavevector $q^\star$ gives an oscillating tail
with an algebraic decay in its real-space Fourier transform. The period of this tail is fixed by
$q^\star$, and its envelope is set by the \emph{type} of the singularity. We apply this to the change
of density induced by the impurity,
$\delta n(x) = \int \frac{dq}{2\pi}\, e^{iqx}\, \chi_0(q)\, v_0$, in the Born approximation with
impurity strength $v_0$, and we obtain the \textbf{Friedel oscillation},

\begin{equation}
\delta n(x) \;\xrightarrow{|x|\to\infty}\; A(v_0)\, \frac{\cos\!\big(2k_F |x| + \varphi_0\big)}{|x|^{\,d}} \qquad (d = 1,2,3),
\end{equation}

The oscillation has a universal wavevector that is fixed only by the Fermi surface, which is $2k_F$ in
1D, or more generally the diameter of the Fermi surface. It has an algebraic envelope, whose power
depends on the dimensionality $d$, and it has an amplitude and a phase that are not universal and are
set by the impurity strength and by microscopic details. This closes the argument that we started in
Section 9.4. The same singularity at $q=2k_F$ in $\chi_0(q)$, which we
found there as an instability channel, gives here a long-ranged real-space oscillation with a
power-law decay, and not a real instability. The reason is that a single local impurity is a
perturbation that is much too weak to reorganize the whole Fermi sea. The divergence in $\chi_0(q)$
only makes its Fourier partner decay slowly instead of exponentially.

For a concrete example, let us take a one-dimensional tight-binding chain with an impurity at site
$0$. The local density of states oscillates around the impurity with wavelength $\pi/k_F$, which is
half of the Fermi wavelength $\lambda_F=2\pi/k_F$, since the modulation is set by $2k_F$ and not by
$k_F$. If we raise the chemical potential, $k_F^{-1}$ becomes shorter and the oscillation becomes
faster in the same way. The same phenomenon, with the same physical content, appears in a
two-dimensional square lattice. This real-space LDOS modulation around impurities is what an STM
measures in practice, and it is quasiparticle interference again, seen this time in real space and
not in reciprocal space. The explicit plots of the LDOS as a function of position at two different
fillings, for both the 1D and the 2D case, are clearer on the slides than in words. The comparison
that shows that the ``oscillations get faster as $k_F$ grows'' is a visual one, and it is better seen than read from the
formula. As a complementary check, the slides and the
notebook also show the \emph{insulating} case. An impurity placed in a gapped 1D chain gives an LDOS
that converges very quickly to its uniform value, instead of oscillating with a slow power-law
envelope. This is a first view of the diagnostic that separates gapped and gapless systems, which we
will make fully general in Section 9.9 and draw in figure~\ref{fig:s09:decay}.

\section{Impurities as diagnostics II, magnetic impurities in a superconductor}

Superconductors behave in a completely different way under a local perturbation, because for
single-particle excitations a superconductor is an insulator in the bulk. There is a finite gap
$\Delta$, so there is no Fermi surface that can give oscillations of the Friedel type. The interesting
physics is instead the difference between magnetic and non-magnetic impurities.

Let us remember that an s-wave Cooper pair binds one spin-up electron and one spin-down electron. A
\textbf{non-magnetic} impurity, which is an ordinary local potential, does not distinguish spin up from
spin down. So it does not break time-reversal symmetry, and it cannot lower the energy needed to break
any specific Cooper pair. In practice, the density of states of a conventional s-wave superconductor
is almost the same with and without a non-magnetic impurity, including the gap, and the slides show
the two spectra on top of each other.

A \textbf{magnetic} impurity is completely different. It couples with strength $J$ to the local spin,
it distinguishes up from down, and so it \emph{does} break time-reversal symmetry. This lowers the
energy needed to break one specific Cooper pair and gives a real bound state inside the gap. In the
approximation of a classical spin, in the Born approximation, this is the
\textbf{Yu-Shiba-Rusinov} problem, which is not named in the transcript, but the setup is the
same. With $\alpha \equiv \pi
\nu_0 J S$, where $\nu_0$ is the density of states of the normal state at the Fermi level and $S$ is
the impurity spin, the energy of the bound state in the gap is

\begin{equation}
E_b(J) = \Delta\, \frac{1-\alpha^2}{1+\alpha^2}.
\end{equation}

Equation (9.10) gives the qualitative curve that the slides show as a function of $J$. At
$J=0$ ($\alpha=0$), $E_b = \Delta$, which is at the gap edge, meaning that there is no real bound
state yet. As $J$ increases, $\alpha$ grows and $E_b$ moves down through the gap. At $\alpha = 1$,
which is $\pi\nu_0 J S = 1$, the state crosses zero energy exactly, and for $\alpha>1$ the numerator
of (9.10) becomes negative, so the bound state appears again on the other side of zero. This crossing
is a real quantum phase transition, where the fermion parity of the electronic ground state changes.
Below it the electronic ground state is even, with total electron spin $S_{\rm
tot}=0$, where every Cooper pair is intact and the impurity spin is not screened. Above it the
electronic ground state has $S_{\rm tot}=1/2$, since one quasiparticle is bound to the impurity
and partially screens its spin. So a superconductor with a single magnetic impurity that we can tune is a minimal,
exactly solvable model of a quantum phase transition, which is driven only by a local perturbation.
Both parts of this picture are drawn in figure~\ref{fig:s09:ysr}. It shows the in-gap states that a
magnetic impurity adds to a BCS spectrum that is otherwise unchanged, and the path that (9.10)
follows across the gap as $\alpha$ grows.

\section{Impurities as diagnostics III, vacancies in topological matter}

The strongest local impurity that is possible is a \textbf{vacancy}, where we remove a site
completely, which is the same as sending its onsite potential to infinity. Here the bulk-boundary
correspondence that we built in sessions 3 and 4 gives a direct result. This correspondence includes
the Chern number, the Zak phase, and the guarantee of gapless modes at any boundary of a
topologically non-trivial phase. It is known that topological boundary modes survive around a large
hole cut into a topological material. If we make that hole smaller, down to a single missing site,
the argument still works. A vacancy is in practice a boundary of zero size, and the bulk-boundary
correspondence still applies. When the bulk has a non-trivial topological invariant, a vacancy is
guaranteed to host an in-gap bound state, as long as the vacancy itself preserves the symmetry that
protects that invariant.

This gives a useful diagnostic, because a fully gapped bulk spectrum alone cannot distinguish a
topological phase from a trivial one. Let us take a \textbf{chiral, spin-triplet topological
superconductor}, which is the class that we studied with Majorana modes and the Chern-number
classification in session 5. Without impurities, its bulk spectrum is fully gapped, and by itself it
looks the same as the spectrum of a trivial s-wave superconductor. But a vacancy is a non-magnetic
perturbation. It is a local change of the potential that does not distinguish spin, even in the
extreme limit where the local potential diverges, so it does not change the topological invariant of
the bulk, and once we add it, an in-gap mode appears. The
same logic holds for a \textbf{Chern insulator}. A vacancy in a system with a finite Chern number gives
an in-gap mode, while the same vacancy in a topologically trivial insulator gives no such mode. In
both cases the observable signature is the same and easy to read, which is that \emph{if introducing
vacancies produces in-gap modes, the underlying phase is topological, and if it does not, the phase is
trivial}, regardless of what the clean bulk spectrum alone would tell us.
Figure~\ref{fig:s09:vacancy} draws both the geometric argument, where a hole becomes smaller down to
a single site, and the three spectra that make the diagnostic possible to use.

\section{Impurities as diagnostics IV, screening in quantum magnets}

The last case study brings us directly back to the construction of session 8. We take a frustrated
Heisenberg model with a ground state similar to a quantum spin liquid. This ground state has zero net
magnetization on every site, and it does not break time-reversal symmetry. We add one local magnetic
perturbation, and we ask how the spins around it screen it.

For $S=1/2$ we do not need to repeat the construction from the beginning, since session 8 already
built the tool that we need. We write each spin with a pair of auxiliary (Abrikosov) fermions,
$S_i =
\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta} f_{i\alpha}^\dagger \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta} f_{i\beta}$,
with the single-occupancy constraint $\sum_\sigma f_{i\sigma}^\dagger f_{i\sigma} = 1$ at every
site, and we make a mean-field decoupling of the quartic Hamiltonian that we obtain. As we derived in
session 8, this turns the Heisenberg model with a local magnetic perturbation into a bilinear
Hamiltonian for the spinons, similar to a tight-binding one, with an effective onsite term at the
impurity site that mixes with the spinon band. We will not derive this mapping again here, and the
notes of session 8, in the part on the Abrikosov-fermion construction, give the full mean-field
decoupling.

What matters for us in this session is what this mapping gives us. It turns the problem of screening
a magnetic moment inside a quantum spin liquid into \emph{exactly} the same mathematical problem as
the impurity in a one-dimensional electron gas of Section 9.6, where the electrons are replaced by
spinons. The induced spin correlation $\langle S_0 \cdot S_n\rangle$, as a function of the distance
$n$ from the impurity, therefore has the same structure as (9.9). It has an oscillation set by the
antiferromagnetic exchange, and it decays with distance in a way that depends on whether the spinon
band is gapped or gapless.

Here the comparison between the $S=1/2$ and $S=1$ Heisenberg chains becomes clear. Both have a ground
state similar to a spin liquid, with zero onsite magnetization when there is no impurity, and both
develop an oscillating induced moment once the impurity is switched on. However, the decay is
qualitatively different in the two cases.

\begin{equation}
\langle S_0 \cdot S_n \rangle \;\sim\;
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n} & S = 1/2 \quad \text{(power-law, gapless)}, \\[6pt]
(-1)^n\, e^{-n/\xi} & S = 1 \quad \text{(exponential, gapped)}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The reason is the same reasoning as for (9.9). Through the Abrikosov-fermion construction, the
spin-$1/2$ Heisenberg chain maps onto a gapless tight-binding chain of spinons, with a real Fermi
surface of two points, as for the 1D metal of Section 9.4. So the impurity response must decay
algebraically, in the same way as the Friedel oscillations of a metal. The spin-1 chain, on the other
hand, has an excitation gap. This is the Haldane gap, which is not named explicitly in the
transcript, but it is the gap that opens for antiferromagnetic chains with integer spin. This
gap sets a finite correlation length $\xi$, and it forces the impurity response to decay
exponentially, in the same way as charge screening inside a band insulator.

This gives us a completely general diagnostic, which connects all the case studies of this session,
namely that \emph{a power-law decay of an impurity response signals a gapless ground state, and an
exponential decay signals a gapped one, where the decay length in the exponent is the correlation
length.} The metal of Section 9.6, which has a power law, the insulating chain at the end of that
section, which has an exponential, and the two quantum-magnet cases here are all examples of the same
statement, and they are drawn side by side in figure~\ref{fig:s09:decay}.

\section{Disorder and Anderson localization}

So far we have only discussed a \emph{single} impurity. What happens when we put many impurities in
the whole material? A single impurity changes the ground state only a little. Many impurities, in
particular when they are placed at random, can change it completely, because they break translational
symmetry in the whole system and not only locally. It is useful to list all the possibilities, since
there are three different regimes of translational symmetry to consider, and not only the symmetric
and the non-symmetric ones.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Periodic.} There is full translational symmetry. Every eigenstate is extended, with
   weight $\sim 1/N$ on each of $N$ sites.
\item \textbf{Quasiperiodic.} The system is not periodic, since there is no repeating unit cell, but it
   is not random either, since the potential follows a fixed and deterministic rule that never
   repeats. Here the eigenstates can be extended, critical, or fully localized, depending on the
   strength of the potential. Critical means localized with a power law, so that the state is not even
   normalizable in the strict sense.
\item \textbf{Strongly (randomly) disordered.} The eigenstates are localized, and each of them has a
   finite localization length.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Quasiperiodic case.} The standard model here is a 1D tight-binding chain with an onsite
modulation at an irrational wavevector. It belongs to the Aubry-André-Harper family, although it is
not named in this way in the transcript.

\begin{equation*}
H = -t\sum_n \left(c_n^\dagger c_{n+1} + \text{h.c.}\right) + V_0 \sum_n \cos(2\pi q\, n + \phi)\, c_n^\dagger c_n, \qquad q \notin \mathbb{Q}.
\end{equation*}

Since $q$ is irrational, the potential never repeats exactly, so the chain has no translational
symmetry at all. However, it is not random either, because the value of the potential at every site
is completely fixed once we fix $q$ and $\phi$. As we increase $V_0$ from zero, the eigenstates change
from extended to a marginal, critical behavior with a power law close to the self-dual point of the
model, which is $V_0 = 2t$ in the plain Aubry-André model, and then to exponentially localized states
for $V_0$ well above it.

The tool that we use to distinguish these regimes is the \textbf{inverse participation ratio (IPR)},

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IPR}(\psi) = \sum_i |\psi_i|^4, \qquad \sum_i |\psi_i|^2 = 1.
\end{equation}

An extended state with weight $\sim 1/N$ per site gives $\mathrm{IPR} \sim N\cdot(1/N)^2 = 1/N$,
which vanishes as the system grows. A localized state, with $O(1)$ weight on $O(1)$ sites, gives
$\mathrm{IPR} \sim O(1)$, which does not depend on the system size. A critical state is between the
two, and its $\mathrm{IPR}$ follows a non-trivial power law in $N$. This is a multifractal signature,
which is different from both of the two clean limits. Figure~\ref{fig:s09:localization} shows the
three eigenstates side by side, together with the scaling of (9.12) with $N$, which separates them
in a quantitative way.

\input{figures/s09f06_localization}

\textbf{Fully disordered case.} Now we take an onsite potential that is really random, with strength
$W$, $H = -t\sum_n (c_n^\dagger c_{n+1} + \text{h.c.}) + \sum_n w_n\, c_n^\dagger c_n$, where $w_n$ is
taken uniformly from $[-W,W]$. At $W=0$ this is an ordinary clean metal, with the band structure of
the periodic hopping alone, and as $W\to\infty$ every eigenstate becomes concentrated on a single
site. In dimension $d\geq 3$ there is a real \textbf{Anderson localization transition} at a finite
critical disorder $W_c$. There is a real mobility edge that separates extended metallic states from
localized states, which is the kind of ``critical point'' described on the slides. Below the
transition, or at any energy on the extended side of a mobility edge, the system conducts. Above it,
there are states at every energy, so there is \emph{no spectral gap} in the density of states, but
these states cannot carry current, so there \emph{is} a transport gap. This means that the
\textbf{Anderson insulator} is an insulator with no gap at all in its spectrum, which is a very
unusual state of matter compared with every band insulator or Mott insulator that we have seen so far
in this course.

\textbf{The special role of one dimension.} In $d=1$, and in a marginal way also in $d=2$ according to
the scaling theory of localization, no real metallic phase survives at any non-zero disorder in the
thermodynamic limit, since an arbitrarily small $W>0$ localizes every state at long enough distances.
What survives is a finite localization length $\xi(W)$. For weak disorder close to the band center of
the 1D model, it scales as $\xi(W) \propto (t/W)^2$, which diverges as $W\to 0$. A finite sample that
is shorter than $\xi(W)$ still looks metallic in practice, and only when the length of the sample is
larger than $\xi(W)$ do we see the localized, insulating behavior in transport. This is the exact
sense in which ``weak disorder in 1D'' and ``genuinely metallic'' are never the same statement,
although they can look the same at short length scales.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item A perturbation that is applied to a quantum material and measured at another place defines a
  \textbf{response function}. With translational symmetry and time-translation symmetry, it is most
  natural to write it in $(q,\omega)$-space (Section 9.1).
\item A classical first example already contains the logic of the whole session. The sign of the
  divergence in the magnetic susceptibility at high temperature (9.1) predicts, from the disordered
  phase alone, whether the order at low temperature will be ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic.
\item Linear response theory (9.2)-(9.3) computes the leading effect of any perturbation only from the
  unperturbed ground state. It fails for the orthogonality catastrophe, the Kondo effect and the BCS
  gap, since none of them are connected to the unperturbed state in a perturbative way.
\item For a Fermi sea, the static and dynamical Lindhard functions (9.4)-(9.5) are built only from
  matrix elements between occupied and empty states close to the Fermi surface. They diverge when a
  nesting vector connects large parts of that surface, which signals an instability (Section 9.4).
\item If we sum the interaction to all orders with the random phase approximation (9.6), we find two
  different sources of divergence. These are electron-hole excitations from $\chi_0$ itself, and
  collective plasmon excitations (9.7) from the RPA denominator.
\item The Stoner criterion (9.8), $U\chi_0(q,0)=1$, gives again the magnetism of session 1 and
  session 6 as an \emph{instability of the paramagnetic response function}, including the
  preference for antiferromagnetism at a nested (half-filled) Fermi surface, and not as an ordered
  mean-field ansatz imposed by hand.
\item A local impurity in a metal gives Friedel oscillations (9.9). These are a density modulation
  with period set by $2k_F$ and a power-law decay, and they give a direct image of the Fermi surface,
  which is the same physics that an STM measures with quasiparticle interference.
\item A magnetic impurity in a superconductor creates a Yu-Shiba-Rusinov in-gap bound state (9.10).
  Its energy crosses zero at a real quantum phase transition, where the fermion parity of the electrons
changes and their spin goes as $S_{\rm tot}=0 \to 1/2$, while a
  non-magnetic impurity leaves the spectrum almost unchanged.
\item A vacancy in a topological superconductor or in a Chern insulator gives an in-gap mode only
  because of the bulk-boundary correspondence. This is a diagnostic for topology that a clean and
  fully gapped bulk spectrum alone cannot give (Section 9.8).
\item The screening of a magnetic impurity in a quantum spin liquid uses directly the
  Abrikosov-fermion mean-field construction of session 8, and it has the same mathematics as the
  impurity in a metal of Section 9.6. The induced spin correlation (9.11) decays as a power law for
  the gapless spin-1/2 chain and exponentially for the gapped spin-1 chain. This gives a fully
  general diagnostic of gapped and gapless states from the impurity response alone.
\item Many impurities at the same time can break translational symmetry in the whole system, which
  gives rise to Anderson localization. In $d\geq3$ there is a real mobility edge, which we diagnose
  with the inverse participation ratio (9.12), and an Anderson insulator, which has no spectral gap but
  has a transport gap. In one dimension an arbitrarily weak disorder localizes every state, and the
  localization length diverges as the disorder strength goes to zero (Section 9.10).
\end{itemize}

Every session so far has gone in the same direction. We start from a Hamiltonian, and we compute what
it predicts, which can be an observable, a response function or a ground state. Session 10 goes in
the opposite direction. In this whole course we computed observables from Hamiltonians, and in the
next session we ask the inverse question. Given only the observed data, can we find the Hamiltonian
that produced it? We will see that machine learning is the right tool to make this question precise.
