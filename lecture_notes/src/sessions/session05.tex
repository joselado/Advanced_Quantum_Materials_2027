\chapter{Superconductivity, Nambu Representation and Majorana Physics}

Session 4 closed by holding on to the flavor of fractionalization we had just built there, and by
pointing forward to a second, quite different-looking instance of it we were about to meet:
correlations inside a partially filled topological flat band split the electron's charge and turned
its exchange statistics anyonic; here, we were told, pairing correlations in a superconductor would
split the electron into Majorana halves instead. Today is where that promissory note gets cashed in.
Superconductors and fractional quantum Hall states are, as we will restate carefully below, two
correlated states that both host fractional excitations via opposite mechanisms: one has a
mean-field description and needs an attractive interaction, the other has none and needs a strong
repulsive one. Same underlying idea, a correlated state hosting excitations that are not the
electron itself, reached by two genuinely different routes; today we build the machinery for the
superconducting route in full.

That machinery has one further piece of unfinished business behind it. Back in session 1 we built
mean-field theory as a general tool and then ran it through three symmetry-broken states in quick
succession: a charge density wave, a magnet, and, as our third and briefest worked example, a
superconductor. There, starting from an attractive Hubbard interaction, we let ourselves take an
expectation value between two annihilation operators instead of one creation and one annihilation
operator, defined the pairing order $\Delta = \langle c_i c_j\rangle$ (session 1, eq. 1.9), and
showed that this term picks up a phase $e^{2i\varphi}$ under a gauge transformation while every
ordinary bilinear term stays invariant: a superconductor spontaneously breaks gauge symmetry, and
with it, a fixed electron number. That was deliberately a first pass. We said at the time that
magnetism and superconductivity would each get "a full, much deeper treatment in their own
dedicated sessions later in the course," and today is superconductivity's turn (magnetism is
deferred once more, to the next recording, which in this course's arc is session 6).

So today we pick that thread back up and build the actual machinery around it. We will need a new
representation, the Nambu spinor, to even diagonalize a Hamiltonian that mixes creation and
annihilation operators the way a pairing term does; that representation hands us the
Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian, the central object of the whole session. We will use it to
classify superconducting order by symmetry (singlet versus triplet, s-, p- and d-wave), to show
that two superconductors can look spectrally identical and yet carry completely different
topology, reusing exactly the Chern-number machinery from the topological-band-structure and
quantum-Hall sessions to tell them apart. And we will follow that topological distinction all the
way down to its most dramatic consequence: the Kitaev chain, a model we will recognize as nothing
more than the SSH model from the topological-band-structure session, rewritten in the language of
Majorana operators, whose topological zero modes turn out to be their own antiparticles. We will
close by asking what these Majorana modes are good for (topological quantum computing), and by
handing off to session 6, which returns to do for magnetism exactly what we are about to do for
superconductivity, the course's one genuinely spoken cliffhanger: the same interacting Hamiltonian
can mean-field into either a superconductor or a magnet, and magnetism is what got deferred to the
next recording.

\section{Weakly versus strongly correlated matter, revisited}

Let us restate the general setting, because it is worth having sharply in view before we specialize.
Every interacting Hamiltonian we care about in this course has the same generic shape,

\begin{equation*}
H = \sum_{ij} t_{ij}\, c_i^\dagger c_j \;+\; \sum_{ijkl} V_{ijkl}\, c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger c_k c_l,
\end{equation*}

a free bilinear part plus a quartic interaction (this is exactly session 1's eq. 1.5). When mean-field
theory can turn the quartic term into a good effective bilinear Hamiltonian, we call the resulting
state \emph{weakly correlated matter}: this covers magnets, charge density waves, and superconductors, all
of the states session 1 built. When no such single-particle mean-field Hamiltonian captures the
physics, we are dealing with \emph{strongly correlated matter}, and we need exact or numerical solutions
instead; the fractional quantum Hall state from the quantum-Hall session is our standing example of
that harder case.

It is worth noticing that superconductors and the fractional quantum Hall state actually sit at
opposite corners of this classification, even though both eventually host "fractional" excitations.
A superconductor has a mean-field description, arises from an \emph{attractive} interaction, is destroyed
by a magnetic field, and (as we are about to see) hosts Majorana states. The fractional quantum Hall
state has no mean-field description, arises from strong \emph{repulsive} interactions, typically requires
a strong magnetic field, and hosts fractionally charged excitations. Majorana excitations and
fractional-quantum-Hall excitations are both, loosely speaking, "a fraction of an electron," but they
are fractions produced by entirely different mechanisms; it is one of the nicer illustrations in this
course of how very different microscopic physics can converge on a shared qualitative phenomenon,
emergent fractionalization, without the underlying mechanism being at all the same.

\section{Superconductivity as a macroscopic quantum phenomenon}

Before diving into the Hamiltonian, it is worth pausing on why superconductivity gets singled out as
one of the cleanest windows we have onto quantum mechanics at macroscopic scale. A superconductor is
built from trillions of atoms, and yet a measurement on the bulk sample can read off, essentially
exactly, the microscopic constants $\hbar$ and $e$. This works because gauge symmetry breaking gives
the many-body wave function a single, well-defined phase $\theta(\mathbf{r})$, and single-valuedness
of that phase around a closed loop is a very strong constraint.

Consider a superconducting ring (or, equivalently, a hole in a bulk superconductor) threaded by
magnetic flux, and take the loop deep enough inside the superconductor that the supercurrent density
vanishes there (Meissner screening). The supercurrent of Cooper pairs (charge $2e$, since a pair is
made of two electrons) is proportional to the gauge-invariant combination $\hbar\nabla\theta -
2e\mathbf{A}$; setting the current to zero on our loop forces

\begin{equation*}
\oint \nabla\theta \cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \frac{2e}{\hbar}\oint \mathbf{A}\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \frac{2e}{\hbar}\Phi.
\end{equation*}

But $\theta$ is the phase of a single-valued wave function, so going once around the loop it can only
change by an integer multiple of $2\pi$: $\oint \nabla\theta\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = 2\pi n$ for
integer $n$. Combining the two gives flux quantization,

\begin{equation}
\Phi = n\,\Phi_0, \qquad \Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e}.
\end{equation}

The geometry and the phase-winding argument are collected in figure~\ref{fig:s05:flux}.
This is exactly the mechanism behind the precision of Josephson-junction critical-current
measurements: it is a direct, macroscopic readout of $h/e$, made possible entirely by gauge symmetry
breaking. It is worth noticing the parallel with the quantum Hall state from earlier in the course:
there too, a macroscopic measurement (the Hall conductance, quantized in units of $e^2/h$ by the
Chern number) reads off a ratio of the same two constants, but through a completely different
mechanism, the quantization of a single-particle topological invariant rather than the breaking of a
continuous symmetry. Combining the two measurements, the flux quantum and the Hall conductance
quantum, is in fact one of the most precise ways we have of extracting $h$ and $e$ separately; it is
a nice, concrete example of the emergence this course keeps returning to; macroscopic measurements
on billions of atoms handing you exact microscopic constants.

\section{What a superconductor looks like: resistance and the Meissner effect}

Historically, superconductivity was discovered through its transport signature: as you cool an
ordinary metal, resistivity falls smoothly because there are fewer phonons for electrons to scatter
off. A superconductor instead shows a \emph{sudden} drop of the resistance to exactly zero at a critical
temperature $T_c$; below $T_c$ the resistance stays at zero, not just small. $T_c$ is precisely the
temperature at which gauge symmetry breaking sets in. This is slides-only material with no notebook
counterpart (the resistance-versus-temperature schematic and the field-expulsion figure are shown
and described on the slides but there is no accompanying notebook cell): our companion notebook
starts from the Hamiltonian side, not from transport measurements.

The second hallmark is the \textbf{Meissner effect}: above $T_c$ a metal tolerates an external magnetic
field just fine, but below $T_c$ the superconductor actively expels the field from its bulk. In
type-II superconductors, the only field that survives inside the material is confined to quantized
vortices, themselves dynamical topological defects of the broken-gauge-symmetry order parameter.
Both signatures, zero resistance and flux expulsion, are direct consequences of the same underlying
event: the condensation of Cooper pairs into a single macroscopic quantum state with a rigid,
spontaneously chosen phase.

\section{The interacting Hamiltonian and the origin of attractive interactions}

We start, as in session 1, from the generic interacting Hamiltonian $H = \sum_{ij}t_{ij}c_i^\dagger
c_j + \sum_{ijkl}V_{ijkl}c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger c_k c_l$. The interaction $V_{ijkl}$ can come from
plain electrostatic (repulsive) Coulomb repulsion, but it can equally well be an \emph{effective}
interaction mediated by some other quasiparticle in the material: phonons (lattice vibrations),
magnons (fluctuations of a magnetic moment), plasmons (collective charge fluctuations), or even more
exotic mediators like skyrmion fluctuations or valence fluctuations. What matters for
superconductivity is not the microscopic origin of $V_{ijkl}$ but its net sign in whichever channel
turns out to matter: superconductivity requires that channel to be effectively \emph{attractive}.

This is the natural place to draw the distinction between the two families of superconductors:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conventional superconductors}, where the attractive channel is mediated by phonons. This is the
  best-understood case: mean-field (BCS) theory works quantitatively well, and critical temperatures
  of simple conventional superconductors like aluminum or lead can actually be predicted from theory.
\item \textbf{Unconventional superconductors}, mediated by anything that is not a phonon: antiferromagnetic or
  ferromagnetic magnons, plasmons, valence fluctuations, charge fluctuations, and so on. Here we have
  no comparably reliable predictive theory; identifying even \emph{which} quasiparticle mediates the
  attraction in a given unconventional superconductor is often still an open research question, and
  it remains one of the major unsolved problems the field is currently working on.
\end{itemize}

\section{The mean-field pairing Hamiltonian, done properly}

Let us now build the pairing mean-field Hamiltonian with the same level of care session 1 used for
its charge-density-wave and magnetic examples, rather than just gesturing at $\Delta = \langle
cc\rangle$. Take the simplest interacting Hamiltonian that can produce superconductivity, a
one-dimensional tight-binding chain with an attractive on-site interaction between opposite spins,

\begin{equation}
H = -t\sum_{n,\sigma}\left(c_{n\sigma}^\dagger c_{n+1,\sigma} + \text{h.c.}\right) - \mu\sum_{n\sigma}c_{n\sigma}^\dagger c_{n\sigma} + U\sum_n c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow} c_{n\uparrow}, \qquad U<0.
\end{equation}

This is exactly session 1's eq. 1.7, now with $U = -|U| < 0$: the attractive branch of that same
Hamiltonian, whose repulsive branch instead drives magnetism (session 6's subject). We mean-field
decouple the quartic term in the \emph{pairing} (particle-particle) channel this time rather than the
density channel: define the order parameter

\begin{equation}
\Delta_n \equiv U\langle c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow}\rangle,
\end{equation}

and factorize $c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow} \approx
\langle c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger\rangle c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow} +
c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger \langle c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow}\rangle - \langle
c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger\rangle\langle c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow}\rangle$, the
same Wick-type factorization used throughout session 1, just applied to a different pair of
operators. The resulting mean-field Hamiltonian is

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{MF}} = -t\sum_{n\sigma}\left(c_{n\sigma}^\dagger c_{n+1,\sigma}+\text{h.c.}\right) - \mu\sum_{n\sigma}c_{n\sigma}^\dagger c_{n\sigma} + \sum_n\left(\Delta_n\, c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger + \Delta_n^*\, c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow}\right) - \sum_n\frac{|\Delta_n|^2}{U}.
\end{equation}

Self-consistently, $\Delta_n$ comes out exactly zero for $U>0$ (repulsive) and can become nonzero for
$U<0$ (attractive): this is precisely the superconducting order parameter, and it is exactly the term
that breaks gauge symmetry.

\section{Why pairing kills a fixed particle number}

Now let us make the "fluctuating electron number" claim from session 1 fully rigorous, rather than
just asserting it. Look at the eigenvalue equation $H_{\text{MF}}|G\rangle = E|G\rangle$ for the
Hamiltonian in (5.4), and expand the ground state in the occupation-number (Fock) basis, sorted by
total particle number $N$: $|G\rangle = \sum_N a_N |N\rangle$. The hopping and chemical-potential
terms in (5.4) conserve $N$ exactly (one $c^\dagger$ and one $c$ each). The pairing terms $\Delta_n
c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger$ and $\Delta_n^* c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow}$ change
$N$ by $+2$ or $-2$ respectively; crucially, \emph{no term in $H_{\text{MF}}$ ever changes $N$ by $\pm 1$}.
So $H_{\text{MF}}$ only ever connects Fock states whose particle numbers differ by an even integer.
For $|G\rangle$ to be an eigenstate, this forces $a_N$ to be nonzero only within one fixed parity
class of $N$ (all-even or all-odd), but says nothing about which particular even (or odd) $N$'s
appear: generically $a_N \ne 0$ for several different values of $N$ within that class, e.g.
$N=0,2,4,\dots$ simultaneously. The mean-field ground state is therefore \emph{not} an eigenstate of the
total number operator $\hat N$, even though it does have a well-defined fermion parity. This is the
precise sense in which "a superconductor has a fluctuating electron number, but a fixed parity,"
exactly the statement session 1 flagged back in its discussion of the quantum-dot superposition
puzzle (session 1, section 1.5).

\section{The gauge transformation, made explicit}

We already wrote the second-quantized gauge transformation in session 1 (eq. 1.10): $c \to c\,
e^{i\varphi}$, $c^\dagger \to c^\dagger e^{-i\varphi}$, the second-quantized shadow of the statement
that the phase of a first-quantized wave function carries no physical meaning. Let us now check
explicitly which terms in a realistic Hamiltonian survive this transformation and which do not.
Ordinary hopping $c_i^\dagger c_j$ is invariant, since the two phases cancel: $c_i^\dagger c_j \to
e^{-i\varphi}c_i^\dagger \cdot e^{i\varphi} c_j = c_i^\dagger c_j$. The same cancellation protects
essentially every other bilinear term we have met so far: magnetic (spin-dependent) terms, spin-orbit
coupling, and any density-density interaction term $c_i^\dagger c_i c_j^\dagger c_j$, since all of
these are built from an equal number of creation and annihilation operators pairing up index by
index.

The pairing term is the one exception. Under the same transformation,

\begin{equation}
\Delta\, c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger \;\longrightarrow\; \Delta\, e^{-2i\varphi}\, c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger,
\end{equation}

it picks up the phase \emph{twice}, because both operators are creation operators rather than one
creation and one annihilation operator. There is no way to choose $\varphi \ne 0 \pmod \pi$ that
leaves this term invariant, so a Hamiltonian containing a nonzero $\Delta$ is not gauge invariant
term by term; equivalently, if we insist on writing the transformation as acting on the order
parameter itself, $\Delta \to \Delta\, e^{2i\varphi}$, then any particular fixed phase of $\Delta$ is
a choice the ground state makes spontaneously, exactly the hallmark of continuous symmetry breaking.
This is the same logic, applied with full explicit rigor, that session 1 used to establish the
gauge-symmetry-breaking of eq. 1.9 there; we have now seen precisely why the factor is $2\varphi$
and not $\varphi$.

\section{The Nambu spinor and the Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian}

Here is the technical obstacle the pairing term creates: we cannot diagonalize (5.4) with an
ordinary vector of orbital operators, because the anomalous term mixes creation and annihilation
operators rather than pairing them up the way every Hamiltonian we diagonalized in sessions 1-4 did.
The fix is to \emph{enlarge} the operator basis so that creation and annihilation operators of the
original problem both become "annihilation-like" entries of a bigger spinor. Working in momentum
space, with a single orbital and both spins, define the \textbf{Nambu spinor}

\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} c_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow} \\ c_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow} \\ c_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}^\dagger \\ -c_{-\mathbf{k}\uparrow}^\dagger \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}

an "electron sector" (the first two components, ordinary annihilation operators) glued to a "hole
sector" (the last two components, creation operators at $-\mathbf{k}$, spins swapped, with an extra
minus sign planted on the very last entry). That sign is not decorative: it is exactly what is
needed to make the pairing term come out looking like two identical, decoupled copies of the same
$2\times 2$ problem once we write $H_{\text{MF}}$ in terms of $\Psi_{\mathbf k}$, rather than mixing
the two spin blocks together through a Pauli matrix. For a spin-singlet pairing $\Delta(\mathbf k)$
(momentum-independent and equal to $\Delta$ for the on-site case of (5.4) Fourier-transformed), one
checks directly, using the fermionic anticommutation relations to reorder operators where needed,
that the mean-field Hamiltonian can be written exactly as

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{MF}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}} \Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^\dagger\, H_{\text{BdG}}(\mathbf{k})\, \Psi_{\mathbf{k}} + \text{const.}, \qquad H_{\text{BdG}}(\mathbf{k}) = \begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon(\mathbf{k}) & 0 & \Delta(\mathbf k) & 0 \\ 0 & \varepsilon(\mathbf k) & 0 & \Delta(\mathbf k) \\ \Delta(\mathbf k)^* & 0 & -\varepsilon(\mathbf{k}) & 0 \\ 0 & \Delta(\mathbf k)^* & 0 & -\varepsilon(\mathbf k) \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}

where $\varepsilon(\mathbf k) = -2t(\cos k_x + \cos k_y) - \mu$ is the ordinary band dispersion
(assumed inversion symmetric, $\varepsilon(-\mathbf k) = \varepsilon(\mathbf k)$). This $4\times 4$
matrix is the \textbf{Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian}: it is, in a very concrete sense, the single
most important object for describing excitations of a superconductor. Note that the two diagonal
entries in the lower-right carry $-\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$, not $+\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$: this is a
direct consequence of rewriting $c_{-\mathbf k\sigma}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf k\sigma}$ in terms of
$c_{-\mathbf k\sigma}c_{-\mathbf k\sigma}^\dagger = 1 - c_{-\mathbf k\sigma}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf
k\sigma}$ via the anticommutation relations (this is exactly where the "hole" language comes from: a
missing electron at $-\mathbf k$ behaves like a particle of opposite energy).

Because the spinor (5.6) was built with exactly that extra minus sign, the $4\times 4$ problem in
(5.7) already comes out block-diagonal: one $2\times 2$ block built from $(c_{\mathbf k\uparrow},
c_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}^\dagger)$ and an identical one from $(c_{\mathbf k\downarrow}, -c_{-\mathbf
k\uparrow}^\dagger)$, each with reduced BdG matrix

\begin{equation}
h_{\text{BdG}}(\mathbf k) = \begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon(\mathbf k) & \Delta(\mathbf k) \\ \Delta(\mathbf k)^* & -\varepsilon(\mathbf k)\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

This is exactly the "$4\times 4$ matrix, single orbital with two spins, diagonal entries
$\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$, $\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$, $-\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$,
$-\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$,
off-diagonal entries $\Delta$" structure read directly off the slides and quoted in the lecture, now
derived rather than merely asserted. Diagonalizing (5.8) gives the celebrated BCS quasiparticle
dispersion

\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf k) = \pm\sqrt{\varepsilon(\mathbf k)^2 + |\Delta(\mathbf k)|^2}.
\end{equation}

\section{A worked example: gap opening independent of dispersion}

Equation (5.9) already tells us something the transcript's slides illustrate with explicit band
plots: the gap opens at the minimum of $E(\mathbf k)$, which happens wherever $\varepsilon(\mathbf
k) = 0$, i.e. exactly on the original Fermi surface, and the minimum gap there is $|\Delta(\mathbf
k)|$ evaluated on that Fermi surface. Nothing in this argument depends on the details of
$\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$: not the range of hopping, not the lattice geometry, not the position of the
chemical potential. Concretely, take the single-orbital square lattice with $\varepsilon(\mathbf k) =
\cos k_x + \cos k_y$ (units where $t=1$) and a chemical potential tuned so that the "physical" red
band and the "spurious" blue mirror band cross; turning on a constant $\Delta$ opens a gap of size
$2|\Delta|$ at every such crossing, for \emph{any} choice of chemical potential along that dispersion.
Exactly the same statement holds if we redo the calculation on the triangular lattice instead of the
square lattice: as we move the chemical potential across the triangular-lattice band, a uniform
$\Delta$ still gaps the spectrum everywhere the physical and spurious bands cross. This is the
generic signature of \emph{conventional} (fully gapped) superconductivity: a uniform, momentum-independent
$\Delta(\mathbf k)$ always fully gaps the Fermi surface, regardless of microscopic detail.

\section{Three scenarios for one Fermi surface: metal, gapped SC, nodal SC}

Not every pairing function $\Delta(\mathbf k)$ is momentum-independent, and this is where things get
more interesting. Given one electronic dispersion $\varepsilon(\mathbf k)$, there are, in general,
three qualitatively different outcomes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No superconductivity} ($\Delta = 0$): a metal, gapless everywhere the Fermi surface sits.
\item \textbf{Conventional (s-wave) superconductivity}: $\Delta(\mathbf k) \ne 0$ everywhere on the Fermi
surface, so $E(\mathbf k)$ in (5.9) is bounded away from zero everywhere; a full gap.
\item \textbf{Unconventional, nodal superconductivity}: $\Delta(\mathbf k)$ vanishes along particular lines or
points of the Brillouin zone. Wherever the Fermi surface crosses one of those nodal lines, $E(\mathbf
k) = 0$ even though $\Delta \ne 0$ nearby; the system is gapped in most of reciprocal space but
remains gapless (metallic) at isolated momenta or along nodal lines.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:s05:fsgap} puts the three side by side, in the Brillouin zone and again as the gap
read off around the Fermi surface itself, which is the only place it can be read off at all.
The high-temperature cuprate superconductors are the standard real-world example of scenario 3: they
are genuine superconductors, but with a gap function that has nodes, so parts of their Fermi surface
remain gapless even in the superconducting state. This three-way split holds for any lattice geometry;
the lecture works through it explicitly for both the square and triangular lattice Fermi surfaces.

\section{The general pairing Hamiltonian and its symmetry classification}

So far $\Delta$ has been a single number. In general the pairing order can depend on momentum and
carry spin structure: $\Delta_{\mathbf k,\sigma_1\sigma_2}$, with $\sigma_1,\sigma_2 \in
\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}$, conveniently packaged into the $2\times 2$ pairing matrix $\Delta_{\mathbf
k} = \begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{\mathbf k,\uparrow\uparrow} & \Delta_{\mathbf k,\uparrow\downarrow}\\
\Delta_{\mathbf k,\downarrow\uparrow} & \Delta_{\mathbf k,\downarrow\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}$, and
built from a generic mean-field Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{pair}} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf k}{}^{\prime}\, \sum_{\sigma_1\sigma_2} \left[\Delta_{\mathbf k,\sigma_1\sigma_2}\, c_{\mathbf k\sigma_1}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf k\sigma_2}^\dagger + \Delta_{\mathbf k,\sigma_1\sigma_2}^*\, c_{\mathbf k\sigma_1} c_{-\mathbf k\sigma_2}\right],
\end{equation}

Real-space and reciprocal-space pictures of $\Delta$ are Fourier-transform equivalent, so we can
equally well think of this order in terms of how the pair wave function looks as a function of the
separation between the two paired electrons in real space. The key move is to classify
superconducting order purely by the \emph{symmetry} of $\Delta_{\sigma\sigma'}(\mathbf k)$, because that
symmetry alone controls how the superconductor is going to respond to perturbations (disorder,
magnetic fields), independent of microscopic detail.

Fermionic antisymmetry (the operators $c_{\mathbf k\sigma}^\dagger c_{-\mathbf k\sigma'}^\dagger$
anticommute) forces

\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\sigma\sigma'}(\mathbf k) = -\Delta_{\sigma'\sigma}(-\mathbf k),
\end{equation}

a minus sign under the \emph{combined} operation of exchanging spin indices and sending $\mathbf k \to
-\mathbf k$; this combined statement always holds regardless of the type of pairing. The physically
meaningful classification comes from splitting $\Delta_{\sigma\sigma'}(\mathbf k)$ into its part
symmetric and its part antisymmetric under spin exchange alone, which (via (5.11)) is equivalent to
splitting it by its parity under $\mathbf k \to -\mathbf k$ alone:

\begin{equation*}
\textbf{Spin singlet (even parity):} \quad \Delta(\mathbf k) = \Delta(-\mathbf k), \qquad \Delta_{\sigma\sigma'} = -\Delta_{\sigma'\sigma}\ \text{(spin-antisymmetric)},
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation}
\textbf{Spin triplet (odd parity):} \quad \Delta(\mathbf k) = -\Delta(-\mathbf k), \qquad \Delta_{\sigma\sigma'} = +\Delta_{\sigma'\sigma}\ \text{(spin-symmetric)}.
\end{equation}

Concretely, on-site s-wave pairing ($\Delta(\mathbf k) = \Delta$, constant, arising from a local
attractive interaction as in section 5.5) is even in $\mathbf k$, hence spin-singlet. This
symmetry classification has an immediate physical payoff: a spin-singlet superconductor is
essentially insensitive to non-magnetic disorder (Anderson's theorem, the pair wave function only
cares about time-reversed partners, which non-magnetic disorder preserves), while a spin-triplet
superconductor is strongly suppressed by even weak non-magnetic disorder, because scattering mixes
the different momentum directions that the order parameter needs to keep coherent phase relations
between. This is precisely why spin-singlet, s-wave superconductors vastly outnumber spin-triplet
ones in nature: essentially any real material has some level of non-magnetic disorder, and that
disorder selectively kills the triplet channel while leaving the singlet channel intact.

\section{Building a spin-triplet superconductor out of a magnet}

Given how fragile spin-triplet order is, how would we ever engineer one deliberately? One route is a
toy model that combines a ferromagnetic exchange field with an attractive interaction in the same
Hamiltonian,

\begin{equation*}
H = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}c_{i\sigma}^\dagger c_{j\sigma} \;\underbrace{-\, h\sum_i \left(c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger c_{i\uparrow} - c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger c_{i\downarrow}\right)}_{\text{exchange field}} \;+\; \underbrace{U\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} c_{i\sigma}^\dagger c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger c_{j\sigma'}c_{i\sigma}}_{\text{attractive interaction}}, \qquad U<0.
\end{equation*}

Solved self-consistently at mean-field level, both order parameters (the ferromagnetic exchange
field and the pairing amplitude) can be nonzero simultaneously: the ground state breaks both
time-reversal symmetry (it is magnetic) \emph{and} gauge symmetry (it is superconducting) at once, and the
pairing that survives is, necessarily, spin-triplet, since the exchange field explicitly splits the
$\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$ Fermi surfaces and thereby suppresses the singlet channel that needs them
degenerate. Physically, s-wave singlet pairing is normally the leading instability precisely because
it can gap the \emph{entire} Fermi surface (maximal condensation energy); once the exchange field quenches
that channel, a different pairing channel, here the odd-parity, spin-triplet, effectively p-wave
channel, becomes the leading instability instead.

The truly striking feature of this state shows up when we put it on a finite ribbon rather than an
infinite plane: the bulk spectrum is gapped, exactly as we would expect from a superconductor, but
the edges of the ribbon carry gapless excitations. A superconductor that is fully gapped in the bulk
and gapless at the edge; this is, by now, a familiar signature from the topological-band-structure
and quantum-Hall sessions, and indeed these edge modes are identified as topological in origin,
tied to a finite Chern number of the underlying BdG band structure exactly the way a Chern
insulator's edge states were tied to its Chern number.

\section{Nodal singlet order: d-wave, cuprates, and the angular-momentum expansion}

Within spin-singlet, even-parity order there is a further useful distinction: a singlet gap can be
uniformly nonzero (the s-wave case of section 5.9) or it can be nodal. The classic example is
\textbf{d-wave} order on the square lattice,

\begin{equation}
\Delta_{d}(\mathbf k) = \Delta_0\left(\cos k_x - \cos k_y\right),
\end{equation}

which vanishes exactly along the Brillouin-zone diagonals $k_x = \pm k_y$. Combined with (5.9), the
quasiparticle spectrum $E(\mathbf k) = \sqrt{\varepsilon(\mathbf k)^2 + \Delta_d(\mathbf k)^2}$ is
gapless precisely where the Fermi surface crosses these diagonals: a nodal singlet superconductor.
This is exactly the pairing symmetry realized in the cuprate high-temperature superconductors, many
of which have critical temperatures in the hundreds of kelvin and are spin-singlet, but decidedly
\emph{not} s-wave.

There is a clean, general organizing scheme behind this, directly analogous to the spherical-harmonic
expansion of an atomic orbital wave function: expand the superconducting gap in an angular-momentum
basis on the Fermi surface,

\begin{equation}
\Delta(\mathbf k) \;\sim\; \sum_L \Delta_L\, Y_L(\mathbf k),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
L=0:\quad &\text{s-wave (conventional, typically phonon-driven)},\\
L=1:\quad &\text{p-wave (spin-triplet, often ferromagnetic-magnon-driven)},\\
L=2:\quad &\text{d-wave (spin-singlet, often antiferromagnetic-magnon-driven; cuprates)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

In real/reciprocal space, s-wave order looks flat and constant, with no momentum dependence at all;
$d_{x^2-y^2}$ order, as in (5.13), alternates sign around the Fermi surface in a four-lobed
$+,-,+,-$ pattern (the same nomenclature and the same lobe structure as an atomic $d$ orbital); and
there is a third possibility, \emph{extended s-wave}, which is still $L=0$ (same overall symmetry as plain
s-wave under the lattice point group) but is nodal along the \emph{radial} direction: positive near the
zone center $\Gamma$, passing through a node, and negative further out. Figure~\ref{fig:s05:gapsymmetry}
draws these sign structures over the Brillouin zone alongside the p- and f-wave cases below.

The lecture repeats this same three-way classification (uniform s-wave, a nodal order, and extended
s-wave) on the triangular lattice, where the nodal case shows six alternating-sign lobes around the
Fermi surface: an \textbf{f-wave order, $L=3$} (confirmed by the instructor; the "L equal to two"
heard in the raw recording was a slip, since a six-lobe alternating-sign pattern is the standard
signature of $L=3$, not $L=2$, which is already used above for the square lattice's four-lobed
$d_{x^2-y^2}$ state). Note that this one steps outside the present section's singlet family: six
lobes alternating in sign means the gap changes sign under a half turn, so $\Delta(-\mathbf k) =
-\Delta(\mathbf k)$, and by (5.12) an $L=3$ order is odd-parity and hence necessarily
\emph{spin-triplet}, not singlet like the s-, extended-s- and d-wave cases above.

\section{Same gap, different topology: trivial s-wave versus chiral p-wave}

Here is the pivot point of the whole session. Two superconducting gaps can look completely identical
in their \emph{spectral} properties, both fully gapping the Fermi surface with, say, the same minimum gap
size, and yet be topologically inequivalent. Compare plain s-wave pairing, $\Delta(\mathbf k) =
\Delta_0$, with \textbf{chiral p-wave} pairing,

\begin{equation}
\Delta_{p+ip}(\mathbf k) = \Delta_0\left(\sin k_x + i \sin k_y\right),
\end{equation}

an odd-parity, spin-triplet order (consistent with (5.12): $\Delta_{p+ip}(-\mathbf k) =
-\Delta_{p+ip}(\mathbf k)$) that winds once around the origin of the complex plane as $\mathbf k$
circles the Fermi surface. Both (5.9)-with-$\Delta_0$ and (5.9)-with-$\Delta_{p+ip}$ can fully gap the
same Fermi surface, so a measurement of the bulk gap alone cannot distinguish them. But exactly as in
the topological-band-structure session's Chern-number classification of gapped bands, we can compute
the Chern number of the occupied BdG bands for each case, and they come out completely different: the
trivial s-wave state has Chern number $0$, while the chiral p-wave state has Chern number $\pm 2$
(the sign set by the chirality, $p_x + ip_y$ versus $p_x - ip_y$).

What distinguishes them, drawn in figure~\ref{fig:s05:topology}, is the winding of the \emph{phase}
of $\Delta(\mathbf k)$ as $\mathbf k$ is carried once around the Fermi surface: constant for s-wave,
one full turn for $p+ip$.

This has exactly the consequence the bulk-boundary correspondence would lead us to expect. On a
finite system, the s-wave superconductor stays gapped everywhere, bulk and edge alike, just like a
trivial (Chern-number-zero) insulator. The chiral p-wave superconductor, by contrast, is gapped in
the bulk but carries gapless \emph{chiral} edge modes: on any one edge, $|C|$ branches all
propagating the same way (here two, one from each of the two identical spin blocks), with the
opposite edge carrying the same number of branches running the opposite way, precisely as a
Chern-number-$\pm 2$ state must. On a ribbon, then, one sees two counter-propagating sets of
branches, but they are localized on opposite edges rather than sharing one. These particular edge
modes turn out to be
"fractions of an electron" in a very specific and rigorous sense: they are Majorana modes, and
understanding exactly what that means is the subject of the rest of this session.

\section{Majorana bound states: definition and the electron-splitting trick}

A \textbf{Majorana fermion} is defined algebraically by the condition that its field operator is its own
Hermitian conjugate,

\begin{equation}
\gamma^\dagger = \gamma,
\end{equation}

meaning creation and annihilation of the quasiparticle are the same operation: the particle is its
own antiparticle. This sounds like it has no place in an ordinary material, since an ordinary electron
is emphatically not its own antiparticle ($c^\dagger \ne c$), and a semiconductor, say, never hosts
anything of the kind.

But there is a purely algebraic fact that opens the door: \emph{any} ordinary fermionic operator can be
written as a combination of two Hermitian, mutually anticommuting Majorana operators,

\begin{equation}
\gamma_A = c + c^\dagger, \qquad \gamma_B = -i\left(c - c^\dagger\right),
\end{equation}

so that $\gamma_A^\dagger = \gamma_A$, $\gamma_B^\dagger = \gamma_B$, $\{\gamma_A,\gamma_B\}=0$,
$\gamma_A^2=\gamma_B^2=1$ (using $\{c,c^\dagger\}=1$), and, inverting (5.17),

\begin{equation}
c = \frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma_A + i\gamma_B\right), \qquad c^\dagger = \frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma_A - i\gamma_B\right).
\end{equation}

This is initially just a change of variables, a mathematical trick with no new physics in it: every
electron is \emph{always} secretly two Majorana operators, tightly bound together into an ordinary
fermion, and normally there is no way to observe $\gamma_A$ or $\gamma_B$ individually, only their
fixed combination $c$. What makes superconductors special is that BdG eigenstates are already
electron-hole mixtures by construction (recall the Nambu spinor of section 5.8 explicitly combines
$c_{\mathbf k\sigma}$ with $c_{-\mathbf k\sigma'}^\dagger$), so a superconductor is exactly the kind
of setting where, with the right pairing symmetry and the right geometry, the two Majorana
components of an electron can become spatially \emph{separated} (deconfined) rather than staying locked
together at one site. That deconfinement is what the rest of this section is about.

\section{The Kitaev chain: the minimal model with isolated Majoranas}

The simplest superconductor capable of hosting isolated Majoranas is, appropriately, about as simple
as a superconducting Hamiltonian can be: a one-dimensional, \emph{spinless} chain with nearest-neighbor
hopping and nearest-neighbor pairing,

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{Kitaev}} = -t\sum_n \left(c_n^\dagger c_{n+1} + \text{h.c.}\right) - \mu\sum_n c_n^\dagger c_n + \Delta\sum_n \left(c_n c_{n+1} + c_{n+1}^\dagger c_n^\dagger\right).
\end{equation}

Pairing has to sit on a bond here rather than on-site: with spinless fermions, an on-site term
$c_n c_n$ vanishes identically by the Pauli principle ($c_n^2=0$), so the simplest allowed pairing is
exactly the nearest-neighbor term above. Fourier transforming, the dispersion and the pairing become

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(k) = -2t\cos k - \mu, \qquad \Delta(k) = 2i\Delta\sin k,
\end{equation}

so the anomalous term is manifestly odd in $k$: this is, as (5.12) already tells us, necessarily a
spin-triplet-type (here, spinless p-wave) superconductor, consistent with the Pauli-forbidden on-site
channel.

Now set $\mu=0$ and tune to the special point $\Delta=t$, and substitute the Majorana decomposition
(5.17)-(5.18) at every site, $c_n = \frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{2n-1}+i\gamma_{2n})$. Carrying the
substitution through both the hopping and the pairing term (the algebra is a direct but slightly
lengthy exercise in the anticommutation relations of the $\gamma$'s, which we encourage you to check
yourself), the two terms combine so that every $\gamma_{2n-1}\gamma_{2n+2}$ contribution cancels
exactly between hopping and pairing, leaving a remarkably clean result:

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{Kitaev}}\Big|_{\Delta=t,\ \mu=0} = it\sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \gamma_{2n}\,\gamma_{2n+1}.
\end{equation}

Every Majorana operator $\gamma_2, \gamma_3, \gamma_4, \dots, \gamma_{2N-1}$ appears in this sum,
paired up between neighboring \emph{sites}, but the very first Majorana, $\gamma_1$, and the very last,
$\gamma_{2N}$, never appear at all. They drop out of the Hamiltonian entirely: they cost zero energy,
and they are completely uncoupled from everything else in the system. This is precisely the same
structure we met in the topological-band-structure session's SSH model, in its dimerized
strong-coupling limit: there, at full dimerization, the bulk pairs up into bonding/antibonding
dimers while a leftover site at each end decouples and produces a zero-energy edge mode. The Kitaev
chain in (5.21) \emph{is} that same structure, just written in Majorana language rather than ordinary
fermion language: two decoupled end Majoranas standing in for the SSH model's two decoupled end
sites. We are not re-deriving the SSH model here; we are simply recognizing it, dressed up in a new
set of operators. Which Majorana is bound to which, in this phase and in the trivial one, is drawn
in figure~\ref{fig:s05:kitaev}.

\section{Periodic versus open boundaries: the topological zero modes}

Diagonalizing (5.19) on a \emph{periodic} chain gives a fully gapped spectrum, with a superconducting gap
set by $\Delta$ and a nontrivial bulk topological invariant (the same kind of $\mathbb{Z}_2$/winding
invariant that classified the SSH model's two phases via the Zak phase in the topological-band-
structure session, now computed from the BdG bands instead of an ordinary Bloch Hamiltonian). On an
\emph{open} chain, say $N=100$ sites with the two ends disconnected, the bulk spectrum keeps essentially
the same gap, but two extra states appear pinned exactly at zero energy, localized respectively at
the left end and the right end of the chain: these are precisely the unpaired Majoranas $\gamma_1$
and $\gamma_{2N}$ from (5.21), now visible as genuine mid-gap states of the open chain.

These zero modes are remarkably robust. Moving away from the fine-tuned point ($\Delta \ne t$,
finite $\mu$), the zero modes survive as long as we stay within the same topological phase. The
precise, standard criterion for the general Kitaev chain (5.19) with $\Delta \ne 0$ is

\begin{equation}
|\mu| < 2t \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{topological phase (unpaired end Majoranas)}, \qquad |\mu| > 2t \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{trivial phase.}
\end{equation}

In the trivial phase, large chemical potential effectively pins each site to be either empty or
doubly occupied in the fermionic language, and the two Majoranas that make up each site's electron
recombine locally into an ordinary, confined fermion; there is nothing left to deconfine. In the
topological phase, by contrast, p-wave pairing dominates over the trivial on-site physics, and the
two Majoranas at each site pair up with their neighbors' Majoranas instead of with each other,
leaving the two end Majoranas with no partner at all. This is exactly the same "strong coupling /
dimerized limit versus weak coupling / atomic limit" contrast that distinguished the SSH model's
topological and trivial phases; the Kitaev chain reproduces it because, as we saw in section 5.16,
it literally is the SSH model, just relabeled.

\section{Engineering a topological superconductor from a quantum spin Hall insulator}

Chiral and p-wave superconductors of the kind we need are rare in nature, so in practice they are
usually \emph{engineered} rather than found. One instructive recipe reuses the quantum spin Hall (QSH)
insulator from the topological-band-structure session directly. Take a QSH insulator, tungsten
ditelluride (WTe$_2$) is the concrete material example used, whose edge carries helical modes:
spin-up electrons propagate one way, spin-down electrons propagate the other way, on the same edge.
Now place ordinary ferromagnets on two segments of that edge. A magnet locally breaks time-reversal
symmetry, which gaps out the helical edge modes underneath it (recall from the topological-band-
structure session that the QSH edge's protection relies on time-reversal symmetry via Kramers
degeneracy; break that symmetry locally and the protection is gone there). What remains ungapped is
a short, isolated segment of helical edge sandwiched between the two magnetic regions.

That isolated segment only has spin-up electrons moving right and spin-down electrons moving left
(no spin-down moving right, and no spin-up moving left): counting states purely by propagation
direction, this segment is \emph{effectively spinless}, exactly the ingredient the Kitaev-chain
construction of section 5.16 needs. Proximity-coupling this segment to an ordinary s-wave
superconductor then reproduces, on this effectively spinless one-dimensional system, exactly the
physics of (5.19): a one-dimensional p-wave superconductor with Majorana zero modes pinned at the
two ends of the isolated segment. The whole stack is drawn in figure~\ref{fig:s05:qsh}.

\section{Topological quantum computing with Majoranas}

The reason Majorana modes attract so much attention beyond their own intrinsic strangeness is their
proposed use in fault-tolerant quantum computing. Majoranas are \textbf{non-Abelian anyons}: exchanging
(braiding) one Majorana around another multiplies the many-body ground state by a phase, and unlike
an ordinary phase, the \emph{order} in which different braiding operations are carried out matters, an
inherently non-commuting (non-Abelian) algebra. This follows directly from the structure of the BdG
Hamiltonian once more than two Majoranas are present.

Concretely, four Majorana operators $\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3,\gamma_4$ can be recombined, via
(5.18)-type pairings, into two ordinary ("artificial") fermionic operators, so a system with four
Majoranas encodes two ordinary fermionic modes. The resulting many-body ground-state manifold, empty,
one mode filled, the other mode filled, both filled, is four-fold degenerate (all four states cost
zero energy, by the same argument that made $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_{2N}$ zero-energy in the Kitaev
chain), and this degenerate manifold is exactly the proposed computational basis for a topological
qubit.

Braiding two Majoranas, say $\gamma_1$ around $\gamma_2$, acts on the ground-state manifold as a unitary

\begin{equation}
U_{12} = \exp\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\,\gamma_1\gamma_2\right),
\end{equation}

which multiplies states by a relative phase built from $e^{i\pi/4}$ times the Majorana product; the
precise form is not the point so much as what it implies: braiding the pair $(1,2)$ and then the pair
$(3,4)$ produces a different final state than braiding $(3,4)$ first and $(1,2)$ second, and braiding
$(1,4)$ is different again. This non-commuting braid algebra is exactly what lets sequences of purely
topological, purely geometric moves (physically moving Majoranas around one another) implement
non-trivial quantum logic gates. Because the resulting qubit is stored non-locally, split between
spatially separated Majoranas rather than living in one local degree of freedom, it is intrinsically
resistant to local decoherence and to weak, local disorder; this robustness is the whole appeal of
the topological-quantum-computing proposal. This braiding-and-quantum-computing material is
slides-and-transcript-only content with no counterpart in \texttt{session5.ipynb}: none of the notebook's
sections implement braiding dynamics or a qubit manifold, which makes sense given how far this is
from a static tight-binding diagonalization; it is a genuinely separate, dynamical calculation.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Superconductivity is a mean-field, attractive-interaction phenomenon.} Starting from an
attractive Hubbard chain (5.2), pairing mean-field theory produces the order parameter
$\Delta_n = U\langle c_{n\downarrow}c_{n\uparrow}\rangle$ (5.3) and mean-field Hamiltonian (5.4).
\item \textbf{Pairing breaks particle-number conservation down to parity} (section 5.6), and this is exactly
the statement that gauge symmetry is spontaneously broken: the pairing term picks up a phase
$e^{-2i\varphi}$ under the gauge transformation (5.5), unlike ordinary bilinear terms.
\item \textbf{Flux quantization}, $\Phi = n\, h/2e$ (5.1), is a direct, measurable macroscopic consequence of
that broken gauge symmetry, on par with the quantum Hall conductance quantum from earlier sessions.
\item \textbf{The Nambu spinor (5.6) and the Bogoliubov-de Gennes Hamiltonian (5.7)-(5.8)} are the tools that
let us diagonalize any mean-field superconducting Hamiltonian, giving the quasiparticle dispersion
$E(\mathbf k) = \pm\sqrt{\varepsilon(\mathbf k)^2 + |\Delta(\mathbf k)|^2}$ (5.9); a gap opens at
the original Fermi surface regardless of microscopic dispersion details.
\item \textbf{The symmetry of $\Delta(\mathbf k)$ classifies the superconductor}: singlet (even in $\mathbf
k$) versus triplet (odd in $\mathbf k$) via (5.11)-(5.12), with an angular-momentum ($L=0,1,2,\dots$)
sub-classification (5.14) that recovers s-, p-, and d-wave order, the last of these realized by
cuprate high-$T_c$ superconductors via the nodal $d_{x^2-y^2}$ gap (5.13).
\item \textbf{Spectrally identical gaps can carry different topology.} Trivial s-wave ($C=0$) and chiral
p-wave ($C=\pm2$, eq. 5.15) pairing both fully gap the same Fermi surface, but only the
topological one hosts gapless edge modes, exactly the Chern-number bulk-boundary logic from the
topological-band-structure and quantum-Hall sessions.
\item \textbf{The Kitaev chain (5.19) is the SSH model in Majorana language.} At the fine-tuned point it
reduces to $H = it\sum_n \gamma_{2n}\gamma_{2n+1}$ (5.21), leaving two unpaired, zero-energy end
Majoranas; these survive away from fine-tuning throughout the topological phase $|\mu|<2t$ (5.22).
\item \textbf{Majoranas are non-Abelian anyons.} Braiding them implements a non-commuting operator algebra
(5.23) on a topologically degenerate ground-state manifold, the basis for proposed topological
quantum computing schemes; this last piece is slides-and-transcript material with no notebook
counterpart.
\end{itemize}
