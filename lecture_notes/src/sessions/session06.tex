\section{Two classes of magnets and their excitations}

Let us start with a map of where we are going, since this lecture first previews material from two
later sessions and only then moves to its own topic. There are two big classes of magnetic systems.

The first class contains the magnets that break time-reversal symmetry. These materials have a net
magnetization on every site, or a staggered one, and a finite spin angular momentum is what makes a
system look different when we play its ``movie'' backwards, which is the definition of broken
time-reversal symmetry. Ferromagnets, antiferromagnets and noncollinear spin spirals are in this
class. Iron and cobalt are the two materials that the lecture names, several times, as concrete
examples of this family. In this session we spend all our time on this class.

The second class contains the magnets that do \emph{not} break time-reversal symmetry, at least not
classically. These are quantum magnets, and quantum spin liquids are among them. They have a local
moment on every site, but the spins are in a quantum superposition and do not form a static classical
arrangement, so time-reversal symmetry does not need to be broken overall. A related case, which is
also mentioned only briefly in this lecture, is the heavy-fermion Kondo system. It is a combination of
local moments in a quantum superposition with a sea of itinerant metallic electrons. The ``Classical
and quantum magnetism'' slide shows the three families with photographs of materials, which are iron
for the ferromagnet and antiferromagnet family, herbertsmithite for quantum spin liquids and samarium
hexaboride SmB$_6$ for heavy-fermion insulators. None of these three compound names is said in this
year's narration. They are only labels on the slide, which are good to know but which the lecture does
not say out loud.

Each class has its own emergent excitation. Magnets that break time-reversal symmetry have
\textbf{magnons}, which are spin-1 and charge-neutral fluctuations of the order parameter, and they
are the direct magnetic analogue of a phonon. Magnons have no charge, so electric fields do not affect
them at all, and they can propagate in materials that are electronic insulators. A ferromagnetic or
antiferromagnetic Mott insulator does not let any current through, but a magnon can still carry spin
and heat across it. Quantum magnets have instead \textbf{spinons}, which are spin-$1/2$ and
charge-neutral excitations. They are sometimes described informally as ``half an electron,'' since the
spin and the charge degrees of freedom of the electron have in practice separated. In the rest of this
session we will construct magnons in detail. Spinons and the Kondo lattice model only appear here as a
name and a spin quantum number. Their full construction is the material of a later session, session 8,
and this lecture does not come back to them.

\section{A recap of symmetry breaking and the mean-field philosophy}

We have already used this idea twice in this course, first in a small form in session 1 and then in
full detail for superconductivity in session 5. Let us state it once more, in a short way, before we
apply it to magnetism. Many-body interactions can spontaneously break a symmetry that the Hamiltonian
itself has. Three cases appear in the whole course. Magnets break time-reversal symmetry, and they
also break spin-rotation symmetry, since a magnetic moment breaks both. Charge density waves break the translational
symmetry of the lattice. Superconductors break gauge symmetry. All three emerge from the same generic
starting point, which is a kinetic term that is single-particle and exactly solvable, plus a quartic
many-body interaction. All three are also obtained in the same way. Mean-field theory factorizes the
quartic term by replacing part of it with its expectation value, which turns a many-body problem that
we cannot solve into a single-particle problem that we can solve, and we solve it self-consistently.

Where do the interactions themselves come from? There are two sources. The electrostatic, or Coulomb,
repulsion between electrons is the classical contribution, and it is always present. Interactions can
also be mediated by other quasiparticles, such as phonons, magnons or plasmons, and these can change
the \emph{net} effective interaction from repulsive to attractive. Magnetism needs a net interaction
that is repulsive. The bare Coulomb repulsion is already repulsive, so this case is easy to realize,
which is one of the reasons why magnetism is so common in nature.

\section{The Hubbard model and mean-field magnetism, revisited}

The minimal Hamiltonian for all of this is the one-dimensional Hubbard chain that we first saw in
session 1,

\begin{equation}
H = -t\sum_{n,\sigma}\left(c_{n\sigma}^\dagger c_{n+1,\sigma} + \text{h.c.}\right) + U\sum_n c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\uparrow}\, c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow},
\end{equation}

which is the hopping $t$ plus a purely local onsite density-density interaction between opposite
spins, called the Hubbard term. A negative $U$, which is attractive, gives rise to superconductivity,
which was the topic of session 5. A positive $U$, which is repulsive, gives rise to magnetism, which is
our topic in this session.

The mean-field decoupling is done in the same way as before. We replace
$c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\uparrow}$ and $c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}$ by their
expectation values, and we obtain a bilinear Hamiltonian with onsite energies that depend on the spin.
Nothing forces these two expectation values to be equal, and the difference between them is the
magnetization,

\begin{equation}
m = \langle c_\uparrow^\dagger c_\uparrow\rangle - \langle c_\downarrow^\dagger c_\downarrow\rangle,
\end{equation}

which plays the role of an exchange field. In a schematic form, the resulting mean-field Hamiltonian is
$H_\text{MF} \sim -\tfrac{U}{2}\, m\, \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot c$, where $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is the
vector of Pauli matrices. Self-consistency gives $m=0$ for $U<0$ and a magnetic solution with
$m\neq 0$ for $U>0$, as in session 1.

There is a more careful point here, which the slides show but the spoken explanation only mentions.
Nothing in this construction chooses the $z$ axis. For a single site, the Hubbard interaction can be
rewritten exactly in terms of the local spin operator
$\mathbf{S} = \tfrac12 c_\alpha^\dagger \boldsymbol\sigma_{\alpha\beta} c_\beta$
and the total occupation $n = n_\uparrow + n_\downarrow$, as
$U\, n_\uparrow n_\downarrow = \tfrac{U}{2}n - \tfrac{2U}{3}\mathbf{S}^2$, where we used that a single site has
$\mathbf{S}^2=\tfrac34(n-2n_\uparrow n_\downarrow)$, since only a singly occupied site has a spin. Mean-field theory then factorizes the quadratic operator
$\mathbf{S}^2$ in the same way as any product of two operators, $\mathbf{S}^2 \approx
2\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle\cdot\mathbf{S} - \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle^2$, and the resulting exchange
field points along $\mathbf{m} \equiv \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$, in \emph{any} direction where this
expectation value is nonzero. If we choose $\langle c_\uparrow^\dagger c_\uparrow\rangle \neq
\langle c_\downarrow^\dagger c_\downarrow\rangle$, as in (6.2), we select $m_z\neq 0$. If we choose
instead $\langle c_\uparrow^\dagger c_\downarrow\rangle \neq 0$, we select $m_x$ or $m_y$. For this
reason mean-field magnetism is not restricted to the $z$ axis. It gives magnets with a magnetization
along any direction in space, and this includes noncollinear arrangements where $\mathbf{m}$ points in
different directions on different sites. This is the ``non-collinear mean-field Hamiltonian'' figure
on the slides, which the spoken lecture only names briefly.

Mean field can only be trusted when $U \ll t$. We will see in Section 6.6 that magnetism usually needs
the opposite regime, and this is the reason why the strongly localized limit, from Section 6.7 on, is
so important.

\section{Exchange splitting and antiferromagnetic order in numerical mean-field theory}

Let us now see the method of Section 6.3 in a real calculation. First we take the one-dimensional
chain (6.1) with a unit cell of one site at 20\% filling, and we solve it self-consistently. The
resulting band structure shows an exchange splitting. The spin-up band, which is occupied up to the
chemical potential, is at lower energy than the spin-down band, which is empty at this filling. The
splitting between them is called the \textbf{exchange splitting}, and it grows with $U$. Only spin-up
states are occupied, so this is a fully polarized ferromagnetic solution with $m\neq 0$.

Now we take a unit cell with two sites, and we work at half filling. At $U=0$ there are two bands, one
per site in the cell, and they cross at one point, so the system is metallic. When we turn on $U>0$ a
mean-field gap opens, and in real space the two sublattices get opposite spin polarization, with one
site mostly up and its neighbor mostly down. The \emph{net} magnetization is zero, but the
\emph{staggered} magnetization is not, and this is antiferromagnetic order. As always when a
mean-field gap opens, it lowers the total electronic energy with respect to the state without a gap,
because the states that the gap pushes down in energy are the occupied ones.

\section{Competition between the ferromagnet and the antiferromagnet}

The Hubbard chain (6.1) has \emph{both} a uniform self-consistent solution, with one site per cell and
without the gap of the doubled cell, which is the paramagnet below $U\approx 4t$ and a fully polarized
ferromagnet above it, where the spin-up band is full and the spin-down band is empty, and an antiferromagnetic one, with two sites per cell, insulating and
gapped, at the same filling and the same $U$. Both are real fixed points of the self-consistency loop.
Only one of them can be the true ground state, and we decide which one by comparing total energies.
Here the gapped antiferromagnetic solution always has the lower energy. Opening a gap pushes the
occupied single-particle energies down, so the insulating configuration has a lower total energy than
the gapless ferromagnetic one for every $U>0$ that we can reach numerically in this half-filled chain.

Let us follow how large this energy difference is as a function of $U$. At $U=0$ the two
configurations are the same, so the energy difference is zero. When $U$ grows from zero the gap opens
and the energy difference grows with it. However, when $U$ keeps growing, the energy difference
starts to \emph{decrease} again, which is not what one would expect. In the limit $U\to\infty$, which
is the same as the limit where the kinetic energy can be neglected, the two configurations become
degenerate. Numerically, this energy difference follows a $1/U$ form. In Section 6.8 we will derive
this exact $1/U$ scaling analytically from a completely independent calculation, which is a real
cross-check between the mean-field numerics and a many-body perturbative treatment.

\section{The Stoner threshold as a criterion for magnetism}

A natural question is whether \emph{any} repulsive $U>0$ gives magnetism, or if there is a threshold.
We write the mean-field bands as $E_\sigma(\mathbf{k}) = E(\mathbf{k}) - \sigma\, \tfrac{U}{2}m$ for
$\sigma=\pm1$, meaning spin up and down, where $m$ is the self-consistent magnetization of (6.2). At
zero temperature, the self-consistency condition is

\begin{equation*}
m = \sum_{\mathbf k}\Big[n_F\big(E(\mathbf k)-\tfrac{U}{2}m\big) - n_F\big(E(\mathbf k)+\tfrac{U}{2}m\big)\Big].
\end{equation*}

If we expand the right-hand side for small $m$ we obtain $m \approx U\,m\,D(E_F)$, where $D(E_F)$ is
the density of states at the Fermi level. The trivial solution $m=0$ always exists, but a
\emph{nontrivial} solution appears when the linear coefficient reaches one, which gives the condition

\begin{equation}
U\, D(E_F) \geq 1.
\end{equation}

This condition is called the Stoner criterion. Below the threshold only $m=0$ is stable, and the
system stays nonmagnetic for any perturbation. Above the threshold the nonmagnetic solution becomes
unstable, and a finite $m$ appears spontaneously. This is the reason why some metals with a sizable
Coulomb repulsion, such as aluminum or lead, stay nonmagnetic, since their density of states at the
Fermi level is too small for the product in (6.3) to reach one. Iron and cobalt, on the other hand,
have a large density of states from their narrow $d$ bands together with a sizable effective $U$, so
they are clearly above the threshold. In the numerics we see what (6.3) predicts. The self-consistent
magnetization of the chain stays at zero below a critical $U$, and it grows continuously once $U$ is
above this threshold. Figure~\ref{fig:s06:stoner} shows both parts of this statement together. It
shows why a nontrivial solution of the self-consistency condition only exists above the threshold,
and how the resulting magnetization curve looks.

One important consequence ends this section and starts the next one. From (6.3), magnetism in general
needs $U D(E_F) \gtrsim 1$, and for a reasonable bandwidth this usually means that $U$ has to be
comparable to $t$ or larger, and not smaller. However, mean-field theory itself is only reliable for
$U \ll t$, as we saw in Section 6.3. So the regime where magnetism appears is, in general, the same
regime where the method that we have been using stops working. We need a different approach for
$U \gg t$, and we construct it in the rest of this session.

\section{The strongly localized limit, where mean field breaks down}

Let us look again at the half-filled chain of Section 6.4, but now we follow the magnetization per site
as $U$ grows from zero to very large values. At $U=0$ there is no interaction and $m=0$. When $U$
increases, $m$ grows, and for $U\to\infty$ it saturates at exactly one Bohr magneton per site. This is
a full localized spin-$1/2$ moment on every site, and the moments point in alternating directions on
the two sublattices, which is the ``Ising-like'' antiferromagnetic state to which the numerics
converge.

This saturation tells us something physical, which is that in the large-$U$ limit the electrons stop
moving completely. If an electron hops onto a neighboring site that is already occupied, this costs an
energy $U$. When $U\gg t$ this cost is too large, so each site keeps its one localized electron, and
only its spin can still fluctuate. For this reason, in this limit perturbation theory should \emph{not}
be organized around the kinetic term, as mean field does implicitly, but around the localized state.
We take the interacting and localized configuration as the ground state at zeroth order, and we treat
the hopping as a small perturbation on top of it.

\section{From the Hubbard dimer to the Heisenberg model}

Let us make this precise in the smallest possible example. We take two sites, $0$ and $1$, with a
hopping $t$ between them, a local repulsion $U$ on each of them, and exactly two electrons in total,
which is half filling. The Fock space at this filling has $\binom{4}{2}=6$ states. Four of them have
one electron on each site, where the electron of each site can be up or down independently. The other
two have both electrons on the same site, with one electron up and one down because of Pauli
exclusion.

Let us group these states by energy in the atomic limit ($t=0$). The four configurations with single
occupation, $|\!\uparrow,\uparrow\rangle$, $|\!\downarrow,\downarrow\rangle$, $|\!\uparrow,\downarrow\rangle$
and $|\!\downarrow,\uparrow\rangle$, have no interaction energy. We call them the \textbf{low-energy
manifold}, at energy $0$. The two configurations with double occupation,
$|\!\uparrow\downarrow,0\rangle$ and $|0,\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$, each have the full charging
energy $U$, and they form the \textbf{high-energy manifold}. In the low-energy manifold every state has
exactly one electron per site, so the charge degree of freedom is completely frozen and only the spin
on each site can still fluctuate. For this reason the natural low-energy effective Hamiltonian is a
Hamiltonian for the spin only, and rotational invariance fixes its form up to an overall coupling,
$H_\text{eff} = J\,
\mathbf S_0\cdot\mathbf S_1$, which is the two-site Heisenberg Hamiltonian. What
remains is to obtain $J$ from the microscopic $t$ and $U$.

The hopping, which is the perturbation, conserves the total spin, so for the \emph{parallel}-spin
states it cannot connect the low-energy manifold to the high-energy one at all. If we apply $H_t$ to
$|\!\uparrow,\uparrow\rangle$, we would need to move a spin-up electron onto a site that already has
one, and Pauli exclusion forbids this completely. The three parallel or triplet-like states,
$|\!\uparrow,\uparrow\rangle$, $|\!\downarrow,\downarrow\rangle$ and the symmetric combination
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|\!\uparrow,\downarrow\rangle+|\!\downarrow,\uparrow\rangle)$, are therefore exact
eigenstates of the \emph{full} Hamiltonian at energy exactly $E_T=0$, since the hopping does not act
on them at any order. The antiparallel or singlet combination
$|S\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|\!\uparrow,\downarrow\rangle-|\!\downarrow,\uparrow\rangle)$ is
different. The hopping \emph{can} connect it to the symmetric combination with double occupation,
$|D\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|\!\uparrow\downarrow,0\rangle+|0,\uparrow\downarrow\rangle)$, with
matrix element $\langle D|H_t|S\rangle = -2t$. In the two-dimensional subspace
$\{|S\rangle,|D\rangle\}$, the Hamiltonian is exactly

\begin{equation*}
H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2t \\ -2t & U\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}

with exact eigenvalues $E_\pm = \tfrac{U}{2}\pm\sqrt{(U/2)^2+4t^2}$. The energy of the singlet ground
state is the lower root, $E_S = \tfrac{U}{2}-\sqrt{(U/2)^2+4t^2}$, and if we expand it for $U\gg t$
we obtain

\begin{equation*}
E_S \;\approx\; -\frac{4t^2}{U} + O(t^4/U^3), \qquad E_T = 0 \text{ exactly.}
\end{equation*}

We compare this with the exact spectrum of the Heisenberg dimer, $E_\text{singlet}=-\tfrac34 J$ and
$E_\text{triplet}=+\tfrac14 J$, where the singlet-triplet gap is $\Delta E = E_T-E_S = J$, and we
obtain

\begin{equation}
J = \frac{4t^2}{U}.
\end{equation}

We have $J>0$, which is antiferromagnetic, and the singlet is below the triplet. This confirms, with
the correct numerical prefactor, the second-order perturbation theory that the lecture sketches, where
the unperturbed Hamiltonian is the Hubbard term, the perturbation is the hopping, the matrix element is
$t$ and the energy denominator is $U$. It also reproduces exactly the $1/U$ scaling that we found
numerically for the energy difference between the ferromagnet and the antiferromagnet in Section 6.5.
That mean-field energy difference per bond \emph{is} one half of the exchange coupling (6.4), so the two completely
different calculations, the mean-field numerics and the many-body perturbation theory, agree.
Figure~\ref{fig:s06:dimer} shows the whole argument. It shows the Pauli blockade that protects the
triplet, the virtual process that lowers the energy of the singlet, and the exact spectrum, whose
splitting is $J$.

\section{The Heisenberg model on a general lattice}

The argument of Section 6.8 did not use the fact that we had only two sites. For a generic lattice
with hopping $t_{ij}$ between sites $i$ and $j$ and a local interaction $U$, the same second-order
argument applies to every connected pair independently in the limit $U\gg t_{ij}$, and it gives the
generalized Heisenberg Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
H_\text{Heis} = \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} J_{ij}\, \mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j, \qquad J_{ij} = \frac{4t_{ij}^2}{U}.
\end{equation}

Since $J_{ij}\propto t_{ij}^2$, this superexchange coupling, derived directly from a single-orbital
Hubbard model, is always positive and antiferromagnetic, regardless of the sign or the geometry of
$t_{ij}$. More general microscopic models, which are not Hubbard models or have several orbitals, can
give couplings of both signs, and they do. In Section 6.11 we will see explicitly how a ferromagnetic
coupling appears when a second, intermediate orbital takes part. In all of this session we use the
following sign convention. A coupling $J_{ij}>0$ favors antiparallel neighboring spins, which is
antiferromagnetic, and $J_{ij}<0$ favors parallel neighboring spins, which is ferromagnetic. If we
treat the spins in (6.5) as classical vectors, we already obtain both classical ground states on a
bond between two sites, which are antiparallel for $J>0$ and parallel for $J<0$.

\section{Bipartite order versus geometric frustration}

On a \textbf{bipartite} lattice, such as the square or the honeycomb lattice, the sites split into two
sublattices $A$ and $B$ so that every bond connects an $A$ site with a $B$ site. On such a lattice the
antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model (6.5) has an obvious classical ground state. All spins on $A$ point
in one direction and all spins on $B$ point in the opposite direction, and in this way every bond has
antiparallel neighbors at the same time, which is what it prefers. This is the collinear
antiferromagnetic order with two sublattices that we already saw at the mean-field level in Section
6.4, and (6.5) explains \emph{why} it appears. In the large-$U$ limit, the mean-field Hubbard model on
a bipartite lattice reduces exactly to this Heisenberg model.

Frustrated lattices are different. On a \textbf{non-bipartite} lattice, of which the kagome and the
triangular lattices are the standard examples, it is not possible to color the sites with two colors
so that every bond connects opposite colors. For instance, a triangular plaquette has three sites that
are all neighbors of each other, and no choice of up and down spins makes all three bonds antiparallel
at the same time. The antiferromagnetic tendency of $J_{ij}>0$ is still present on every single bond,
but it cannot be satisfied everywhere at the same time, and this is what is called \textbf{geometric
frustration}. The best compromise for the system is usually a noncollinear arrangement, and the
standard case is spins at $120^\circ$ from each other on a triangular plaquette. This arrangement
maximizes the total antiferromagnetic character bond by bond, without fully satisfying any single
bond. This noncollinear character also appears directly in the mean-field solution of the
corresponding electronic Hamiltonian, and not only in the spin model. Figure~\ref{fig:s06:frustration}
shows next to each other the bipartite case, the frustrated collinear attempt and the $120^\circ$
compromise, together with the classical bond energies that decide between the last two.

\section{Superexchange as the origin of ferromagnetic coupling}

The direct $t^2/U$ mechanism of Section 6.9 is always antiferromagnetic, but many real magnetic
materials are ferromagnets. So a ferromagnetic coupling needs a different microscopic mechanism, and
it usually requires an intermediate atom, called a ligand, between the two magnetic ions, instead of a
single direct hop. Let us take two transition-metal ions, for example cobalt, iron or nickel, connected
by a ligand such as oxygen. As before, electrons can hop directly between the two metal sites, and
this channel is antiferromagnetic as in Section 6.9. However, there is a second virtual process. An
electron hops from the ligand onto one metal ion, and another electron hops from the ligand onto the
other one. The two electrons are now on the metal ions, and this leaves a virtual state with two
holes in the $p$ shell of the ligand, which was full before.
Depending on which $d$ orbitals take part and on the metal-ligand-metal bond angle, the energy
correction from this virtual channel with two holes on the ligand can favor the ferromagnetic
configuration over the antiferromagnetic one, and in this way it changes the net sign of the effective
coupling. The systematic version of this argument is given by the \textbf{Goodenough-Kanamori rules},
which predict the sign of the exchange coupling from the orbital filling and the bond geometry. These
rules can also be derived systematically from self-consistent multi-orbital calculations, for instance
with density functional theory. This material comes from the slides and the transcript, and there is no
dedicated notebook exercise for it in this session, since the exercises of the notebook stay within
the single-orbital Hubbard model.

\section{Anisotropic exchange with Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya, Ising and Kitaev terms}

In (6.5) we assumed that the coupling is an isotropic scalar product, $\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf
S_j$, which is what the rotational spin symmetry requires when there is no spin-orbit coupling. Once
spin-orbit coupling is included in the underlying electronic Hamiltonian, other spin couplings with
lower symmetry become allowed. Three of them appear often in real materials, and we list them now.

\textbf{Antisymmetric, or Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya, exchange},
\begin{equation*}
H_\text{DM} = \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} \mathbf D_{ij}\cdot\big(\mathbf S_i\times\mathbf S_j\big),
\end{equation*}
which is a cross product and not a dot product. It requires broken mirror symmetry, which is the
reason why it is strong at surfaces and interfaces, where the substrate itself breaks this mirror
symmetry. It favors neighboring spins rotated by roughly $90^\circ$ with respect to each other, so it
favors noncollinear order, which is often a spiral.

\textbf{Anisotropic, or Ising-like, exchange}, which is a coupling of a single component such as
$\Lambda\, S_i^z S_j^z$. It favors an easy axis, or an easy plane for the opposite sign, which means
that the system magnetizes more easily along one direction than along another.

\textbf{Kitaev interaction}, which is a coupling that depends on the bond, on a lattice of honeycomb
type. It has an $S^x_iS^x_j$ term on one bond direction, $S^y_iS^y_j$ on a second one and
$S^z_iS^z_j$ on a third one,
\begin{equation*}
H_\text{Kitaev} = \sum_{\langle ij\rangle \in x\text{-bonds}}\! K\, S_i^xS_j^x \;+\!\!\sum_{\langle ij\rangle \in y\text{-bonds}}\!\! K\, S_i^yS_j^y \;+\!\!\sum_{\langle ij\rangle \in z\text{-bonds}}\!\! K\, S_i^zS_j^z.
\end{equation*}
Even on a bipartite honeycomb lattice, where the geometry alone would not give any frustration, this
dependence on the bond is frustrating by itself, since no single spin orientation can satisfy the
three types of bonds at the same time (figure~\ref{fig:s06:kitaev}). This interaction is what makes the
Kitaev model a paradigm for quantum spin liquids, and this is why we point it out here explicitly, as
an important preparation for the material on frustrated magnets in sessions 7 and 8.

\section{Magnons as fluctuations of the order parameter}

Until now we have only asked about ground states. Let us now ask about excitations. We take the
Heisenberg model (6.5) with $J<0$, which is ferromagnetic, and we promote the spins to real quantum
operators that satisfy the angular-momentum algebra $[S_i^a,S_j^b] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}\epsilon^{abc}S_i^c$.
From here on we set $\hbar=1$, which is the standard choice for this kind of calculation, and we ask
for the low-lying excited states. In contrast with the interacting electron problem, the exact ground
state of the ferromagnetic Heisenberg model is known immediately. It is the fully polarized state with
all spins aligned, since for $J<0$ no other configuration can lower $\langle \mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf
S_j\rangle$ further. A \textbf{magnon} is a small fluctuation of the magnetization that propagates on
top of this fully polarized state. Qualitatively, it is a single spin tilted slightly out of
alignment, delocalized coherently over the whole lattice as a wave. The question is how to obtain
these excitations systematically from a many-body spin Hamiltonian. The answer is a change of
variables that turns the spin problem into a bosonic tight-binding problem.

\section{The Holstein-Primakoff transformation}

There is an exact mapping between spin operators and bosonic creation and annihilation operators
$a_i^\dagger, a_i$ with $[a_i,a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}$, which is called the \textbf{Holstein-Primakoff
transformation}. For a spin of magnitude $S$ on site $i$, it reads

\begin{equation}
S_i^z = S - a_i^\dagger a_i, \qquad S_i^+ = \sqrt{2S-a_i^\dagger a_i}\;a_i, \qquad S_i^- = a_i^\dagger\sqrt{2S-a_i^\dagger a_i}.
\end{equation}

This is an \emph{exact} rewriting. If we insert (6.6) and use the bosonic commutation relations, we
obtain exactly the full $SU(2)$ spin algebra $[S_i^z,S_i^\pm]=\pm S_i^\pm$, $[S_i^+,S_i^-]=2S_i^z$,
without any approximation so far. The physical picture is direct. The operator $a_i^\dagger a_i$ counts
how many units of spin have been removed from the fully polarized value $S$ on site $i$, which means
that it counts the \textbf{magnons} on that site, and $S_i^z=S-a_i^\dagger a_i$ states exactly this.
The square-root operators are what make (6.6) exact even far from full polarization. They are also the
reason why the Hamiltonian, written in this form, looks more complicated and not simpler.

The simplification comes from linearizing. When the expectation value of $a_i^\dagger a_i$ stays much
smaller than $S$, which means when we stay close to the fully polarized ground state, we can expand
the square roots and keep only the leading term. This is the regime that we want, since we are looking
for low-lying excitations above this ground state, and the result is

\begin{equation}
S_i^+ \approx \sqrt{2S}\, a_i, \qquad S_i^- \approx \sqrt{2S}\, a_i^\dagger, \qquad S_i^z = S - a_i^\dagger a_i.
\end{equation}

This linearized form is the one that is used in practice to compute magnon spectra. The counting that
follows from it is the easiest way to remember the whole transformation, so we write it out. The
operator $S^+$ \emph{raises} $S^z$, meaning that it increases the local magnetization back toward
saturation, and it \emph{destroys} a magnon. The operator $S^-$ lowers the magnetization, and it
\emph{creates} a magnon. The total magnetization of the system is the fully saturated value minus the
total number of magnons, $\sum_i \langle S_i^z\rangle = NS - \sum_i\langle a_i^\dagger a_i\rangle$.
Each magnon has exactly one unit of spin, so magnons are $S=1$ excitations, in agreement with what we
said about them in Section 6.1. They exist only because the ground state on which they live has
already broken spin-rotation symmetry spontaneously. Figure~\ref{fig:s06:magnon} shows the three
parts, which are the fully polarized ground state, the delocalized precession wave that carries a
single magnon, and this rule for raising and lowering.

\input{figures/s06f05_magnon}

We insert (6.7) into the ferromagnetic Heisenberg Hamiltonian, $H = J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}
\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j = J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\big[S_i^zS_j^z + \tfrac12(S_i^+S_j^-+S_i^-S_j^+)\big]$,
and we keep the terms up to quadratic order in the bosonic operators,

\begin{equation*}
S_i^zS_j^z \approx S^2 - S\big(a_i^\dagger a_i + a_j^\dagger a_j\big), \qquad
\tfrac12\big(S_i^+S_j^- + S_i^-S_j^+\big) \approx S\big(a_i^\dagger a_j + a_j^\dagger a_i\big),
\end{equation*}

and in this way the spin Hamiltonian becomes a bosonic tight-binding Hamiltonian for magnons, and its
hopping amplitude is set directly by the exchange coupling $J$, as we said before. The magnon ``hops''
between sites with an amplitude proportional to $JS$, in direct analogy with the electronic
tight-binding Hamiltonians that we have diagonalized during the whole course.

\section{Magnon dispersions in ferromagnets and antiferromagnets}

We Fourier transform the bosonic Hamiltonian of Section 6.14 on a Bravais lattice with coordination
number $z$ and first-neighbor structure factor $\gamma(\mathbf k) = \tfrac1z\sum_{\boldsymbol\delta}
e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta}$. For the \textbf{ferromagnet}, with $J<0$ and $|J|=-J$, this
gives a diagonal magnon Hamiltonian with dispersion

\begin{equation}
E_\text{FM}(\mathbf k) = |J|\,S\,z\big(1-\gamma(\mathbf k)\big).
\end{equation}

This dispersion is zero at $\mathbf k=0$, as it has to be. A uniform rotation of all the spins costs no
energy, and this is the Goldstone mode of the broken spin-rotation symmetry. If we expand
$\gamma(\mathbf k)$ for small $\mathbf k$ we obtain $E_\text{FM}(\mathbf k)\propto k^2$, which is a
\textbf{quadratic} magnon dispersion, and this is the ``gapless, quadratic'' ferromagnetic magnon that
the lecture points out.

The \textbf{antiferromagnetic} case needs one more step, because the ground state is not ``all spins
up.'' On a bipartite lattice, we apply the Holstein-Primakoff transformation (6.7) separately on the
two sublattices, and on the $B$ sublattice the roles of $S^+$ and $S^-$ are exchanged, since these
spins point in the opposite direction. We have $S_i^z=S-a_i^\dagger a_i$ on $A$, but $S_j^z=-S+b_j^\dagger b_j$ on $B$.
If we insert this into $\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$ for a bond between the two sublattices, we obtain
not only density terms $a^\dagger a$ and $b^\dagger b$, but also \emph{anomalous} terms
$a_i b_j$ and $a_i^\dagger b_j^\dagger$, which do not conserve the particle number. This tells us that
the magnon vacuum on top of the Néel state is not the bosonic vacuum, and that to diagonalize the
Hamiltonian we need a Bogoliubov transformation and not only a Fourier transform. If we do this, we
obtain the antiferromagnetic magnon dispersion

\begin{equation}
E_\text{AFM}(\mathbf k) = J\,S\,z\,\sqrt{1-\gamma(\mathbf k)^2}.
\end{equation}

Close to $\mathbf k=0$ we have $1-\gamma(\mathbf k)^2 \propto k^2$, so $E_\text{AFM}(\mathbf k)\propto |k|$.
This is a \textbf{linear} dispersion similar to a Dirac cone, which is gapless at the same point but
linear and not quadratic. This is the rule ``quadratic in ferromagnets, linear in antiferromagnets''
from the lecture, which we have now derived and not only stated. It is also one more example, together
with the Dirac cones of session 2 and 3, of a linear dispersion that comes from the structure of a
symmetry and is not put in by hand. Figure~\ref{fig:s06:dispersion} shows (6.8) and (6.9) together,
with a magnified view of the zone center, so that the parabola and the cone can be distinguished.

\section{Topological magnons}

Once magnons have a real band structure, which is a dispersion $E(\mathbf k)$ over the Brillouin zone,
we can ask about \emph{magnon} bands the same question that we have already asked twice about
\emph{electronic} bands. Can this band structure have a nontrivial topological invariant? To answer
it, we use again the Chern number of sessions 3 and 4, where the Berry curvature $\Omega(\mathbf k)$ of
a Bloch band is integrated over the Brillouin zone and gives an integer,

\begin{equation}
C = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_\text{BZ} \Omega(\mathbf k)\, d^2k,
\end{equation}

and this is the third time that we apply it, now to the bosonic Bogoliubov Hamiltonian of Section 6.15
instead of an electronic tight-binding Hamiltonian. We add to the plain Heisenberg exchange a
spin-orbit coupling of the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya type of Section 6.12. This term plays the same role
as the complex second-neighbor hopping of the Haldane model for electrons in session 4. It introduces
complex phases in the magnon hopping amplitudes, which can open a gap in the magnon band structure at
finite energy and give the bands on both sides of this gap a nonzero Chern number (6.10). When we put
such a topological magnon Hamiltonian on a ribbon, the bulk-boundary correspondence guarantees chiral
magnon edge modes inside the gap. These modes propagate in only one direction along each edge, and
they are the magnon analogue of the chiral electronic edge states of a Chern insulator. They carry spin
and energy and not charge, but they are topologically protected in the same sense as their electronic
counterparts. It is the same tool that we used for electrons in sessions 3 and 4, now applied to
magnon bands.
