\chapter{Symmetries, Tight-Binding Band Structure Theory, and Effective Models}

In the last session we introduced second quantization and used it in practice. Mean-field theory
turned an interacting, quartic Hamiltonian into an effective single-particle one, and we saw that
the same method gives a charge density wave, a magnet and a superconductor, one for each of the
three symmetries that a many-body ground state can spontaneously break. We also spent some time on
what this breaking means. We used the example of the bread on the table, where there is a set of
solutions with the same energy and no single one of them has the full symmetry. We saw that this
picture only becomes sharp in the thermodynamic limit, we discussed the ferroelectric as a fourth
example next to magnets and superconductors, and we compared all this with topological order, which
can distinguish two materials that have the same broken symmetries. This is a complete topic by
itself, and in this session we will not repeat any of it.

In this session we ask a different question, which is about the structure of the problem. Once
the interactions are included in a mean-field, single-particle Hamiltonian, how do we solve it? For a
few sites we can diagonalize a matrix directly, but a real material has of order $10^{23}$ sites,
and no computer can diagonalize a matrix of this size. The answer is again symmetry, but now it has a
different role than in the last session. We do not use it to classify which order a ground state
breaks, and instead we use it to simplify the diagonalization. When the ground state of a
Hamiltonian \emph{shares} the symmetry of the Hamiltonian, the symmetry is not only a classification
tool, and it becomes a real shortcut. It turns an infinite-dimensional problem that we cannot solve
into infinitely many finite-dimensional problems that are easy to solve. For a periodic system, this
shortcut is called Bloch's theorem. At the end of this session we will have used it to derive
several real band structures, including the Dirac cone of graphene, which the topology lecture that
comes right after this one will take and gap open.

\section{Motivation and recap}

Let us recall the form of a many-body Hamiltonian once it is written in second-quantized,
tight-binding form,

\begin{equation*}H = \sum_{ij} t_{ij}\, c_i^\dagger c_j,\end{equation*}

where $t_{ij}$ are the hopping matrix elements between sites $i$ and $j$. In this language, to solve
a quantum material means to diagonalize this Hamiltonian, which is to find the unitary transformation
that brings it into the diagonal form

\begin{equation*}H = \sum_n \epsilon_n\, \Psi_n^\dagger \Psi_n, \qquad \Psi_n^\dagger = \sum_i a_{n,i}\, c_i^\dagger.\end{equation*}

Symmetries allow us to find this diagonal form, or at least strong qualitative and quantitative
properties of it, without doing the diagonalization explicitly. This is the motivation for
everything in this session. We will use symmetry not to classify order, but to simplify how we solve
a quantum many-body problem.

\section{Simplifying problems with symmetry in simple examples}

We have all used this method before, also outside quantum mechanics. Let us take an infinite plane
with a uniform positive charge, and let us ask in which direction the electric field points at some
position away from the plane. It can point straight out, or it can be tilted by some angle. We can
apply a mirror symmetry that exchanges left and right. The charge distribution does not change under
this reflection, so the true field must also map to itself under the same reflection. A tilted field
does not pass this test, since its mirror image is tilted in the other direction, while a field that
points straight out does pass it. Since the electric field at that point is unique, symmetry alone
removes every answer except the one perpendicular to the plane, and we never have to compute the
integral from Coulomb's law.

The same argument also works for a less simple shape. Let us replace the infinite plane by an
ellipse with a uniform charge, which is an egg-shaped charge distribution, and let us ask the same
question about the field at some point on its mirror axis. Mirror symmetry again gives the answer
without tilt, this time with the reflection axis of the ellipse, and again without any explicit
integration. Note that this only works on the mirror axis, since at a point away from it the
reflection maps the point to a different point, and symmetry alone does not fix the direction there.

Mirrors are only the simplest case. A circle stays the same under any rotation. A square stays the
same only under rotations by $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, and $270^\circ$, and a shape with less symmetry
may stay the same only under a single mirror. Quantum systems can have much richer symmetries, which
are also much less obvious than these, and in the following we turn this richer structure into a
systematic tool.

\section{Symmetry operators in a formal way}

To make the argument from electromagnetism precise for a quantum system, we need three objects. The
first is a wavefunction $\psi$, the second is the transformed wavefunction $\phi = S\psi$ that we
obtain when we apply a symmetry operation, and the third is the operator $S$ itself, which is a
linear map from one to the other and a matrix in a finite-dimensional representation. A concrete
case makes this clear. If $\psi$ is a $p_x$ orbital and $S$ is a $90^\circ$ rotation, the
transformed state $\phi = S\psi$ is a $p_y$ orbital, meaning that the two lobes on the $x$-axis are
rotated into two lobes on the $y$-axis. Note that this $p_x$ orbital is \emph{not} an eigenstate of
the rotation, since it is mapped to a different state.

Symmetry operators are always unitary. In physical terms, a symmetry is only a change of labels of
our reference frame, for example a rotation of the axes, and any such change of labels must keep the
norm of every state in the Hilbert space. In equations,

\begin{equation}S^\dagger S = S S^\dagger = \mathbb{1}, \qquad S^\dagger = S^{-1}.\end{equation}

A direct consequence is that all the eigenvalues of $S$ have unit modulus, since $\langle\psi|S^\dagger
S|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ gives $|\lambda|^2=1$ for any eigenvalue $\lambda$. So a
symmetry eigenvalue can always be written as a pure phase,

\begin{equation}S|\psi\rangle = e^{i\varphi}|\psi\rangle, \qquad \varphi \in (-\pi,\pi].\end{equation}

Before we include a Hamiltonian, let us look at two small examples. We take a wavefunction with real,
staggered values $(+1,-1,+1,-1)$ on four sites on a ring, and we apply a $90^\circ$ rotation
that shifts the sites by one in a cyclic way. The transformed wavefunction is $(-1,+1,-1,+1)$, which
is $-1$ times the original. So this state is an eigenstate of the rotation with eigenvalue $-1$, that
is $\varphi=\pi$ in (2.2). Now we take a complex wavefunction $(+1,-i,-1,i)$ on the same ring and
apply the same rotation. The transformed wavefunction is $i$ times the original, so it is an
eigenstate with eigenvalue $i$, that is $\varphi=\pi/2$.

The next example is less visual but more useful in practice. A two-site tight-binding Hamiltonian
with a single hopping $t$ between sites 1 and 2,

\begin{equation*}H = t\left(c_1^\dagger c_2 + c_2^\dagger c_1\right),\end{equation*}

has two eigenstates, the bonding and antibonding combinations $c_1^\dagger+c_2^\dagger$ and
$c_1^\dagger-c_2^\dagger$. Under the mirror symmetry that exchanges sites 1 and 2, the bonding state
has eigenvalue $+1$ and the antibonding state has eigenvalue $-1$. This means that different
eigenstates of the same Hamiltonian can have different eigenvalues under the same symmetry.

We now come to the central fact of this session. Let us assume that a Hamiltonian is invariant under
a symmetry operation, $SHS^{-1} = SHS^\dagger = H$, which is the same as the statement

\begin{equation}[H,S] = 0.\end{equation}

From linear algebra we know that two operators that commute have a common eigenbasis. So whenever
(2.3) holds, every eigenstate of $H$ can be chosen so that it is also an eigenstate of $S$. In the
words of the lecturer, this is perhaps the most important idea of this session. The reason for this
is that it is very often much easier to classify the eigenstates of a symmetry operator, whose
eigenvalue is a simple phase by (2.2), than to classify the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian directly.
In practice, this means that to find all the symmetries of the Hamiltonian is the first step to solve
it, and the most useful one, not a side remark. Once we know that a matrix $S$
commutes with $H$, to classify the eigenstates of $S$ is the same task as to classify the
eigenstates of $H$.

\section{Exercises where we guess eigenstates from symmetry only}

We will now see with a sequence of small examples how much (2.3) gives us. In each example we add
sites, and at the end we take the infinite limit.

\textbf{Three sites on a ring.} We take a tight-binding Hamiltonian on three sites in a triangle,
with a symmetry $R$ that rotates $1\to2\to3\to1$ by $120^\circ$. Since $R^3=\mathbb{1}$, any
eigenvalue $\lambda=e^{i\varphi}$ of $R$ must satisfy $\lambda^3=1$, so

\begin{equation}\varphi_n = \frac{2\pi n}{3}, \qquad n=0,1,2.\end{equation}

So we know a lot about the eigenstates of $H$ before we write $H$ explicitly. They must have the form
$\alpha_1c_1^\dagger+\alpha_2c_2^\dagger+\alpha_3c_3^\dagger$. Since $R$ permutes the sites in a
cyclic way and multiplies the whole state by $e^{i\varphi_n}$, the coefficients must have the same
phase from one site to the next, $\alpha_j\propto e^{i\varphi_n j}$. The eigenstates of any
Hamiltonian with this rotational symmetry are exactly the three discrete Fourier modes on the ring,
regardless of the values of the hopping amplitudes. Figure~\ref{fig:s02:ring} shows the three modes
explicitly, and it also shows how the set of allowed phases changes when the ring becomes larger.

\input{figures/s02f01_ringmodes}

\textbf{Five sites on a ring.} The same argument on a five-site ring with nearest-neighbour hopping,
$H=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} c_i^\dagger c_j$, gives $\varphi_n=2\pi n/5$, $n=0,\dots,4$, and the
eigenstates $|\Psi_n\rangle \propto \sum_{j=0}^4 e^{i\varphi_n j}\,c_j^\dagger|0\rangle$. With this
we can already answer a quantitative question without diagonalizing anything. Let us ask what the
weight of site 1 is in the lowest-energy eigenstate built from the vacuum $|\Omega\rangle$, in the
sense of the overlap $\big|\langle\Omega|c_1\Psi_{\rm GS}^\dagger|\Omega\rangle\big|^2$. Every
$|\Psi_n\rangle$ is a superposition over all five sites with the same modulus on each site, so this
overlap is $1/5$ for every $n$, for the lowest energy and for all other states. This follows from the
symmetry only, and it does not depend on the specific hopping amplitudes. The same argument stays
valid if the nearest-neighbour hopping is replaced by any long-range hopping, and a six-site ring
with additional long-range couplings shows the same thing. As long as the rotational symmetry of the
ring is present, the symmetry eigenvalues, the eigenstates and expectation values such as this one
are all fixed by symmetry only, however complicated the Hamiltonian becomes.

Note that such models with few sites are not only of academic interest. With scanning tunnelling
microscopy one can place single atoms with essentially arbitrary precision, and tight-binding rings
with few sites like these have been built and measured in this way. There is a famous stop-motion
film made only from atoms moved with STM, which is sometimes called ``the smallest film ever made,''
where every dot on the screen is a single atom. It shows how precisely such models can now be realized in the lab.

\textbf{The infinite chain.} Now we let the ring grow without limit, so that it becomes an infinite
one-dimensional chain with nearest-neighbour hopping. The relevant symmetry is a translation by one
site, $T: c_n \to c_{n+1}$, and $[H,T]=0$ for any Hamiltonian that only has hoppings that depend on
the distance between sites and not on the absolute position. By Section 2.3, $H$ and $T$ have a
common eigenbasis, and by (2.2) the eigenvalue of $T$ in this common eigenbasis is a pure phase
$e^{i\varphi}$. This $\varphi$ is called the \textbf{Bloch phase}. The statement that $H$ and $T$
have a common eigenbasis, when we apply it to a system with translational symmetry, \emph{is}
Bloch's theorem, which says that if two operators commute, there is a common basis for both of them.
The relation between this phase and the corresponding energy of the Hamiltonian is what we call the
\textbf{dispersion relation} of a periodic system.

\section{Bloch's theorem and the first band structure}

Let us now do this fully explicitly for the one-dimensional tight-binding chain,

\begin{equation}H = -t\sum_n \left(c_n^\dagger c_{n+1} + \text{h.c.}\right).\end{equation}

We take $t>0$ in all of this session, the same sign convention as in (1.6) of the previous
session.

Since $[H,T]=0$, we look for eigenstates that are a superposition over all sites that transforms in a
simple way under translation,

\begin{equation}|\Psi_\varphi\rangle = \sum_n e^{in\varphi}\, c_n^\dagger|0\rangle.\end{equation}

It is cleaner to invert this relation and to define new operators that are labelled by $\varphi$
instead of by site,

\begin{equation}\Psi_\varphi^\dagger = \sum_n e^{in\varphi}\, c_n^\dagger, \qquad
c_n^\dagger = \sum_\varphi e^{-in\varphi}\, \Psi_\varphi^\dagger,\end{equation}

which is an ordinary Fourier transform between real space and the space of Bloch phases. Putting (2.7) into (2.5) gives

\begin{equation*}\sum_n c_n^\dagger c_{n+1} = \sum_{n,\varphi,\varphi'} e^{-in\varphi}\,e^{i\varphi'(n+1)}\,
\Psi_\varphi^\dagger \Psi_{\varphi'} = \sum_{\varphi,\varphi'} e^{i\varphi'}\,\delta_{\varphi\varphi'}\,
\Psi_\varphi^\dagger\Psi_{\varphi'} = \sum_\varphi e^{i\varphi}\, \Psi_\varphi^\dagger\Psi_\varphi,\end{equation*}

where the sum over $n$ gave the orthogonality relation $\sum_n e^{in(\varphi'-\varphi)} =
\delta_{\varphi\varphi'}$. The Hermitian conjugate term gives the complex conjugate phase, so

\begin{equation}H = -t\sum_\varphi \left(e^{i\varphi}+e^{-i\varphi}\right)\Psi_\varphi^\dagger\Psi_\varphi
= \sum_\varphi \epsilon_\varphi\, \Psi_\varphi^\dagger\Psi_\varphi, \qquad
\boxed{\epsilon_\varphi = -2t\cos\varphi}.\end{equation}

This is our first band structure, and we obtained it without writing down an infinite matrix, and
without diagonalizing it. We only needed the fact that the eigenstates must have a definite phase
under translation, and then a single Fourier transform gave the rest.

Some features of this dispersion should be discussed explicitly, since they appear again and again
in the course. Near the bottom of the band ($\varphi=0$), $\epsilon_\varphi \approx -2t + t\varphi^2$,
which is a parabola that opens upward. Near the top of the band ($\varphi=\pi$), we write
$\varphi=\pi+\delta$ and obtain $\epsilon_\varphi\approx 2t-t\delta^2$, which is a parabola that opens
downward. The two curvatures have \emph{opposite} sign. Once we define the effective mass properly in
Section 2.9, this means that low-energy quasiparticles near the bottom of the band behave like
ordinary electrons with positive mass, while those near the top behave like holes with negative mass.
Near the middle of the band, at $\varphi=\pi/2$ where $\epsilon_\varphi$ crosses zero, the dispersion
is closer to linear than to quadratic, and this is a first sign of the Dirac-like physics that we will
see in detail in Section 2.11. All of this is shown in figure~\ref{fig:s02:cosband}, which also shows
the density of states of this band.

\section{The meaning of the phase, which is the quasi-momentum}

Let us stop here and ask what the phase $\varphi$ in (2.2) means physically, and not only formally.
We go back to the Schrödinger equation in the continuum and ask how a wavefunction translated by the
lattice constant $a$, $\psi(x+a)$, is related to $\psi(x)$. A Taylor expansion gives

\begin{equation*}\psi(x+a) = \psi(x) + a\,\partial_x\psi(x) + \frac{a^2}{2}\partial_x^2\psi(x) + \cdots
= e^{a\partial_x}\psi(x) = e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}ap}\,\psi(x),\end{equation*}

where $p=-i\hbar\partial_x$ is the momentum operator. If $\psi$ is an eigenstate of the translation
operator with eigenvalue $e^{i\varphi}$, when we compare the two expressions we find

\begin{equation}\varphi = \frac{a\,p}{\hbar} = k\,a,\end{equation}

meaning that the symmetry eigenvalue is directly the crystal momentum $k=p/\hbar$ times the lattice
constant. This is the reason why $\varphi$ is called the \emph{quasi-momentum}. It is directly
related to the momentum operator of the original Schrödinger equation, only measured in units where
the lattice constant is absorbed, and it takes values in $\varphi\in(-\pi,\pi]$, which is the first
Brillouin zone.

This identification also tells us where the orthogonal basis with one orbital per site comes from,
which we have used so far without saying it. The atomic orbitals of a real material, $s$, $p$, $d$,
and so on, are not orthogonal between neighbouring sites. Before we write a clean tight-binding
Hamiltonian, we first go to an orthogonal local basis with one orbital per site, which we obtain in a
systematic way with what is called \textbf{Löwdin orthogonalization}. The contact session works this
construction out properly, and here we only assume it, and the slides also call this basis ``Wannier
states''. In this basis, symmetry alone already tells us that the eigenstates have the form
$e^{in\varphi}c_n^\dagger|0\rangle$, which is the ansatz (2.6). This is true for a finite ring of $7$
sites, for $11$ sites, and for the infinite chain that we want in order to describe a solid. We can
build the same intuition by working with finite rings that become larger and larger, before we take
the limit $L\to\infty$.

\section{Multi-orbital band structures}

Real unit cells almost never have a single orbital. Graphene has two, one per sublattice, compounds
of transition metals often have several $d$-orbitals per site, and so on. Let $\alpha$ label the
orbital within a unit cell and $n$ label the unit cell itself. We keep couplings only within a cell
and to the nearest-neighbouring cell, and longer-range couplings work with the same method, only
with more terms. The most general bilinear Hamiltonian built from these operators is then

\begin{equation}H = \sum_{n,\alpha,\beta} t_{\alpha\beta}\, c_{n,\alpha}^\dagger c_{n,\beta}
+ \sum_{n,\alpha,\beta} \gamma_{\alpha\beta}\, c_{n,\alpha}^\dagger c_{n+1,\beta} + \text{h.c.},
\end{equation}

where $t_{\alpha\beta}$ are the intracell hoppings and $\gamma_{\alpha\beta}$ are the intercell
hoppings.

The symmetry that diagonalizes (2.11) is a slightly richer version of a pure translation. It shifts
the unit-cell index $n\to n+1$ \emph{and} applies a unitary rotation among the orbitals within each
cell. The corresponding eigenstates are

\begin{equation*}\Psi_{\varphi,\alpha}^\dagger \sim \sum_{n,\beta} e^{in\varphi}\,U_{\alpha\beta}\,c_{n,\beta}^\dagger,\end{equation*}

for some unitary $U$. We put this ansatz into (2.11) and repeat the Fourier argument of Section 2.5
for each orbital, and we find that the required $U$ is exactly the matrix that diagonalizes the
\textbf{Bloch Hamiltonian}

\begin{equation}H(\varphi) = t + \gamma\, e^{i\varphi} + \gamma^\dagger e^{-i\varphi},\end{equation}

which is a matrix whose size is the number of orbitals per unit cell. Each entry is labelled by the
pair of orbitals $(\alpha,\beta)$ that it couples. The intracell hoppings $t_{\alpha\beta}$ enter this
entry directly, with no phase, while the intercell hoppings $\gamma_{\alpha\beta}$ enter multiplied by
the phase $e^{\pm i\varphi}$ that comes from the symmetry eigenvalue. This is the general method that
we will use many times. First we count the orbitals per cell, which gives the size of the matrix.
Then we put each hopping in the entry of its pair of orbitals, we multiply the intercell ones by
$e^{\pm i\varphi}$, and we diagonalize. Note that an intracell hopping between \emph{different}
orbitals goes off the diagonal, as the example below shows.

A concrete example makes this clear, and it will be important again soon. We take a chain with two
orbitals ($A,B$), with a strong intracell coupling of magnitude $1$ between $A$ and $B$, and a weaker
intercell coupling $\eta$ between $B$ in one cell and $A$ in the next cell. The Bloch Hamiltonian
(2.12) is then purely off-diagonal,

\begin{equation}H(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1+\eta\,e^{-i\varphi} \\ 1+\eta\,e^{i\varphi} & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \epsilon_\pm(\varphi) = \pm\left|1+\eta\,e^{-i\varphi}\right|.\end{equation}

This chain with alternating hoppings should be remembered by its name, since it is the Hamiltonian of
Su-Schrieffer-Heeger type that appears again, now with a topological aspect, in the topology lecture
right after this one. Figure~\ref{fig:s02:recipe} shows this chain as the chain of (2.8) described
with a unit cell of two sites, so that its single band folds into the two bands of (2.13), and it
also shows how a gap opens at the zone edge when $\eta$ moves away from $1$.

\section{Higher-dimensional band structures}

A two-dimensional lattice has two independent translations that commute with each other, $T_x$ and
$T_y$, so Bloch eigenstates have two independent phases, $\varphi_x$ and $\varphi_y$, one for each
direction. These phases are related to the crystal momentum along the two lattice vectors by the same
Taylor-expansion argument as in (2.10), applied once for each direction. The space of allowed pairs
$(\varphi_x,\varphi_y)$ is the \textbf{Brillouin zone}. Since $\varphi=0$ and $\varphi=2\pi$ give the
same translation eigenvalue in both directions, the Brillouin zone is not an open square but a
periodic one. When we identify the left and right edges, and the top and bottom edges, it becomes a
torus, in the same way as it is a circle in the one-dimensional case of Section 2.5. This compactness
of the Brillouin zone will be very important when topological invariants appear in the next session.

When we apply the Fourier method of Section 2.5 once for each spatial direction, we obtain for a
square lattice with a single orbital and nearest-neighbour hopping $t$, with the same sign
convention as in (2.5),

\begin{equation}\epsilon(\varphi_x,\varphi_y) = -2t\left(\cos\varphi_x + \cos\varphi_y\right),\end{equation}

and, when we apply the same argument a third time, for a cubic lattice with a single orbital,

\begin{equation}\epsilon(\varphi_x,\varphi_y,\varphi_z) = -2t\left(\cos\varphi_x+\cos\varphi_y+\cos\varphi_z\right).
\end{equation}

The same method extends in a systematic way to four, five or more dimensions, since nothing in the
argument depends on being in three-dimensional space. Equations (2.14) and (2.15) come from the
slides and the transcript, and there is no counterpart for them in \texttt{session2.ipynb}. The
two-dimensional example of the notebook goes directly to the honeycomb lattice of Section 2.11 and
does not treat the plain square lattice, and there is no section on the three-dimensional cubic
lattice. If you want to check (2.14) or (2.15) numerically, the same construction that we use for the
honeycomb lattice in the box below can be extended directly, with a square or cubic Bravais lattice
with a single orbital instead.

Before we continue, let us say explicitly that everything above applies far beyond electrons. The same Bloch-Hamiltonian method diagonalizes phonon
bands, magnon bands and the bands of Bogoliubov-de Gennes quasiparticles in a superconductor. It
applies in the same way to artificial lattices that are engineered and have no relation to real
crystals, such as cold atoms trapped in optical lattices. We will focus on electronic bands for
concreteness, but this generality is the reason why the same method appears again for magnons, and
for Bogoliubov quasiparticles, later in the course. The physics that single-particle band theory
gives in this way is very rich. It includes Dirac fermions with zero Dirac mass, Weyl semimetals,
which are Dirac points generalized to higher dimensions, quantum spin Hall insulators, Chern
insulators, crystalline topological phases and topological superconductors. All of this is built only
on the Bloch Hamiltonian $H(\mathbf{k})$ that we constructed here.

\section{Properties of an electronic dispersion}

Once we have a dispersion $E(\mathbf{k})$, from (2.8), (2.13), (2.14) or from a more complicated
model, several quantities that we derive from it have a direct physical meaning.

The \textbf{density of states},

\begin{equation}g(\omega) = \int_{\text{BZ}} \frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\; \delta\!\left(\omega-E(\mathbf{k})\right),
\end{equation}

counts how many states there are at energy $\omega$. It controls how strongly a material responds to
interactions. A large or divergent density of states means that even a weak interaction can drive an
instability toward magnetism, a charge density wave or superconductivity, which are the three cases
of the last session. A vanishing density of states means that interactions can hardly have any effect,
however strong they are.

The \textbf{group velocity},

\begin{equation}v(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_{\mathbf{k}} E(\mathbf{k}),\end{equation}

is the local slope of the dispersion. Where $v(\mathbf{k})=0$, electrons at that $\mathbf{k}$ do not
move in practice. Where $v(\mathbf{k})$ is large, they propagate quickly through the lattice.

The \textbf{effective mass},

\begin{equation}\left(\frac{1}{m^\ast}\right)_{ij} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\,
\frac{\partial^2 E(\mathbf{k})}{\partial k_i\,\partial k_j},\end{equation}

is the curvature of the dispersion, and it is the mass that is measured in essentially every
transport or cyclotron-resonance experiment, since the bare electron mass cannot be observed directly
inside a solid. Depending on the band, $m^\ast$ can go from nearly zero to very large values. For a
Dirac dispersion, as we will see soon, it is formally not defined at the point where the bands touch.

Finally, the \textbf{Fermi surface} is the set of $\mathbf{k}$-points that have the same energy as
the highest occupied state. Its shape controls which electronic instabilities are favoured and how a
material responds to an external magnetic field, and it is one of the quantities that ARPES
(Section 2.12) can map directly.

\section{Three types of dispersion, which are parabolic, Dirac and flat}

Real dispersions are complicated functions of $\mathbf{k}$, but almost every band structure in this
course is built locally from three limiting types.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Parabolic bands}, $E\propto k^2$, which is the case similar to free electrons, describe
   most semiconductors and conventional metals. They appear essentially from a Taylor expansion near
   any extremum of a band. We already saw this explicitly near $\varphi=0$ and $\varphi=\pi$ in (2.8),
   with effective masses of opposite sign at the two extrema.
\item \textbf{Dirac (linear) dispersions}, $E\propto|k|$, are realized in graphene and in several
   topological materials, such as quantum spin Hall insulators, Chern insulators and other
   symmetry-protected topological phases. They have finite topological invariants, in the form of a
   Berry phase, and they give rise to a phenomenology that is similar to relativistic physics, even
   though the electrons themselves are not relativistic at all.
\item \textbf{Flat bands}, $E(\mathbf{k})=\text{const}$, may look like the least interesting case,
   but by (2.16) a flat band gives a \emph{divergent} density of states. As a result, even weak
   interactions have a very large effect when a flat band is near the Fermi level. When the flatness
   has a nontrivial origin, which is destructive interference in real space and not a hopping set to
   zero, the flat band can also have a finite Chern number, which is central to the quantum Hall effect
   and to moiré materials.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:s02:archetypes} shows the three types next to each other, together with the density
of states of each one, which decides whether interactions can have an effect on them.

\section{The Dirac cone of the honeycomb lattice of graphene, and flat-band lattices}

The honeycomb lattice of graphene has two orbitals per unit cell, one on each of two triangular
sublattices, which are usually called $A$ and $B$, and which sit on an underlying triangular Bravais
lattice. Nearest-neighbour hopping only connects $A$ with $B$, and never $A$ with $A$ or $B$ with
$B$, so by the method of Section 2.7 the Bloch Hamiltonian (2.12) is purely off-diagonal,

\begin{equation}H(\mathbf{k}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & f(\mathbf{k}) \\ f^\ast(\mathbf{k}) & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad f(\mathbf{k}) = t\sum_{j=1}^{3} e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_j},\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}_j$, $j=1,2,3$, are the three vectors that connect an $A$ site with its
nearest $B$ neighbours. When we diagonalize (2.19) we obtain $E_\pm(\mathbf{k})=\pm|f(\mathbf{k})|$.
The function $f(\mathbf{k})$ vanishes at two inequivalent points of the Brillouin zone, which are
usually called $K$ and $K'$, since there the three phases in the sum cancel exactly. So both bands touch at exactly zero energy at $K$ and $K'$. It is useful to see this cancellation
explicitly. Figure~\ref{fig:s02:dirac} shows the three phase factors at $\mathbf{K}$ as three unit
vectors at $120^\circ$ from each other, which close a triangle and so sum to zero.

\input{figures/s02f04_dirac}

We expand $f(\mathbf{k})$ to linear order around $K$ and write $\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{q}$ for
small $\mathbf{q}$. The off-diagonal entry then becomes a linear function of $\mathbf{q}$, with one
linear term for its real part and one for its imaginary part, and (2.19) reduces to the standard
two-dimensional \textbf{Dirac Hamiltonian},

\begin{equation}H(\mathbf{q}) \approx \hbar v_F\left(q_x\sigma_x + q_y\sigma_y\right), \qquad
v_F = \frac{3ta}{2\hbar},\end{equation}

where $\sigma_x,\sigma_y$ are the Pauli matrices that act on the $A/B$ sublattice space and $a$ is
the nearest-neighbour bond length. When we diagonalize (2.20) we obtain

\begin{equation}E_\pm(\mathbf{q}) = \pm \hbar v_F |\mathbf{q}|,\end{equation}

which is a strictly linear dispersion with the shape of a cone around $K$, and the same calculation
gives the same result around $K'$. This is what is called the \textbf{Dirac cone}. Note that the
curvature of the cone along the radial direction vanishes, so (2.18) gives an infinite effective
mass in this direction, and at the tip of the cone the effective mass is not defined at all. When
we say that the electrons of graphene are ``massless'', which is the phenomenology for which graphene
is famous, we refer to the Dirac mass term, which is the gap, and not to the curvature of the band. This is the low-energy physics of every material with a honeycomb lattice, and of
graphene in the first place. There is much more structure at $K$ and $K'$, namely a Berry phase and
with it a topological classification, which we leave for the topology lecture right after this one.

Build the two-dimensional honeycomb Bloch Hamiltonian (2.19) directly and diagonalize it across the
Brillouin zone. Zoom in on $K$ and $K'$ and check numerically that the dispersion there is really
linear, which means that it agrees with (2.21) once you are close enough to the Dirac point.
\end{notebookbox}

Two standard lattices realize truly flat bands only from their geometry in real space, and not from
a fine-tuning of hopping parameters to zero. The \textbf{Lieb lattice} is built from a square lattice
where one site out of every four is removed in a regular pattern. Its dispersion has two dispersive
bands, which come from the bands of the square lattice (2.14), and in addition one exactly flat band
at zero energy. Near the corner $M$ of the Brillouin zone the two dispersive bands form a linear,
Dirac-like cone, and the flat band goes through the tip of this cone. The \textbf{kagome lattice} is built instead from a triangular lattice where one site out of
every four is removed. Its dispersion is closer to the one of the honeycomb lattice, with linear
crossings similar to Dirac cones, and in addition it has a flat band at the edge of the spectrum.
With the sign convention of (2.5) this flat band is at the top, at $\epsilon=2t$, and it would be at
the bottom for the opposite sign of $t$. In both cases the flat band
comes from destructive interference between hopping paths in real space. This is a purely geometric
effect and not an effect of interactions, and the physics of strong correlations only enters once
electrons are placed into these bands, which are already flat. Figure~\ref{fig:s02:flatbands} shows
both lattices, both dispersions, and the localized state where the hopping paths cancel.

\section{Measuring band structures with ARPES}

Once we have all this formalism, a natural question is whether any of it can be observed. The energy
eigenvalue $E(\mathbf{k})$ is clearly physical, but the quasi-momentum $\mathbf{k}$ itself was
introduced as a symmetry eigenvalue in Section 2.6. So we ask whether we can measure it directly, and
whether we can measure the \emph{relation} between the two.

The answer is yes, with \textbf{angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)}. The idea is
simple in principle. We send photons of known energy and momentum onto a material, and each photon
can remove an electron from the material completely, which is the photoelectric effect. Conservation
of momentum and energy relates the momentum and kinetic energy of the electron, which are both
measured directly outside the material, to its momentum and energy when it was still inside. So when
we scan the emission angle and the kinetic energy of the emitted electrons, we map $E(\mathbf{k})$
directly. This is how the band structure and the Fermi surface of graphene are measured in practice,
and the measured Dirac cone agrees well with the tight-binding derivation of Section 2.11. This
material is only on the slides and has no counterpart in the notebook. The actual ARPES images of the
measured band structure and Fermi surface of graphene are shown on the slides, and there is no way to
reproduce a real photoemission experiment in a tight-binding notebook. The description above gives the
physical principle, but to see the measured data itself one needs the figure.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Symmetry as a tool to simplify.} When the ground state shares the symmetry of the
  Hamiltonian, we can use a symmetry operator that commutes with it, $[H,S]=0$ in (2.3), and find
  the eigenbasis of $H$ by finding the eigenbasis of $S$ instead. We worked this out explicitly for
  rings of three and five sites in Section 2.4.
\item \textbf{Bloch's theorem} is this same idea applied to translational symmetry on an infinite
  lattice. Eigenstates have a definite phase $\varphi$ under translation, which is the quasi-momentum
  of (2.10), and this turns one infinite-dimensional diagonalization into a continuous family of
  finite ones, given by the \textbf{Bloch Hamiltonian} $H(\varphi)$.
\item We derived the band structure of the one-dimensional chain explicitly,
  $\epsilon_\varphi=-2t\cos\varphi$ in (2.8). We generalized the same method to second-neighbour
  hopping (2.9), to several orbitals per unit cell with the matrix Bloch Hamiltonian
  $H(\varphi)=t+\gamma e^{i\varphi}+\text{h.c.}$ in (2.12)-(2.13), and to higher spatial dimensions,
  (2.14) and (2.15).
\item A dispersion $E(\mathbf{k})$ directly determines the measurable properties of a material, which
  are the density of states (2.16), the group velocity (2.17), the effective mass (2.18) and the Fermi
  surface. Every real dispersion is built locally from three types, which are parabolic, Dirac and
  flat.
\item The \textbf{Dirac cone} of the honeycomb lattice of graphene, $H(\mathbf{q})\approx\hbar v_F(q_x\sigma_x+
  q_y\sigma_y)$ with $E_\pm=\pm\hbar v_F|\mathbf{q}|$ in (2.20)-(2.21), is the most important example
  of this session. We derived it directly from the vanishing of the off-diagonal Bloch Hamiltonian
  (2.19) at the points $K,K'$. The Lieb and kagome lattices realize flat bands with the same kind of
  interference in real space, and with no fine-tuning.
\item All of this band-structure method generalizes directly to phonons, magnons and Bogoliubov-de
  Gennes quasiparticles, and not only to electrons, and it is tested against real materials in
  experiments with ARPES.
\end{itemize}

The main message of this session is the following. The spectra of periodic systems are computed with
band-structure theory, by choosing the common eigenbasis of the symmetry operator and the Hamiltonian,
and the resulting dispersion relation $E(\mathbf{k})$ alone determines many of the electronic
properties of a material, before interactions are included again. The Dirac cone that we derived in
Section 2.11 is not only a fact about graphene. It is the object that the next session takes up, where
we ask what happens when it is gapped open, and this is where topological band structure, Chern
numbers and the SSH model, which we already saw in the two-orbital chain of (2.13), enter the course
in detail. The flat bands are also not the end of the topic. The kagome and Lieb constructions of
this session lead directly to the flat bands of the Landau levels, which the quantum Hall session
builds several sessions from now.
