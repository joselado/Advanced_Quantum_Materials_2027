\chapter{Second Quantization, Mean-Field, and Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking}

This is the first session of the course, so there is no previous cliffhanger to pick up. Instead
let us open with the question the whole course is organized around: how do we get from a single
atom, an object we understand rather well, to a \emph{quantum material}, where electrons moving
together produce phenomena no individual atom could ever show on its own? Answering that question
is going to require a new piece of mathematical language, second quantization, and a first tool
for actually using it, mean-field theory. By the end of today we will have used that tool three
times, to build a charge density wave, a magnet, and a superconductor out of the very same starting
Hamiltonian, just by changing the sign and the geometry of one interaction term. That three-way
split (broken translational symmetry, broken time-reversal symmetry, broken gauge symmetry) is the
skeleton the rest of the course hangs on, so it is worth having it clearly in view from today. It is
also worth flagging early that today is the \emph{only} place in the course where the general logic of
spontaneous symmetry breaking gets developed at length: next session moves straight into symmetry
and band structure without revisiting it, and magnetism and superconductivity only get their full,
dedicated treatments much later, in sessions 6 and 5 respectively. What we build here is meant to
carry that conceptual load on its own.

\section{Why quantum materials, and why now}

Let us start with a bit of perspective on why this subject matters before we get into the
mathematics. Every age of human technology has been named after the material that defined it:
the stone age, the copper age, and, very recently, the silicon age, which gave us the entire
information revolution we are currently living through. If we look ahead, the next age is
plausibly a \emph{quantum} age, one in which our technology relies directly on harnessing quantum
mechanical behavior at microscopic scales rather than working around it. Quantum materials are the
physical platform for that shift: they are what actually realizes quantum matter in the lab.

By the end of today, concretely, we should be able to describe what a quantum material is, explain
why quantum mechanics (rather than classical physics) is needed to understand it, work with the
basics of second quantization, and rationalize why mean-field theory gives rise to spontaneous
symmetry breaking.

This is not just a rhetorical framing. Quantum materials already sit behind technologies we use
today: superconductors make MRI machines possible by generating the very large magnetic fields
nuclear magnetic resonance needs, and semiconductors are the basis of the solar cells much of our
renewable energy strategy depends on. Looking forward, essentially every serious quantum
computing platform, whether built from superconductors, semiconductors, or combinations of the
two, is a quantum materials problem at its core. It is also worth noting, briefly, that this
connects to several of the UN Sustainable Development Goals: quantum computing to goals 8 and 9
(economic growth, industry and innovation), solar energy and low-consumption superconducting or
topological electronics to goals 7 and 13 (clean energy, climate action), and the inherently
international, cross-disciplinary nature of the field to goal 17 (partnerships). None of this is
mathematics, but it is worth remembering why we are doing the mathematics.

Within the course, we will meet two kinds of examples that make a useful contrast. Semiconductors
are, in a sense, the easy case: their properties follow from single-particle physics, which you
have likely already seen in an earlier materials course. Superconductors are the harder case:
understanding them requires accounting for how electrons interact with each other collectively,
which is exactly the many-body machinery we start building today.

\section{A tour of the quantum materials we will meet}

It is worth previewing, briefly, the range of materials this formalism is eventually going to let
us understand, so that today's abstract tools have concrete targets to attach to later.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Insulators}, conventional ones like magnesium oxide, sodium chloride, or boron nitride, where
  a finite band gap simply prevents electrons from propagating. \emph{Topological} insulators look
  identical in the bulk but behave completely differently at the edge, a distinction we will need
  a dedicated session to explain.
\item \textbf{Semiconductors} (gallium nitride, silicon, gallium arsenide), which share the single-particle
  description of insulators but are lightly doped, so they carry free carriers that do the actual
  work in electronics and solar cells.
\item \textbf{Magnets}: ferromagnets, antiferromagnets, and spin spirals, all \emph{correlated} materials in the
  sense that they exist only because electrons repel each other via the Coulomb interaction. A
  distinct and more exotic case is the \emph{quantum magnet}, where local moments exist at every site
  but are entangled with each other so thoroughly that the material has no net magnetization at
  all, microscopically, and every site forms a singlet with its neighbors. We will return to these
  at length later in the course.
\item \textbf{Dirac and topological matter}, with graphene, a single honeycomb layer of carbon, as the
  prototype: electrons here propagate with a topologically protected phase (a Berry phase). The
  same family includes quantum spin Hall insulators (helical edge states), Chern insulators
  (chiral edge states), and chiral superconductors (which host Majorana excitations).
\item \textbf{Superconductors}, from conventional, phonon-mediated ones like aluminum, to \emph{correlated}
  superconductors such as the cuprates and pnictides, whose mechanism remains one of the biggest
  open problems in the field.
\item \textbf{Fractional quantum matter}, where the fundamental excitations are literal fractions of an
  electron. The fractional quantum Hall state is the best-established example, observed for decades
  in materials like gallium arsenide; fractional Chern insulators are a newer, more accessible route
  to the same physics, and a promising building block for new quantum devices.
\end{itemize}

\section{Emergent quasiparticles: what putting atoms in a lattice actually buys you}

Here is the puzzle that motivates everything that follows. All of this phenomenology is built from
the same four ingredients, electrons, protons, neutrons, and photons, and a single, isolated atom
shows none of it: no fractionalization, no vortices, no magnons, no spinons. So how does arranging
identical atoms into a lattice produce such a rich zoo of collective behavior? Before we build the
formalism to answer that question properly, it helps to know what we are looking for. A few of the
emergent quasiparticles we will meet along the way:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Phonons}: lattice vibrations, the simplest quasiparticle of all, since they exist even
  classically.
\item \textbf{Magnons}: spin-1 excitations of a magnetically ordered state, a small precession of the local
  moment that propagates energy (and spin) through the material. Notably, magnons can propagate
  even in a Mott insulator, where charge cannot: a magnetic insulator can still carry spin and
  heat.
\item \textbf{Spinons}: spin-1/2, charge-neutral excitations that live in quantum magnets, where
  time-reversal symmetry is \emph{not} broken. A spinon is, in a sense, only the spin half of an
  electron, with the charge half frozen out, an example of electron fractionalization known as
  spin-charge separation.
\item \textbf{Dirac fermions}: massless-looking electrons with a linear dispersion, arising not from
  interactions but from lattice geometry (graphene's honeycomb lattice is the standard example).
\item \textbf{Vortices}: topological defects in a superconductor's phase, themselves dynamical
  quasiparticles, and useful enough that they underlie some of our most precise measurements of
  the electron charge and Planck's constant.
\end{itemize}

The course's own summary slide for this list also shows Majorana fermions (which we will meet again
shortly, tied to chiral superconductors) and anyons alongside these five. Anyons in particular are
not discussed any further in this session, slides or speech; they belong properly to the fractional
quantum Hall material in a later session, and are mentioned here only as a name to recognize.

\section{Setting up the formalism: the Schrödinger equation, two ways}

Every quantum mechanical system is governed by the Schrödinger equation, built from a Hamiltonian
(think of it, for our purposes, as a matrix) acting on a wave function. Given the wave function at
time zero and the Hamiltonian, we can in principle integrate this equation forward and know the
state at any later time. This statement is completely general: it holds whether the wave function
describes one particle or an entire many-body system. The practical difference is enormous,
though. Single-particle problems can always be solved exactly, in every case that matters to us.
Many-body problems generically cannot, and the difficulty grows extremely fast with system size,
as we will see concretely in a moment. Since most of the quantum matter we care about is genuinely
many-body, we need a systematic (if often approximate) way of handling that difficulty, and that
is what today's session builds.

\subsection{First quantization}

When electrons can be treated as independent of one another, we can describe the system with a
single-particle wave function living in an ordinary Hilbert space: this is \emph{first quantization},
the language you already know from a standard quantum mechanics course. It is a remarkably capable
tool, sufficient to describe metals, semiconductors, insulators, and even topological insulators.

\subsection{Second quantization}

First quantization assumes a fixed number of particles, and that assumption fails for a whole
class of quantum materials: any system where electrons are not independent, or, more strikingly,
where the \emph{number} of electrons itself is not fixed. For those systems we need \emph{second
quantization}, a formalism built not around ``how many electrons are present'' but around how
electrons are created and destroyed. It turns out to be essential for superconductors,
superfluids, and correlated materials generally, and it is what makes phenomena like spinon
fractionalization straightforward to describe at all.

\section{A warm-up in first quantization, and the puzzle that breaks it}

Consider the simplest possible single-particle system: a lattice of sites, one level and one
electron each, forming some finite island (a quantum dot, say). With no many-body interactions,
this is an ordinary diagonalization problem, exactly analogous to a particle in a box: a lowest
energy state, a first excited state, and so on. In this language, ``one electron in the ground
level'' and ``one electron in the first excited level'' are simply two different single-particle
states, or superpositions of the two, but in every case there is exactly one electron in the
system, full stop.

Now here is the puzzle. Suppose we want to describe a quantum dot in a coherent superposition of
having zero electrons and having two electrons, each with 50\% probability. We cannot write this
down as an ordinary wave function, because a wave function needs a fixed number of coordinates,
and there is no sensible way to have zero coordinates and two coordinates ``at the same time.'' This
is not a contrived example either: exactly this kind of reasoning, about a fluctuating number of
electrons, is what lets us understand how local magnetic moments couple to each other in real
oxides (the Goodenough-Kanamori rules for predicting ferro- versus antiferromagnetism), and it is
built into the very definition of a superconductor, whose mean-field order parameter conserves
only the \emph{parity} of the electron number, not the number itself. This particular non-particle-
conserving description of superconductors has a name of its own, the Bogoliubov-de Gennes
formalism, that the slides introduce here but that we will only properly use once we reach the
superconductivity session.

\section{Building second quantization: creation and annihilation operators}

To describe systems like this we introduce two operators at every site $i$: the annihilation
operator $c_i$, which destroys an electron at site $i$, and the creation operator $c_i^\dagger$,
which creates one. The Hamiltonian, and all of the system's dynamics, gets written entirely in
terms of these operators.

We define the vacuum (empty) state $|0\rangle$ by

\begin{equation}
c_i|0\rangle = 0 \quad \text{for every site } i.
\end{equation}

A two-site hopping term, an electron able to jump between sites 0 and 1, is then written

\begin{equation}
H_\text{hop} = t\left(c_0^\dagger c_1 + c_1^\dagger c_0\right),
\end{equation}

read as ``destroy an electron at 1, create one at 0'' plus the reverse process. Building up from the
vacuum by applying creation operators gives us every state of the two-site system: $|0\rangle$,
$c_0^\dagger|0\rangle$ (one electron at site 0), $c_1^\dagger|0\rangle$ (one electron at site 1),
and $c_0^\dagger c_1^\dagger|0\rangle$ (both sites occupied).

\section{Enforcing fermionic statistics}

Nothing so far distinguishes fermions from bosons: everything above applies equally to both. The
distinction is imposed through the operator algebra. Fermi statistics requires the many-body wave
function to pick up a minus sign when we exchange two particles, and as a direct consequence, a
given single-particle level can hold zero or one fermion, never two. We enforce this with
anticommutation relations,

\begin{equation}
\{c_i, c_j^\dagger\} = \delta_{ij}, \qquad \{c_i, c_j\} = \{c_i^\dagger, c_j^\dagger\} = 0.
\end{equation}

The second relation is doing the real work: setting $i=j$ makes $(c_i^\dagger)^2 = 0$, which is
exactly the statement that you cannot create two electrons in the same level. It simultaneously
enforces the wave function's antisymmetry, so Pauli exclusion is built into the algebra itself
rather than added by hand.

\section{First versus second quantization: the same problem, viewed two ways}

For a purely single-particle tight-binding Hamiltonian, first and second quantization are, in
practice, the same amount of work. Write the first-quantized Hamiltonian with hopping matrix
elements $t_{ij}$ between orbitals $i,j$; the second-quantized version has the very same $t_{ij}$
multiplying $c_i^\dagger c_j$. Diagonalizing either one uses the same unitary transformation $U$
and produces the same eigenvalues. The only formal difference is that in first quantization the
diagonalizing coefficients form a vector, while in second quantization they become operators; the
physics is identical either way.

\section{Where the two formalisms diverge: the exponential wall}

The gap between the two languages opens up the moment we add genuine many-body interactions, and
it opens up fast. Consider just three sites with a generic quartic (four-operator) interaction
term added to the kinetic Hamiltonian. We are no longer allowed to restrict ourselves to
single-particle states; we need the full Fock space: the empty state, three states with one
electron, three states with two electrons, and one state with all three sites filled, for a total
of $2^3 = 8$ many-body states; figure~\ref{fig:s01:fockspace} draws both sides of the comparison
explicitly.

\input{figures/s01f01_fockspace}

In general, for $L$ sites, the single-particle problem is an $L \times L$ matrix, while the
many-body problem is $2^L \times 2^L$, and the ground-state wave function itself needs $2^L$
coefficients to write down. This is why $L \approx 20$ already puts us near the practical limit of
what can be handled by brute force. The exact rate of growth depends on what kind of site we are
dealing with: spinful fermions give four states per site (empty, up, down, doubly occupied),
spinless fermions give two (relevant once a single spin species is isolated, by strong magnetism
or a strong field), and pure spin sites, which we will need once we get to quantum magnets and
Kondo systems later in the course, also grow exponentially but with a different underlying
algebra.

\section{A first look at eigenstates: finite tight-binding islands}

Before turning to interactions, it is worth pausing on what a purely single-particle Hamiltonian
already gives us, because it is something we can directly compare to experiment. Take a
tight-binding Hamiltonian $H = \sum_{ij} t_{ij}\, c_i^\dagger c_j$ on a honeycomb lattice cut into
a finite island, triangular, hexagonal, or rectangular, and diagonalize it. Plotting the
probability density of a given eigenvector in real space is directly comparable to what a scanning
tunneling microscope measures. Qualitatively, these look exactly like finite-size ``particle in a
box'' states: the lowest-energy state is peaked near the island's center, and higher states develop
progressively more nodes. This is a nice first checkpoint: single-particle diagonalization,
nothing more, already reproduces a recognizable and experimentally measurable structure.

\section{Spontaneous symmetry breaking: why a symmetric Hamiltonian can have an asymmetric ground state}

Before we set up interactions in earnest, it is worth stepping back and asking a more general
question, because everything we do for the rest of the session is really an instance of it.
Physical theories tend to have a lot of symmetry built in: space is symmetric, time is symmetric,
and so on. But reality is not: an object sits in one place and not another, a system evolves
forward and not (usually) backward. So why do symmetries present in the Hamiltonian so often fail
to survive into the ground state?

Let us start with a very down-to-earth example. Imagine sitting down at a table for a meal with
colleagues, bread in front of you on both sides. You have to choose: bread on the left, or bread on
the right. There is, of course, a ``correct'' etiquette answer, but suppose neither of us knows it.
Some people will pick left, others right, and once the choice is made, everyone at the table settles
into one of two equally good arrangements: everybody-left, or everybody-right. The setup you started
from, bread symmetrically placed on both sides, was perfectly symmetric. The outcome, once chosen,
is not. That mismatch, a symmetric problem with a non-symmetric solution, is spontaneous symmetry
breaking in miniature.

The same logic, made rigorous, is exactly what happens in a classical Ising ferromagnet. The
Hamiltonian's set of ground states is symmetric: all spins up is just as good a solution as all
spins down. But take a \emph{very} large system, formally infinitely large, the thermodynamic limit, and
the system has to commit to one of the two: all spins up, or all spins down. Which one you measure
is decided by fluctuations, not by the Hamiltonian, and once decided, the state you observe is no
longer symmetric even though the Hamiltonian that produced it always was. This thermodynamic-limit
requirement is essential: spontaneous symmetry breaking, strictly speaking, is a statement about
infinite systems, and it is a generic feature of many-body interactions and correlations, not
something that shows up for a handful of particles. Figure~\ref{fig:s01:ssb} collects all of this
into one picture: a symmetric energy landscape whose minima are not symmetric, with a barrier
between them that only becomes insurmountable in the thermodynamic limit.

\input{figures/s01f02_ssb}

This gives us a genuinely useful classification scheme: sort states of matter by \emph{which} symmetry
they spontaneously break.

\begin{itemize}
\item Broken \textbf{time-reversal symmetry} gives classical magnets: the magnetization is odd under time
  reversal, so a magnetic ground state is simply not invariant when you run the movie backward.
\item Broken \textbf{rotational (inversion) symmetry} gives ferroelectrics: materials that spontaneously
  develop an electric polarization, the electric analog of a magnetization, breaking the rotational
  symmetry of the underlying crystal.
\item Broken \textbf{gauge symmetry} gives superconductors: the phase of the pairing order parameter (or,
  equivalently, of the creation and annihilation operators) becomes physical once the order sets
  in. Many observable superconducting phenomena, notably the Josephson effect, are direct
  consequences of fluctuations in this broken phase; we will develop that properly in the
  superconductivity session.
\end{itemize}

It is worth being explicit that this symmetry classification, useful as it is, does not capture
everything. Topological materials, topological insulators and fractional Chern insulators among
them, can share \emph{exactly} the same broken (or unbroken) symmetries as an ordinary, trivial material
and yet behave completely differently, because they differ in their topological classification
rather than their symmetry classification. That distinction is the subject of its own dedicated
session later in the course; for now, just keep in mind that ``which symmetry is broken'' is not the
whole story of quantum matter.

Every instance of spontaneous symmetry breaking also comes with its own emergent quasiparticle, the
low-energy fluctuation of the order that formed:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|c|c|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Broken symmetry} & \textbf{Realized in} & \textbf{Quasiparticle} & \textbf{Spin} & \textbf{Charge} & \textbf{Gap} \\
\hline
Continuous translational & Crystals & Phonon & 0 & 0 & Gapless (always) \\
Spin-rotational $SO(3)$ & Magnets & Magnon & 1 & 0 & Gapless or gapped \\
Gauge & Superconductors & Higgs mode & 0 & 0 & Gapped \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Notice that magnons and the Higgs mode both carry zero charge, but very different spin, and that
only the phonon is guaranteed to be gapless in every material; whether a magnon or the Higgs mode
is gapped depends on the details of the electronic structure.

Since we cannot literally run time backward in the lab to check whether a material breaks
time-reversal symmetry, we instead measure quantities that are themselves odd under time reversal.
Two are especially useful: the \textbf{Hall conductivity} (a transverse current generated by a
longitudinal voltage) and the \textbf{magneto-optical Kerr effect} (a different optical reflection for
left- versus right-handed circularly polarized light). A nonzero signal in either one is direct
evidence of broken time-reversal symmetry.

That gives us, finally, a precise, formal definition of a magnet, beyond ``the thing that sticks to
your fridge'': a magnet is a many-body state whose wave function is not invariant under the
time-reversal operator $\hat T$,

\begin{equation}
\hat T|\Psi\rangle = |\Psi\rangle \;\Rightarrow\; \text{not magnetic}, \qquad
\hat T|\Psi\rangle \neq |\Psi\rangle \;\Rightarrow\; \text{magnetic}.
\end{equation}

A bar magnet's north and south poles swap under time reversal (since a magnetic pole is tied to an
orbital angular momentum, and angular momenta flip sign under $\hat T$), so a bar magnet fails the
invariance test in (1.4) and is magnetic by this definition. A plain, non-magnetic stick, by
contrast, looks the same under time reversal and passes the test.

With this general picture in hand, let us now go build it explicitly, three times over.

\section{The general interacting Hamiltonian, and the tool we'll use on it}

We are now ready to state the problem this whole session is building toward. The Hamiltonians we
care about take the generic form

\begin{equation}
H = \sum_{ij} t_{ij}\, c_i^\dagger c_j \;+\; \sum_{ijkl} V_{ijkl}\, c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger c_k c_l,
\end{equation}

a free (single-particle, exactly solvable) part plus a quartic interaction. The interaction itself
can come from plain electrostatic (repulsive) Coulomb repulsion, or from an effective interaction
mediated by some other quasiparticle (phonons, magnons, plasmons), which can render the net
interaction attractive rather than repulsive. We will treat these interactions phenomenologically,
as given effective models, rather than deriving them microscopically from first principles.

Solving (1.5) exactly is generically hopeless, for exactly the exponential reason in Section 1.9.
\textbf{Mean-field theory} is the tool that lets us extract systematic, if approximate, solutions from
it anyway, and it is capable of predicting a wide range of symmetry-broken states. We are going to
work through three of them today, the same three cases classified in Section 1.11:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Broken time-reversal symmetry}, magnetism: run the system's movie backwards and it looks
   different from the original.
\item \textbf{Broken (lattice translational) symmetry}, the charge density wave: a Hamiltonian that starts
   out the same at every site ends up with charge on some sites and not others.
\item \textbf{Broken gauge symmetry}, superconductivity: the system develops an order parameter with a
   physical phase, something the original Hamiltonian's symmetry forbids.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:s01:channels} sets these three branches side by side, together with the equation
each one is developed from and the session it returns in.

Two of today's three cases (magnetism and superconductivity) will come back for a full,
much deeper treatment in their own dedicated sessions later in the course; think of what follows

\section{Worked example 1: the charge density wave}

Take the simplest possible interacting case: a spinless one-dimensional chain, nearest-neighbor
hopping $t$, plus a nearest-neighbor Coulomb repulsion $V$,

\begin{equation}
H = -t\sum_n \left(c_n^\dagger c_{n+1} + \text{h.c.}\right) + V\sum_n c_n^\dagger c_n\, c_{n+1}^\dagger c_{n+1}.
\end{equation}

As written, every site is equivalent by translational symmetry. The quartic term is the
troublesome one, so mean-field theory factorizes it: replace one pair of operators by its
expectation value, leaving the rest untouched. This is, to be clear, an \emph{uncontrolled}
approximation (a more rigorous route exists via a path integral and a saddle-point approximation),
but it is the practical workhorse we will use throughout the course. The result is that the
interaction term becomes a spatially dependent single-particle on-site energy: nothing forces the
expectation value $\langle c_n^\dagger c_n\rangle$ to be the same at every site, so even though the
bare Hamiltonian (1.6) is translationally symmetric, the mean-field solution need not be. That
mismatch, a symmetric Hamiltonian with an asymmetric ground state, \emph{is} the charge density wave,
and it is exactly the same mismatch Section 1.11 described in the abstract.

More rigorously, mean-field theory is a variational search over single-particle product states,
minimizing $\langle H\rangle$ with respect to the bilinear expectation values $\langle
c_i^\dagger c_j\rangle$ that parameterize the mean-field Hamiltonian. Since the Hamiltonian depends
on these expectation values and the expectation values depend on the Hamiltonian, we solve it
self-consistently: guess a Hamiltonian, compute the expectation values it implies, build a new
Hamiltonian from those, and repeat until the process converges. Figure~\ref{fig:s01:mfloop} draws
that loop; every worked example below, and every notebook callout in this chapter, runs it.

\input{figures/s01f05_selfconsistency}

Numerically, this shows up exactly as you would hope: at $V=0$ the spectrum is gapless (a metal);
as $V$ grows, a gap opens in the single-particle spectrum and widens, while the charge imbalance
between even and odd sites grows from zero toward its maximal, fully polarized value.
Figure~\ref{fig:s01:cdw} shows the two limits in real space, alongside the spectrum each one has.

\input{figures/s01f04_cdw}

\section{Worked example 2: magnetism}

Now restore the spin degree of freedom and switch the interaction to an on-site (Hubbard) term
between spin-up and spin-down electrons,

\begin{equation}
H = -t\sum_{n,\sigma}\left(c_{n\sigma}^\dagger c_{n+1,\sigma} + \text{h.c.}\right) + U\sum_n c_{n\uparrow}^\dagger c_{n\uparrow}\, c_{n\downarrow}^\dagger c_{n\downarrow}.
\end{equation}

The sign of $U$ alone decides the physics: $U<0$ (attractive) drives superconductivity, which we
will get to in Section 1.15; $U>0$ (repulsive) drives magnetism, our focus here.

The mean-field decoupling proceeds exactly as before, now applied to $\langle
c_{\uparrow}^\dagger c_\uparrow\rangle$ and $\langle c_\downarrow^\dagger c_\downarrow\rangle$
separately, giving a bilinear Hamiltonian with spin-dependent on-site energies. Define the
magnetization

\begin{equation}
m = \langle c_\uparrow^\dagger c_\uparrow\rangle - \langle c_\downarrow^\dagger c_\downarrow\rangle.
\end{equation}

Self-consistency gives $m=0$ automatically for $U<0$, and a finite $m$ for $U>0$: a genuinely
magnetic solution.

It is worth being precise about \emph{which} symmetry this breaks, tying back to the formal definition
in (1.4). Time-reversal symmetry reverses the arrow of time and, with it, every angular momentum in
the system, including spin; on the fermionic operators this means it swaps $c_\uparrow^\dagger
c_\uparrow \leftrightarrow c_\downarrow^\dagger c_\downarrow$. Since $m$ in (1.8) flips sign under
exactly that swap, a nonzero $m$ means the ground state fails the invariance test in (1.4): a
magnet, by definition. More generally this is a breaking of the \emph{continuous} spin-rotational
symmetry, and it is that continuous symmetry breaking which produces the gapless magnon
excitations named in Section 1.11's table; we will study them properly in a later session, and only
need the mean-field ground state itself today.

Numerically, ramping up $U$ opens a \textbf{Mott gap} in the spectrum, growing linearly with $U$ at
large $U$ and associated with the formation of a genuine local moment on every site.

\section{Worked example 3: superconductivity and broken gauge symmetry}

Return to (1.7) with $U<0$. The mean-field decoupling we used above always keeps one creation and
one annihilation operator together, but nothing forces that choice: we are equally free to take an
expectation value between two creation operators (or two annihilation operators),

\begin{equation}
\Delta = \langle c_i c_j\rangle.
\end{equation}

This anomalous, ``pairing'' term looks qualitatively different from anything we have written so far,
but it is a perfectly legitimate mean-field term. Self-consistently, $\Delta$ comes out zero for
repulsive interactions and can become nonzero for attractive ones: precisely the superconducting
order parameter. This worked example is slides-only, with no accompanying notebook section this
session; the pairing (Bogoliubov-de Gennes) machinery needed to solve it numerically is left for the
dedicated superconductivity session.

Why does this order break gauge symmetry? A gauge transformation acts on the operators as

\begin{equation}
c \to c\, e^{i\varphi}, \qquad c^\dagger \to c^\dagger e^{-i\varphi}.
\end{equation}

Any term built from one creation and one annihilation operator, $c^\dagger c$, is invariant under
(1.10), since the two phases cancel. But the pairing term in (1.9) is built from \emph{two} annihilation
operators (or two creation operators), so it picks up a phase $e^{2i\varphi}$ (or $e^{-2i\varphi}$)
under the very same transformation, and does not cancel. A superconductor with $\Delta \neq 0$
therefore spontaneously breaks the Hamiltonian's gauge symmetry, exactly the ``broken gauge
symmetry'' entry from Section 1.11's classification. This is tied directly back to Section 1.5's
fluctuating-electron-number puzzle: breaking gauge symmetry and losing a fixed particle number are,
physically, the same statement. A mean-field superconductor genuinely does not have a fixed number
of electrons, only a fixed parity.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item Quantum materials realize an enormous range of phenomena (topological order, fractionalization,
  correlated superconductivity) that no single atom shows on its own; understanding how they
  emerge from electrons in a lattice is this course's central question.
\item \textbf{Second quantization} is the language that lets us treat both fixed-particle-number
  (single-particle) and fluctuating-particle-number (many-body) systems on the same footing, built
  from creation/annihilation operators satisfying the anticommutation relations (1.3).
\item The many-body Hilbert space grows as $2^L$ (Section 1.9), which is why interacting problems need
  a systematic approximation scheme rather than brute-force diagonalization.
\item \textbf{Spontaneous symmetry breaking} is the general phenomenon of a symmetric Hamiltonian producing
  an asymmetric ground state in the thermodynamic limit; states of matter can be classified by which
  symmetry they break (time-reversal for magnets, rotational for ferroelectrics, gauge for
  superconductors), each with its own emergent quasiparticle, and a magnet has the precise, formal
  definition given in (1.4). Topological materials are a reminder that this classification is not
  exhaustive.
\item \textbf{Mean-field theory} supplies the practical machinery: factorize the interacting Hamiltonian
  (1.5) by taking an expectation value over part of the quartic term, then solve the resulting
  single-particle problem self-consistently.
\item The same recipe, applied to three different interaction channels, produces three different
  symmetry-broken states: a charge density wave via (1.6) (broken translational symmetry), a
  magnet via (1.7)-(1.8) (broken time-reversal symmetry), and a superconductor via (1.9)-(1.10)
  (broken gauge symmetry). Magnetism and superconductivity each get a full session of their own
  later in the course; today's versions are the first pass.
\end{itemize}

Next session, we leave interactions aside for a while and go back to the single-particle side of
today's Hamiltonian, to see how \emph{symmetry} (translational symmetry, specifically) lets us
diagonalize it exactly, no approximation required, via Bloch's theorem. The general
symmetry-breaking framework built in Section 1.11 will not be revisited there, so it is worth
carrying forward from here rather than expecting a recap.
