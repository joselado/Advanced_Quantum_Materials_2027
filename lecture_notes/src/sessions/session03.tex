\chapter{Topological Band Structure Theory}

In the last session we ended with the most important feature of the honeycomb lattice. Close to the
corners of its Brillouin zone, the tight-binding band structure is not parabolic anymore and becomes
linear, which is the Dirac cone. This is one of the three types of dispersion that we chose to
organize the rest of the course, which are parabolic, Dirac and flat. We also saw that this is not
only a convenient theoretical picture, since ARPES measures this linear dispersion directly in real
graphene. The question for this whole session is what happens if we open a gap at this Dirac point.
A Dirac cone with a gap turns a metal into an insulator, but this insulator is not always an ordinary
one. Depending on \emph{how} the gap opens, the insulator can have a hidden invariant, which is called
a topological invariant. This invariant does not appear anywhere in the bulk spectrum, but it forces
the material to have conducting states at its edge, and no local disorder can remove these states.
To reach this statement in an exact way we need several steps. First, we need linear response theory
as a tool. Then we need the Hall conductivity, which is the observable where the invariant first
appears, and the Chern number, which is the invariant itself. Finally, we need the general
bulk-boundary correspondence, which connects the invariant to the physics of the edge. We will build
all of this with the quantum Hall state, which is the simplest example. Then we will use the same
tools for a purely one-dimensional model, the SSH chain. At the end, in Section 3.10, we will come
back to the Dirac cone of the honeycomb lattice, open a gap in it, and see a real Chern insulator
appear without any magnetic field.

\section{What topological materials are}

Before we go into the formalism, it is useful to have a rough classification in mind, so that the
abstract tools that we are going to build have concrete targets. Topological materials are systems
with an excitation gap in the bulk that at the same time have protected excitations at their surface
or edge. This combination of a gapped bulk and a gapless boundary will appear again and again in this
session, and it is the defining feature of these materials. The point is not that the edge happens to
have low-energy states. The point is that the topology of the bulk \emph{forces} them to be there.

Within this class, we will see the following systems in this session and in the next one.

\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{quantum Hall effect}, which appears in two-dimensional electron gases and in graphene
  under a strong perpendicular magnetic field. It is the simplest topological state, and it is our
  starting point.
\item The \textbf{quantum anomalous Hall effect}, which is the same physics without any external
  magnetic field, and which we will construct explicitly in Section 3.10.
\item \textbf{Quantum spin Hall insulators}, which are in practice two copies of a quantum Hall state
  related by time reversal and put together, as we will see in Section 3.11.
\item \textbf{Topological superconductors}, whose surface excitations are Majorana modes. They are the
  basis of one route to topological quantum computing, and we will come back to them in detail in
  session 5.
\item \textbf{Topological semimetals}, including Weyl semimetals, whose low-energy quasiparticles are
  Weyl fermions.
\item \textbf{Topological Kondo insulators}, which are the example given in the lecture this year of
  topology that survives in a strongly correlated many-body system, ``and many others that do not fit
  on this slide.''
\end{itemize}

This material appears only on the slides and has no counterpart in the notebook. For each entry, the
slides name a specific material realization, which is graphene for the quantum Hall effect, Cr-doped
(Bi,Sb)$_2$Te$_3$ for the quantum anomalous Hall effect, HgTe/CdTe quantum wells for the quantum
spin Hall effect, Fe chains on Pb for a topological superconductor, TaAs for a Weyl semimetal, and SmB$_6$ for
the many-body example. These were shown but not explained in detail in the lecture, and the notebook
of the course does not simulate any of these specific compounds. The many-body example on the
classification slide is the topological Kondo insulator, with SmB$_6$ as the material realization.
What we will simulate, with the models that we build in this session, are minimal tight-binding
versions of the first three items of the list above.

\section{Linear response theory and how a measurement reads the wavefunctions}

To see why a bulk band structure can have an invariant at all, we need a tool that connects the
wavefunctions of a Hamiltonian to something that we can measure. This tool is linear response
theory, and the idea behind it is very general. We perturb the system at a position $x$ and time
$t$, and then we measure an observable, such as the charge, the current, the magnetization or the
heat, at another position $x'$ and time $t'$. Linear response theory tells us that this measured
response depends only on the electronic structure of the unperturbed material.

Before we specialize to the Hall conductivity, let us look at two examples. Let us put an impurity in
a two-dimensional metal and look at how the electron density, or the local density of states, is
modulated around it. Linear response theory shows that this modulation pattern, which is called the
Friedel oscillation, directly contains the Fermi wavelength of the electron gas, and this is what
scanning tunneling microscopy uses in practice. In a similar way, when we scatter neutrons on a
magnetic material, the magnetic moment of the neutron couples to the local moments in the sample. The
resulting scattering pattern gives both the magnetic structure and its excitations, in particular
magnons. Both examples are illustrations that appear only on the slides, in the sense that the
figures of the Friedel oscillation and of neutron scattering were shown on screen without a full
explanation. However, the physics of both examples was explained in the lecture, and it is useful to
keep it in mind as the general result of the formalism that we build next.

Formally, we start from an unperturbed Hamiltonian $H_0$ and add a perturbation controlled by a
dimensionless parameter $\lambda$,

\begin{equation*}
H(\lambda) = H_0 + \lambda V,
\end{equation*}

so that $\lambda = 0$ gives the original system and $\lambda = 1$ is the fully perturbed one. Any
observable, such as the energy, the density, the magnetic moment, the current, or the wavefunctions
themselves, can then be expanded in a Taylor series in $\lambda$. The derivative of an observable with
respect to $\lambda$ at $\lambda = 0$ is what we call the response of the system, and in the case of
a magnetic perturbation it is called the susceptibility. Since every observable can be built from
the wavefunctions, the most useful object to expand is the ground-state wavefunction itself. For a
state $|n\rangle$ of the unperturbed Hamiltonian $H_0$ with energy $\varepsilon_n$, standard
first-order perturbation theory gives

\begin{equation}
|n\rangle_\lambda = |n\rangle + \lambda \sum_{m \neq n} \alpha_m\, |m\rangle + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^2),
\qquad \alpha_m = \frac{\langle m|V|n\rangle}{\varepsilon_n - \varepsilon_m}.
\end{equation}

Everything that we do in the rest of this session, which is the Hall conductivity, the Berry
curvature and the Chern number, comes directly from putting (3.1) into the definition of some
observable.

\section{Where perturbation theory breaks down}

Before we use (3.1), we should say when it fails, because several of the most important phenomena in
this course appear in this failure. The expansion (3.1) assumes that we are in the \emph{perturbative
regime}, where a small change in the input, which is the strength of the perturbation, gives a small
change in the output. This is not always the case. We name three cases now, because we will see each
of them in detail later in the course.

\begin{itemize}
\item An impurity in a metal gives rise to the \textbf{orthogonality catastrophe}. The many-body
  ground state after the perturbation becomes exactly orthogonal to the original ground state, even
  for a very small perturbation, so no Taylor expansion of finite order in $\lambda$ can connect the
  two.
\item A quantum spin, which is a local magnetic moment, coupled to a metal gives rise to the
  \textbf{Kondo effect}. The relevant energy scale, which is the Kondo temperature, is a
  non-perturbative function of the coupling constant, and no order of perturbation theory in this
  coupling gives it. We will study the Kondo lattice model in detail in session 8, and here we only
  mention it as a first sign that ordinary perturbation theory has a strict limit.
\item \textbf{Superconductivity} is also non-perturbative, since the BCS gap does not appear at any
  finite order in the interaction strength if we start from the normal state. We will work this out
  in detail in session 5.
\end{itemize}

In this lecture, and for every model that we build in this session, we stay inside the regime where
(3.1) holds. For now, it is enough to know that this is not always the case.

\section{The Hall conductivity from linear response}

Now we apply (3.1) to the measurement that will bring us to topology, which is the transverse
conductivity, also called the Hall conductivity. We take a two-dimensional material, apply a voltage
in the $y$ direction, and ask what current flows in the $x$ direction. In momentum space, a voltage in
$y$ enters as a perturbation proportional to $\partial H/\partial k_y$, since it shifts $k_y$ through
the Peierls substitution. The current operator in the $x$ direction is, up to the electron charge,
the group-velocity operator $\partial H/\partial k_x$. Both are the kind of matrix element that
appears in (3.1).

When we put (3.1) into the definition of the Hall conductivity, we obtain an expression with two
velocity operators and an energy denominator, which is the standard Kubo formula for a filled band
$n$ and empty bands $m$,

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{xy} \;\propto\; \sum_{n\ \text{filled}} \sum_{m\ \text{empty}} \int_{\text{BZ}}
\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}\;
\frac{\langle n|\partial_{k_x}H|m\rangle\langle m|\partial_{k_y}H|n\rangle - (x\leftrightarrow y)}
{(\varepsilon_n - \varepsilon_m)^2}.
\end{equation}

At this point the expression still depends explicitly on the band energies through the denominator,
so it seems that it should depend on all the details of the band structure. This is not the case,
and the next section shows why.

\section{The Berry curvature, and why the Hall conductivity is an integer}

We take the derivative of the Schrödinger equation $H(k)|u_n(k)\rangle = \varepsilon_n(k)|u_n(k)\rangle$
with respect to $k_\mu$ and project it onto a different band $\langle u_m(k)|$, with $m \neq n$, which
gives

\begin{equation}
\langle u_m|\partial_{k_\mu} H|u_n\rangle
= \big(\varepsilon_n(k) - \varepsilon_m(k)\big)\,\langle u_m|\partial_{k_\mu} u_n\rangle.
\end{equation}

This identity is the piece that we need, since it replaces a matrix element of $\partial_k H$ by a
matrix element of the $k$-derivative of the wavefunction, divided by the energy difference. When we
use (3.3) to rewrite the numerator and the denominator of (3.2) together, the explicit dependence on
the energies cancels completely. What remains is an object built only from overlaps of wavefunctions,
which is called the \textbf{Berry curvature} of band $n$,

\begin{equation*}
\Omega_n(k) = -2\,\mathrm{Im}\, \langle \partial_{k_x} u_n(k)|\partial_{k_y} u_n(k)\rangle,
\end{equation*}

so that the Hall conductivity of a set of filled bands is

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{xy} = \frac{e^2}{h}\, C, \qquad
C = \sum_{n\ \text{filled}} \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{BZ}} \Omega_n(k)\, d^2k.
\end{equation}

This is the central result of the session, and there are two important points about (3.4). First,
$\sigma_{xy}$ depends only on how the wavefunctions change across the Brillouin zone, and not on the
energies. We can deform the band structure in any way, and as long as the wavefunctions do not
change, the Hall conductivity stays the same. Second, and more surprising, for a gapped system, which
is an insulator, $C$ is always an integer, regardless of the number of bands and of the shape of the
dispersion. This is a topological statement. The Brillouin zone of a two-dimensional system is a
compact manifold, which is a torus since opposite edges of the zone are identified, and the integral
of a curvature over a closed surface always gives an integer. The same happens for the Gaussian
curvature of an ordinary closed surface in differential geometry. This integer $C$ is the
\textbf{Chern number}, and (3.4) is the first place in the course where a measurable quantity, the
Hall conductivity, is fixed by a topological invariant.

\section{Landau levels and chiral edge states in the quantum Hall state}

The Chern number appears in its simplest form in the quantum Hall state itself, which is a
two-dimensional electron gas in a strong perpendicular magnetic field. The resulting band structure is
the flat-band type of dispersion from session 2, which is a set of Landau levels without dispersion,
and each Landau level has Chern number $+1$. If we fill the first Landau level, the total Chern
number is $C=1$. If we fill the first two, we get $C=2$, and so on, so that $\sigma_{xy} = C\, e^2/h$
goes through successive integer plateaus as the chemical potential goes up through successive Landau
levels. This is the integer quantum Hall effect, and in session 4 we will work out its full
phenomenology, including the fractional case. In this session we only need to count the Chern
numbers.

This raises a puzzle. We have an insulator, with a gap between Landau levels, but $\sigma_{xy}$ is a
\emph{conductivity}, which means that some electron has to move somewhere. The answer is that any real
sample has an edge, and at this edge the bulk gap closes. Gapless edge states appear, and they carry
the current that (3.4) predicts. These edge states have another key property, which is that on a
given edge the states propagate in only \emph{one} direction. The top edge of a sample carries current
in one direction only, and the bottom edge carries it in the opposite direction only. A mode that
propagates in a single direction is called \textbf{chiral}, and chirality is what protects the quantum
Hall state against disorder. An electron in a chiral edge mode cannot backscatter, since there is no
state that propagates in the opposite direction at the same edge and the same energy. Without a
backscattering channel there is no resistance in this channel. Figure~\ref{fig:s03:chiraledge} shows
both parts of this picture, which are the closed bulk orbits that carry no current and the skipping
orbits that carry all of it, and it also shows what happens when an impurity is put in their way.

\input{figures/s03f01_chiraledge}

Let us look at a concrete model. We take a tight-binding square lattice with a perpendicular magnetic
field and cut it into a ribbon of finite width, which has translational invariance along the ribbon
but is finite across it. When we diagonalize this ribbon, we find the structure described above.
There are flat bulk bands similar to Landau levels, and there are dispersive edge branches that cross
the gap, with one branch confined to each edge and propagating in only one direction. If we look at
the low-energy region close to zero energy, the spacing between the flat bands approaches the equal
spacing of a continuum two-dimensional electron gas in a magnetic field, so the tight-binding model
reproduces the standard Landau problem in the appropriate limit. This computed band structure, with

\section{What topology means for a Hamiltonian, and the bulk-boundary correspondence}

We have now seen one topological invariant at work. Let us now step back and say what the word
topological means, in the mathematical sense from which the word is taken. Topology classifies
objects by whether they can be smoothly deformed into each other without a drastic operation, such as
cutting, tearing or gluing. Closed surfaces in three dimensions are classified by their number of holes. A
donut and a coffee cup both have one hole and can be continuously deformed into each other, while a
sphere has no hole and cannot be deformed into either of them. Closed curves embedded in three dimensions
have a similar classification by knots, since curves with different knots cannot be smoothly untangled into
each other.

For a Hamiltonian, the equivalent of a deformation is to change the band structure continuously while
the bulk gap stays open. Two Hamiltonians related by such a deformation have the same Chern number,
because $C$ in (3.4) is quantized and so it cannot change continuously. It can only jump, and a jump
requires the gap to close at some point of the deformation. So the Chern number plays the role that
the number of holes plays for a surface. Hamiltonians with the same Chern number are topologically
equivalent, meaning that they can be deformed into each other without closing the gap, and
Hamiltonians with different Chern numbers are not.

This has a direct and strong consequence. Let us build a Hamiltonian that changes in space and is
equal to $H_1$, with Chern number $C_1$, far to the left, and to $H_2$, with Chern number
$C_2 \neq C_1$, far to the right. Since $H_1$ and $H_2$ cannot be deformed into each other without
closing the gap, and the Hamiltonian in between goes continuously from one to the other, the gap
\emph{must} close somewhere in between, for any microscopic construction of the interface. An
interface between two bulk phases with different Chern numbers always has a gapless state, and the
existence of this gapless state depends only on $C_1$ and $C_2$, and not on any detail of the
interface. This is called the \textbf{bulk-boundary correspondence}. The vacuum has $C=0$, since it is
topologically trivial, so a quantum Hall state with $C=1$ always has a gapless edge state at every
boundary with the vacuum. This is the edge physics of Section 3.6, now obtained from a completely
general argument. Figure~\ref{fig:s03:bulkboundary} shows the three steps of this argument in order.

\input{figures/s03f02_bulkboundary}

The same argument explains the robustness that we used before. Let us put a local impurity at the
edge of a quantum Hall sample. This only changes the microscopic details of the interface between the
vacuum and the quantum Hall state. Since the topological excitation of the interface must exist for
any such details, the edge state reorganizes itself around the impurity and is not scattered by it.
This is a general mechanism, which is not special to the quantum Hall case, and it will appear again
for every topological invariant that we see from now on.

\section{Three topological material classes}

With the general tools in place, let us name three classes of topological matter, before we build
explicit models for two of them.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Chern insulators.} They have a bulk Chern number $C \neq 0$ and chiral edge states, and
  time-reversal symmetry is necessarily broken, since we will see in Section 3.10 that time-reversal
  symmetry forces $\sigma_{xy}=0$. Without a backscattering channel there are no electrical losses at
  the edge, which makes them an attractive platform for dissipationless electronics.
\item \textbf{Quantum spin Hall insulators.} They are two copies of a Chern insulator, one for each
  spin, with opposite Chern numbers, so the net Chern number vanishes and time-reversal symmetry is
  preserved. However, the edge states are \textbf{helical}, meaning that spin-up and spin-down
  propagate in opposite directions along the same edge. This is the natural platform for
  dissipationless spin transport, which is spintronics.
\item \textbf{Topological superconductors.} They combine a bulk superconducting gap with gapless
  surface excitations that are their own antiparticle, which are called Majorana excitations. Since
  the corresponding quantum information is stored non-locally, the usual local decoherence channels
  do not act on it directly, and this is the basis of topological quantum computing. We will build
  the minimal model for this case, the Kitaev chain, in session 5, and we will see there that it is
  exactly the same model as the one that we construct next.
\end{itemize}

\section{The SSH model as a minimal one-dimensional topological insulator}

We now build the simplest model that shows this physics explicitly, which is the
Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) chain. It looks too simple to be useful, but we should take it seriously,
also because the simplest model for a topological superconductor, the Kitaev chain, is exactly this
model written in terms of Majorana operators.

We take a one-dimensional lattice with two sites per unit cell, $A$ and $B$, and two hopping
amplitudes. The first one is an intracell hopping $t$, within a unit cell, and the second one is an
intercell hopping fixed to $1$, between neighboring unit cells. Let us first take the extreme limit in
which the intracell hopping dominates, $t \gg 1$, so that the chain is fully dimerized with
alternating bonds. Strong bonds of strength $t$ couple the sites $(0,1)$, $(2,3)$, $(4,5)$, \ldots
and the coupling between $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$, \ldots is negligible. Each dimer is then an isolated
two-level system with bonding and antibonding eigenstates at energies $\pm t$. The spectrum of the
whole chain is this pair of levels repeated once for each dimer, and there is nothing at zero energy.

Now let us take the opposite limit, $t \to 0$, in which the strong bonds are the intercell ones and
the dimer pattern is shifted by one site. We leave the first site uncoupled, so that it is a dangling
site, we couple $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$, \ldots, and we also leave the last site dangling. In the bulk this
looks the same as the previous chain, since it has the same alternating dimer pattern shifted by one
site, but the two ends are qualitatively different, because each end now has a site that is not
coupled at all. When we diagonalize this chain, we obtain the same bonding and antibonding pairs at
$\pm 1$ in the bulk, and also \textbf{two states pinned exactly at zero energy}, one localized at each
end. Figure~\ref{fig:s03:ssh} shows the two chains side by side together with their spectra.

\input{figures/s03f03_ssh}

This is the minimal example of a topological phase. The fully dimerized chain of the first case is the
\textbf{trivial phase}, which is gapped everywhere, including at the ends. The shifted chain of the
second case is the \textbf{topological phase}, which is gapped in the bulk but has gapless modes at
exactly zero energy at the edges. This construction looks very fine-tuned, and it is, since real
chains have hopping between every pair of neighboring sites. The important part is what survives when
we remove the fine-tuning. If we turn on a small coupling $t$ between the sites at the two ends that
were decoupled, the zero modes are \emph{not} removed, as long as $t$ stays below the bulk hopping
strength. Intuitively, for $t \ll 1$ we weakly couple an isolated zero-energy level to a gapped bulk,
and a weak coupling cannot push a level all the way across a finite gap. In a more exact way, this is
the deformation argument of Section 3.7. The Hamiltonians at different small $t$ are topologically
equivalent, since they are connected without closing the bulk gap, so the invariant that protects the
zero mode at $t=0$ also protects it when the coupling is finite.

We can make this fully precise with the Bloch Hamiltonian. With intracell hopping $t$ and intercell
hopping normalized to $1$, the Hamiltonian in momentum space in the $(A,B)$ basis is the $2\times 2$
matrix

\begin{equation}
H(k) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & d(k) \\ d^*(k) & 0 \end{pmatrix}
= (t + \cos k)\,\sigma_x + \sin k\,\sigma_y, \qquad d(k) = t + e^{ik},
\end{equation}

with eigenvalues $E_\pm(k) = \pm|d(k)| = \pm\sqrt{1 + t^2 + 2t\cos k}$. The gap closes exactly when
$d(k)=0$, which requires $t=1$ and $k=\pi$. This is the topological phase transition point, and it
agrees with the qualitative separation between $t<1$ and $t>1$ above, since at $t=0$ the chain is in
the limit where the end sites are fully dangling, which is topological, and at $t=1$ it becomes the
fully symmetric gapless chain.

The topological invariant here is the one-dimensional Berry phase that the lower-band Bloch state
$|u_-(k)\rangle$ accumulates as $k$ goes once around the Brillouin zone, which is called the
\textbf{Zak phase},

\begin{equation}
\gamma = i \oint_{\text{BZ}} \langle u_-(k)|\partial_k u_-(k)\rangle\, dk \pmod{2\pi}.
\end{equation}

Geometrically, $\gamma/\pi$ counts how many times $d(k)$ winds around the origin as $k$ goes from $0$
to $2\pi$. From (3.5), $d(k) = t + e^{ik}$ draws a unit circle centered at $(t,0)$ in the complex
plane. This circle encloses the origin exactly when $|t|<1$, and it does not enclose it when $|t|>1$.
This agrees with everything above, since $\gamma = \pm\pi$ for $t<1$, which means one winding, a
topological insulator and gapless edge modes, and $\gamma = 0$ for $t>1$, which means no winding, a
trivial insulator and no edge modes. The value of the Zak phase is in exact one-to-one correspondence
with the presence or absence of a zero-energy edge state, and this is the one-dimensional version of
the bulk-boundary correspondence of Section 3.7. Figure~\ref{fig:s03:winding} shows this winding
together with the bands that it controls, and it shows that the two insulating phases cannot be
distinguished from their spectra alone.

\input{figures/s03f04_winding}

notebook. The notebook \texttt{session3.ipynb} does not have a dedicated section on the SSH chain,
and its numerical exercises start directly from two-dimensional ribbons, which are the quantum Hall

\section{Chern insulators without Landau levels from a gapped Dirac cone}

Now we come back to the question from the beginning of the session. A Chern insulator needs a broken
time-reversal symmetry. In fact, one can show directly from (3.4) that $\sigma_{xy}\equiv 0$ whenever
time-reversal symmetry holds, since time reversal changes the sign of the Berry curvature. However,
nothing in the derivation of the Chern number required a magnetic field or Landau levels. So the
question is if we can obtain a finite Chern number from an ordinary lattice band structure, using only
terms that break time-reversal symmetry and that are entirely inside the lattice itself.

The method is to look for a lattice that has a \textbf{massive Dirac equation} in its low-energy
expansion. Several two-dimensional lattices have massless Dirac points in their band structure, such
as the honeycomb lattice, the kagome lattice and a square lattice with $\pi$-flux, and this is the
same Dirac cone that we derived for graphene in session 2. Close to one such point, the low-energy
Hamiltonian has the form

\begin{equation}
H(k) = k_x\,\sigma_x + \lambda\, k_y\,\sigma_y + m\,\sigma_z,
\end{equation}

with $\lambda = \pm 1$ fixed by the chirality of the lattice at this Dirac point, and $m$ a mass term
that opens a gap, $E_\pm(k) = \pm\sqrt{k_x^2+k_y^2+m^2}$. We compute the Berry curvature of the lower
band and integrate it, with the same procedure as in (3.4) but now over the small region of momentum
space where the curvature is concentrated. This gives the Chern number of this single gapped Dirac
cone,

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{Dirac}} = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(m)\,\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda).
\end{equation}

The half-integer in (3.8) is not an inconsistency. A real lattice always has an even number of Dirac
points related by time-reversal symmetry, and the honeycomb lattice has two, which are usually called
$K$ and $K'$. Only the \emph{sum} of their contributions needs to be an integer.

This is where the choice of the mass term matters, and we will work out the two natural choices
explicitly, because they give qualitatively different physics. At $K$ and $K'$ the chirality
$\lambda$ has opposite sign, since the two points are time-reversal partners. Let us first open the
gap with a staggered sublattice potential, which raises the onsite energy on one honeycomb sublattice
and lowers it on the other. This mass term is an ordinary scalar potential, so it has the \emph{same}
sign $m$ at both $K$ and $K'$. From (3.8), the two Dirac points then give Chern numbers of opposite
sign, $+\tfrac12\,\mathrm{sgn}(m)$ and $-\tfrac12\,\mathrm{sgn}(m)$, which cancel. The result is $C=0$,
which is an ordinary insulator that is topologically trivial, and this is the same kind of gap as in
boron nitride. Now let us instead open the gap with a purely imaginary second-neighbor hopping, which
is called the \textbf{Haldane coupling}. Physically, this term is a pattern of local orbital currents
that circulate inside each hexagon, and it is odd under time reversal. This mass term changes sign
between $K$ and $K'$ \emph{relative to} $\lambda$, so the two Dirac points now give Chern numbers of
the \emph{same} sign, which add and do not cancel. The result is $C = \pm 1$, a real Chern insulator,
built only from lattice hoppings and without any external magnetic field. This was the first model
ever proposed with a finite and quantized Hall conductivity and zero net flux, and it is the mechanism
to open a gap in the Dirac cone of session 2 that we were looking for. Figure~\ref{fig:s03:masses}
shows the two cases side by side, with the mechanism and the counting together. A gap can also be
opened by combining an ordinary magnetization with spin-orbit coupling, which gives the same sign
pattern as the Haldane term and an equivalent quantum anomalous Hall state. Both routes have material
realizations. Physics similar to the Haldane model has been realized in twisted graphene bilayers on
boron nitride, and the route with magnetism and spin-orbit coupling has been realized and measured in
Cr-doped (Bi,Sb)$_2$Te$_3$.

\section{Quantum spin Hall insulators}

A Chern insulator needs broken time-reversal symmetry. The quantum spin Hall insulator is what we
obtain if we keep time-reversal symmetry and still want a topological gapped state. We take two
copies of the massive Dirac construction (3.7), one for each spin, with \textbf{opposite} mass for
opposite spins, $m_\uparrow = -m_\downarrow$. From (3.8), the two spin sectors then have opposite
Chern numbers,

\begin{equation}
C_\uparrow = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{sgn}(m_\uparrow)\,\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda), \qquad
C_\downarrow = -C_\uparrow, \qquad
C = C_\uparrow + C_\downarrow = 0, \qquad
C_s \equiv C_\uparrow - C_\downarrow = \pm 2.
\end{equation}

The total Chern number $C$ vanishes, in agreement with the unbroken time-reversal symmetry, but the
\textbf{spin} Chern number $C_s$ does not vanish, and it is $C_s$ that controls the edge physics. At
each edge, spin-up propagates in one direction and spin-down propagates in the other direction, which
is a \textbf{helical} edge state and not a chiral one.

The natural mechanism that gives this opposite-sign mass is spin-orbit coupling. The relativistic
spin-orbit term has the structure $\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}$, and its $z$-component, $L_zS_z$, acts
like an effective magnetic field that points in one direction for spin-up electrons and in the
opposite direction for spin-down electrons. This is the opposite-sign mass structure that (3.9)
needs, and it appears with no net magnetic field and no net breaking of time-reversal symmetry.
Graphene itself, when we include its intrinsic spin-orbit coupling, which is very small, is
technically a quantum spin Hall insulator through this mechanism. The effect is much larger, and
accessible in experiments, in monolayer 1T$'$-WTe$_2$, where the quantum spin Hall effect survives up
to 100 K.

There is a subtle point that we should state precisely. If spin were perfectly conserved, we could
speak separately of the Chern number for spin-up and the Chern number for spin-down. Real materials
have terms that mix spin, and spin-orbit coupling itself mixes spin to some degree, so one may worry
that these terms could smoothly remove the protection of the helical edge states. They cannot, as
long as time-reversal symmetry survives, and the reason is Kramers' theorem. For spin-1/2 electrons
the time-reversal operator squares to $-1$, which forces an exact twofold degeneracy at every momentum
that is its own time-reversal partner. These are the time-reversal-invariant momenta, which are
$\Gamma$ and $X$ in this setup. This degeneracy exists with or without spin-mixing terms, so the
crossing of the edge states at these momenta cannot be gapped by any perturbation that respects
time-reversal symmetry, but only by one that breaks it. This protection mechanism is different from,
and stronger than, what the picture of two independent Chern insulators would suggest by itself.
Figure~\ref{fig:s03:helical} compares the chiral and helical edge dispersions and shows what a term
that breaks time-reversal symmetry does to the protected crossing.

time-reversal symmetry, since the band structure changes but the gapless crossing at the edge remains.
On the other hand, disorder that breaks time-reversal symmetry opens a real gap in the edge spectrum

\section{Topology beyond electrons}

We built all the tools of this session, which are the Chern number, the Zak phase and the
bulk-boundary correspondence, around electronic band structures. However, none of the underlying
mathematics requires electrons. The same invariants apply to any quantum excitation with a
well-defined dispersion relation. Examples are \textbf{magnons}, which are the spin-wave excitations
of an ordered magnet, and \textbf{spinons}, which are the fractionalized spin excitations of a quantum
magnet. Both allow exactly the same treatment with Berry curvature and Chern number, and we will do it
explicitly for magnon bands in session 6 and for spinon bands in session 8. More generally, the
argument only needed a Hamiltonian, or more generally a matrix that describes how a wave propagates,
so it applies to any system with a dispersion relation. This includes photonic systems, where light
propagates through a periodic medium, and even purely classical mechanical systems, such as coupled
pendula, vibrating lattices and electronic circuits, which have all been shown in tabletop experiments
to have topologically protected modes. In the end, topology is a statement about the internal
structure of the eigenvectors as a function of momentum, and this structure exists wherever there is a
wave equation, quantum or classical.

The main message of this session is the following. Topological systems have protected edge modes, and
these modes are connected to a non-trivial value of a topological invariant that is computed only from
the bulk. This happens in one and in two dimensions, and also in higher dimensions, and it is usually
protected by a specific symmetry. For the Chern number this is translational symmetry and gauge
invariance, and for the quantum spin Hall state it is time-reversal symmetry.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item The Hall conductivity, computed with linear response theory from the wavefunction expansion
  (3.1), becomes an integral of the Berry curvature over the Brillouin zone. For any gapped system it
  is quantized to an integer, the \textbf{Chern number}, so that $\sigma_{xy} = C\,e^2/h$ (3.4).
\item The quantum Hall state realizes this with flat Landau-level bands, and each of them gives Chern
  number $1$. The bulk gap forces the current onto \textbf{chiral} edge states, with one direction per
  edge, which is why they cannot backscatter and why the plateaus are quantized so sharply, as we saw
  in Section 3.6.
\item \textbf{Topology} classifies Hamiltonians by whether their band structures can be continuously
  deformed into each other without closing the gap. The Chern number is the invariant of this
  classification, and an interface between regions with different Chern numbers must have a gapless
  state, which is the general \textbf{bulk-boundary correspondence} of Section 3.7.
\item The \textbf{SSH model} is the minimal one-dimensional realization. It has a $2\times 2$ Bloch
  Hamiltonian (3.5) whose lower-band Zak phase (3.6) is $0$ in the trivial phase or $\pi$ in the
  topological phase, depending on whether the intracell hopping is larger or smaller than the
  intercell hopping, and the topological phase has protected zero-energy modes at the ends of the
  chain.
\item A lattice with a \textbf{massive Dirac equation} (3.7) gives Chern number $\tfrac12\,
  \mathrm{sgn}(m)\,\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda)$ for each Dirac point (3.8). Whether the contributions of
  Dirac points related by time reversal cancel or add depends only on how the mass term transforms
  under time reversal. This is what separates a trivial gap, from a staggered sublattice potential,
  from a real \textbf{Chern insulator}, which is the Haldane model. This is the answer to the opening
  question of the session, which was what happens when we open a gap in the Dirac cone of the
  honeycomb lattice.
\item If we put together two copies of (3.7) with opposite masses, one for each spin, time-reversal
  symmetry is preserved ($C=0$) and the spin Chern number $C_s=\pm2$ stays non-trivial (3.9). This is
  the \textbf{quantum spin Hall insulator}, with helical edge states that are protected by Kramers
  degeneracy and not by a broken symmetry.
\item The same invariants apply beyond electrons, to magnons and spinons in sessions 6 and 8, and also
  beyond quantum mechanics, to any system with a dispersion relation.
\end{itemize}

see the full quantum Hall effect, Landau levels from first principles, and the fractional quantum Hall
state.
