\chapter{Quantum Many-Body Entanglement and Tensor Networks}

Session 6 ran the Hubbard-model magnetism of session 1 through in full numerical depth, built up the
Heisenberg-model strong-coupling limit and Holstein-Primakoff magnons, and applied the topological
machinery of sessions 3 and 4 a third time, now to magnon bands. Along the way it dropped a Kitaev-
interaction aside flagged as important for quantum spin liquids, materials like herbertsmithite,
whose local moments sit on a kagome lattice and refuse to order even at zero temperature, and it
stopped right there, on ``topological magnons,'' without circling back for a closing summary. That
abrupt stop is not a mid-sentence handoff into today; it is simply a session that ran out of material.
But the thread it leaves dangling is exactly where we pick up. Unlike a ferromagnet or an
antiferromagnet, a quantum spin liquid does not break time-reversal symmetry: its spins keep
fluctuating, locally, no matter how cold we make the system, and the ground state ends up being one
enormous entangled object rather than a simple pattern of arrows. That is precisely the kind of
Hamiltonian on which every tool this course has built so far quietly breaks down. Mean-field theory,
our workhorse since session 1, is a search for a symmetry-broken order parameter: a nonzero
magnetization, a nonzero charge imbalance, a nonzero pairing amplitude. A quantum spin liquid has none
of these by definition, so self-consistency has nothing to converge on and simply hands back zero,
telling us nothing at all about the actual ground state underneath. The same failure shows up in a
second family of materials, fractional Chern insulators, where the interactions are again too strong
for any effective single-particle description to capture what is going on.

Today we build the tool that attacks this regime head-on rather than presupposing a broken symmetry:
tensor networks, and specifically matrix product states (MPS). This is mature technology; it already
produces the leading quantitative evidence for spin liquid physics on the triangular and kagome
lattices, the very Hamiltonians session 6 teased. Getting there means first noticing something that
has been sitting underneath the last several sessions without being said explicitly, that fermionic
and spin Hamiltonians are literally the same physics in two languages (the Jordan-Wigner bridge), and
then asking a question that turns out not to be ``how do we diagonalize a huge matrix'' but ``how do we
even \emph{store} a many-body wave function in the first place.'' Entanglement, and a quantity called the
entanglement entropy, is the right lens for answering that question, and it is where the bulk of
today's session lives.

\section{Two regimes of many-body physics}

Let us return to the generic interacting Hamiltonian that has organized the whole course,

\begin{equation}
H = \sum_{ij} t_{ij}\, c_i^\dagger c_j \;+\; \sum_{ijkl} V_{ijkl}\, c_i^\dagger c_j c_k^\dagger c_l.
\end{equation}

The kinetic term is free and exactly solvable; the interaction term is, in general, not. Every session
so far has attacked (7.1) the same way: decouple the quartic term by mean field, replace it with an
effective bilinear Hamiltonian, and read off whatever symmetry the resulting order parameter breaks.
That strategy defines what we can call \textbf{weakly correlated matter}: magnets, superconductors, and
charge density waves are all, in this sense, single-particle physics in disguise, once the right
symmetry-broken background is found.

Today we are explicitly in the opposite regime, \textbf{strongly correlated matter}, where interactions are
strong enough that no effective single-particle Hamiltonian reproduces the ground state, no matter how
we choose the decoupling. Quantum spin liquids and fractional Chern insulators are our two standing
examples, and more generally, getting an accurate phase diagram of a genuinely interacting model runs
into the same wall. In both cases we cannot shortcut the problem with an ansatz for what symmetry
breaks; we need something closer to an exact numerical solution, or at least a controlled approximation
that does not assume symmetry breaking from the outset. That is exactly what tensor networks provide.

\section{Fermions and spins are the same physics: the Jordan-Wigner bridge}

Before building the tool, it is worth noticing something that has been sitting underneath the last
several sessions without being said explicitly. We have used two apparently different families of
many-body Hamiltonian: fermionic Hamiltonians of the form (7.1), with density-density interactions
$V_{ijkl}$, in sessions 1 through 5, and spin Hamiltonians, above all the Heisenberg model $H =
\sum_{ij} J_{ij}\, \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j$, in session 6's magnon discussion and again today
for quantum spin liquids. These are not two different subjects; they are the same physics written in
two different languages, and today is where we make that translation precise.

Look first at a single site. A spinless fermionic site has a two-dimensional local Hilbert space, empty
or full; a spin-1/2 site also has a two-dimensional local Hilbert space, down or up. The obvious guess
is $c_i^\dagger \leftrightarrow S_i^+$ and $c_i \leftrightarrow S_i^-$, and on a single site this guess
is exactly right. It fails, however, the moment we have more than one site, because fermionic
many-body wave functions are antisymmetric under particle exchange while spin many-body wave functions
are symmetric (bosonic): the naive identification gets the relative signs between sites wrong. Fixing
this requires attaching to each site an extra, occupation-dependent phase, a ``string'' running over
every site to its left, so that exchanging two fermions produces exactly the minus sign the spin
operators do not have on their own. This is the \textbf{Jordan-Wigner transformation}:

\begin{equation}
S_i^+ = c_i^\dagger K_i, \qquad S_i^- = c_i K_i, \qquad S_i^z = n_i - \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad
K_i = \exp\!\left(i\pi \sum_{j<i} n_j\right),
\end{equation}

with $n_j = c_j^\dagger c_j$ the fermion number operator on site $j$. The string operator $K_i$ is built
entirely from occupation numbers to the left of site $i$, so it commutes with everything local to sites
$j \ge i$ and simply counts, mod 2, how many fermions sit to the left. One can check directly that
(7.2), built this way, reproduces the full $SU(2)$ commutation algebra of spin-1/2 operators on every
site and between every pair of sites, using only the fermionic anticommutation relations for $c_i,
c_i^\dagger$ plus the string. The slogan the lecture uses for this is exactly right: \emph{spin equals
fermion times string}. Figure~\ref{fig:s07:jwstring} shows what that string actually does: it counts
the fermions sitting to the left of site $i$ and returns their parity, so changing a single occupation
anywhere in that region flips the sign of $S_i^+$.

\input{figures/s07f01_jwstring}

This is the course's one explicit bridge between the two languages we have used throughout: the
fermionic operators of sessions 1 through 5 and the spin operators of session 6 and (as we will see)
session 8 are related by (7.2), not just analogous to each other. It also explains a genuinely
practical choice we make for the rest of today: since interacting fermionic and interacting spin
Hamiltonians are mathematically equivalent, we are free to work entirely in the spin representation
from here on, and we will, because spin wave functions are symmetric and that symmetry is exactly what
makes the entanglement bookkeeping below clean.

\section{The real bottleneck: storing the wave function}

With the spin language settled, let us ask directly why a generic interacting many-body Hamiltonian is
hard. It is tempting to say ``because diagonalizing a huge matrix is slow,'' but that is not quite the
heart of the problem; the heart of the problem is more basic than diagonalization, it is simply
\emph{storage}.

Take the simplest nontrivial case, the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg dimer, $H = \mathbf{S}_0\cdot
\mathbf{S}_1$ on two spin-1/2 sites. Its ground state is the unique singlet,

\begin{equation}
|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle -
|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\right),
\end{equation}

a state that breaks no symmetry (every single-site spin expectation value vanishes) and is, as we will
make precise shortly, maximally entangled. The question of the whole session is how this kind of state
generalizes to a macroscopic system with many sites.

Write a general spin-1/2 wave function in the natural product basis, one up/down label $s_i$ per site:

\begin{equation}
|\Psi\rangle = \sum_{s_1,\dots,s_L} C(s_1,\dots,s_L)\, |s_1,\dots,s_L\rangle, \qquad
s_i \in \{\uparrow,\downarrow\}.
\end{equation}

For $L=2$ there are four basis states, up-up, up-down, down-up, down-down, so four coefficients $C$;
the Hamiltonian is a $4\times4$ matrix. Add one site, $L=3$, and the count doubles to eight. Add
another, $L=4$, and it doubles again to sixteen. In general the dimension of the Hilbert space, and
hence the number of coefficients $C(s_1,\dots,s_L)$ needed to write down the ground state exactly, is
$d = 2^L$: an exponential wall. Even if we never build the full $2^L\times2^L$ Hamiltonian matrix and
only ever generate matrix elements on demand, we still cannot avoid storing the $2^L$ coefficients of
the ground state itself once we have found it. That storage cost, not the cost of diagonalization, is
the actual obstruction, and it is the single most important point of the whole session. Tensor
networks are one answer to this storage question; the lecture also names, in passing, \textbf{neural network
quantum states} as a second, machine-learning-imported answer to the exact same question, but does
not develop that second tool this session. Session 10 returns to neural network quantum states in
depth, from the machine-learning side; today's material is entirely about the first answer, tensor
networks, and specifically matrix product states.

\section{The matrix product state ansatz}

The proposal that fixes this is disarmingly simple. Instead of storing $C(s_1,\dots,s_L)$ as one
enormous array of $2^L$ numbers, write it as a product of matrices, one matrix per possible value of
each local spin:

\begin{equation}
C(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_L) \;\approx\; M^{s_1}_{1}\, M^{s_2}_{2}\, \cdots\, M^{s_L}_{L},
\end{equation}

where each $M^{s_i}_i$ is an $M\times M$ matrix (a vector at the two open ends, for a finite open
chain), $M$ the \textbf{bond dimension}, a free parameter we choose. There are two matrices per site for a
spin-1/2 chain, one for $s_i=\uparrow$ and one for $s_i=\downarrow$, so the total number of parameters
needed to specify the state is proportional to $L\times M^2$: linear in the system size, in stark
contrast to the $2^L$ of the exact representation (7.4). This is the \textbf{matrix product state} (MPS)
ansatz. It is, on its face, just a choice, and a fairly arbitrary-looking one; the two questions we
spend the rest of Sections 7.5 through 7.8 answering are why it is a \emph{good} choice, and what exactly it
is capturing (and failing to capture) about the physical state.

It is worth knowing up front that this is not a toy proposal. Matrix product states currently give the
most accurate available results for two of the paradigmatic hard problems in quantum materials: the
doped two-dimensional Hubbard model with second-neighbor hopping (implicated in unconventional
superconductivity), and the two-dimensional frustrated Heisenberg model on the triangular and kagome
lattices, which is where the strongest numerical evidence for genuine quantum spin liquid physics,
session 6's herbertsmithite teaser, actually comes from. Both examples are shown as labeled figure
panels on slide 15 of the deck (``Solving the 2D Hubbard model at finite doping'' and ``Solving the 2D
Heisenberg model in frustrated lattices''), underscoring that this is squarely a working tool for the
strongly correlated regime, not just a formal device.

\section{Measuring entanglement: the entanglement entropy}

To see why (7.5) works, we need a quantitative handle on how entangled a many-body state actually is.
Split the system into two complementary pieces, subsystem $A$ and subsystem $B$. A state is \textbf{not
entangled} (``a product state'') if it factorizes as $|\Psi\rangle = |\psi_A\rangle \otimes
|\psi_B\rangle$; the state $|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle$, and a product of two independently
$x$-polarized single-site states, are both examples. The singlet (7.3) is the opposite extreme: it
cannot be written as any such product, and is our working example of an \textbf{entangled} state.

To turn ``entangled or not'' into a number, define the full density matrix $\rho = |\Psi\rangle
\langle\Psi|$ and trace out subsystem $B$ to obtain the \textbf{reduced density matrix} of $A$, $\rho_A =
\mathrm{Tr}_B\,\rho$. The \textbf{entanglement entropy} is then the von Neumann entropy of $\rho_A$,

\begin{equation}
S(\rho_A) = -\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\rho_A \log \rho_A\right).
\end{equation}

If $|\Psi\rangle$ is a product state, $\rho_A = |\psi_A\rangle\langle\psi_A|$ is a rank-one projector,
its only nonzero eigenvalue is $1$, and $S=0$ immediately: no entanglement, zero entropy, exactly as we
would want.

Let us check (7.6) explicitly on the singlet (7.3). Writing $\rho = |\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|$ out in
the product basis and tracing over site $1$ (our subsystem $B$) leaves

\begin{equation*}
\rho_A = \tfrac{1}{2}\,|{\uparrow}\rangle\langle{\uparrow}| + \tfrac{1}{2}\,
|{\downarrow}\rangle\langle{\downarrow}|,
\end{equation*}

a maximally mixed single-site density matrix, whose two eigenvalues are both $1/2$. Then

\begin{equation}
S = -\left(\tfrac12\log\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\log\tfrac12\right) = \log 2.
\end{equation}

This is the maximum possible entanglement entropy a single spin-1/2 can carry (a $d$-dimensional local
Hilbert space can carry at most $\log d$), which is exactly the sense in which the singlet is ``maximally
entangled'': every last bit of the local Hilbert space's capacity for entanglement is used.

\section{A solvable warm-up: entanglement entropy of a dimerized chain}

Two exactly solvable models let us see how (7.6)-(7.7) scale with system size, and they are the key to
the whole session, so it is worth working through them carefully.

Take a one-dimensional chain of spin-1/2's, but couple them only in disjoint pairs: an antiferromagnetic
bond between sites $0$ and $1$, another between $2$ and $3$, another between $4$ and $5$, and so on,
with \emph{no} coupling between different pairs,

\begin{equation*}
H = \sum_n J\,\mathbf{S}_{2n}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{2n+1}.
\end{equation*}

This Hamiltonian is trivial to solve exactly, because it is just a sum of independent Heisenberg
dimers, and its ground state is therefore an exact tensor product of singlets, one per dimer bond:

\begin{equation}
|\Psi\rangle = |\text{singlet}\rangle_{01} \otimes |\text{singlet}\rangle_{23} \otimes
|\text{singlet}\rangle_{45} \otimes \cdots.
\end{equation}

Physically this is the minimal model of a gapped quantum spin liquid: the ground state breaks no
symmetry, and it is separated from the lowest triplet excitation by a large gap (the singlet-triplet
splitting on a single dimer), so it is about as far from criticality as a spin model can be.

Now bipartition the chain and ask for the entanglement entropy. Because (7.8) is a tensor product over
disjoint bonds, the reduced density matrix $\rho_A$ factorizes bond by bond: any dimer sitting entirely
inside $A$ (or entirely inside $B$) contributes a pure state to $\rho_A$ and therefore contributes
exactly zero to $S$, by the same argument as Section 7.5's product-state case. The only bonds that
contribute are the ones the bipartition boundary actually cuts, and each cut bond contributes precisely
the single-dimer result (7.7), $\log 2$, since tracing out one half of a singlet always leaves the same
maximally mixed single-site density matrix regardless of what else is going on elsewhere in the chain.
So if the boundary cuts $n_\text{cut}$ bonds, $S = n_\text{cut} \log 2$.

Take a small subsystem $A$ containing just two dimers: the boundary cuts exactly two bonds (one on each
side), so $S = 2\log 2$. Take a much larger subsystem $A$, many dimers wide: the boundary still only
touches two bonds, the ones immediately at its two edges, so $S = 2\log 2$ again, unchanged. \textbf{The
entanglement entropy of this state does not grow with the size of the subsystem}; it is set entirely
by the two points where the one-dimensional boundary sits, however far apart those points are.

\section{Two dimensions and the area law}

Now repeat the same construction one dimension up. Take a two-dimensional lattice and group sites into
disjoint clusters of four (tetramers) instead of pairs, coupled internally but not to each other, so
the ground state is again an exact tensor product, now of tetramer ground states rather than dimer
singlets. The same factorization argument applies: a cluster fully inside $A$ or fully inside $B$
contributes nothing, and only clusters straddling the bipartition boundary contribute to $S$.

The difference is purely geometric. Compare a small bipartition $A$, whose boundary cuts eight bonds,
against a much larger bipartition $B$, whose longer boundary cuts many more. Because the entropy again
accumulates one contribution per cut bond, $S$ is now set by the \emph{length of the boundary}, and in two
dimensions that length genuinely grows as the subsystem grows, unlike the 1D case in Section 7.6 where
the boundary was always exactly two points no matter how large the subsystem got. This is the crucial
contrast: \textbf{in one dimension the entanglement entropy of a gapped ground state saturates}, independent
of subsystem size; \textbf{in two (or more) dimensions it grows with the size of the boundary}, $S_A \sim
L_A \sim \partial A$ and $S_B \sim L_B \sim \partial B$, so $S_B > S_A$ for the larger bipartition.
Figure~\ref{fig:s07:arealaw} puts the two cases side by side, with the cut bonds marked so that the
counting can be done by eye in each.

\input{figures/s07f02_arealaw}

This behavior has a name, the \textbf{area law}: for the ground state of a gapped, local Hamiltonian, the
entanglement entropy of a subsystem scales with the length (in 1D) or area (in 2D and higher) of the
boundary separating it from the rest of the system,

\begin{equation}
S_A \;\sim\; \alpha\, |\partial A|,
\end{equation}

rather than with the \emph{volume} of $A$ itself. This is genuinely restrictive. A uniformly random state
drawn from the full $2^L$-dimensional Hilbert space would typically have $S_A$ close to its maximum
possible value, $|A|\log 2$ for a spin-1/2 subsystem of size $|A|$: a volume law. Gapped ground states
are wildly atypical states in exactly this sense; they occupy only the tiny corner of Hilbert space
satisfying the area law, and that is precisely the corner both dimer and tetramer models above sit in
exactly, and that realistic gapped Hamiltonians sit in approximately.

\section{Why MPS work in 1D, and where they stop working}

The area law is the missing piece that explains why the ansatz (7.5) is a sensible one. Consider an MPS
at a given bond, and use it to build the Schmidt decomposition of the state across a bipartition there:

\begin{equation*}
|\Psi\rangle = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{M} \lambda_\alpha\, |\alpha\rangle_A \otimes |\alpha\rangle_B,
\end{equation*}

with at most $M$ terms, because the internal (virtual) index running through that bond only takes $M$
values. The reduced density matrix $\rho_A = \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha^2 |\alpha\rangle_A
\langle\alpha|_A$ therefore has rank at most $M$, and among all rank-$\le M$ density matrices the
entropy (7.6) is maximized when every eigenvalue is equal, $\lambda_\alpha^2 = 1/M$, giving

\begin{equation}
S \;\le\; \log M.
\end{equation}

An MPS of bond dimension $M$, in other words, can represent entanglement entropy at most $\log M$
across any given bond, growing only logarithmically with $M$. Compare this to (7.9): a 1D gapped ground
state has entanglement entropy that \emph{saturates} at a finite, subsystem-size-independent value, so a
modest, fixed bond dimension is already enough to capture it essentially exactly. That is the precise,
quantitative reason matrix product states are such an excellent ansatz specifically in one dimension:
the area law caps exactly the quantity the bond dimension caps. Geometrically, we can picture the whole
many-body Hilbert space as a large set, of which MPS parameterize only a tiny corner, precisely the
corner of area-law states, reached with a bond dimension that stays modest ($M \sim 10$, $100$) as long
as we stay in the area-law regime, and that would have to blow up ($M \sim 10^{L/40}$ or worse) to reach
a generic, volume-law state. Figure~\ref{fig:s07:ceiling} draws the comparison directly: the ceiling
$\log M$ set by (7.10) against the entropy actually demanded by a gapped, a critical and a generic
state as the subsystem grows.

\input{figures/s07f03_entropyceiling}

The same argument tells us immediately where MPS must fail: whenever the true entanglement entropy
exceeds what any practical bond dimension can supply through (7.10). Concretely, this happens for
long-time dynamics (entanglement grows \emph{linearly} with time after a quench, not logarithmically), for
genuinely higher-dimensional systems (the area law's boundary length itself grows with system size, per
Section 7.7, so $M$ has to grow correspondingly, often exponentially in the subsystem's linear size),
for highly excited states (the area law is a ground-state statement, not a generic eigenstate one), and
for states far from equilibrium generally. Where the physics stays close to a gapped 1D ground state,
though, MPS remains usable well beyond the pure ground-state problem: it extends, at a cost, to time
evolution, excited states, and open quantum systems, in 1D routinely and sometimes in 2D.

\section{Tensor diagrams: notation for networks and operators}

Matrix product states come with a convenient graphical notation that makes both the ansatz and the
algorithms built on it much easier to reason about. A scalar (rank-0 tensor) is drawn as a bare point;
a vector (rank-1) is a point with one dangling leg; a matrix (rank-2) is a point with two legs; a
rank-3 tensor is a point with three legs, and so on for any rank. Each leg represents one index of the
tensor, and joining two legs together between two tensors means summing over that shared index
(contracting it).

An MPS is then simply a horizontal chain of such points, one per site, each with one \emph{physical} leg
dangling downward (the index $s_i$, the actual local spin value) and one or two \emph{virtual} legs
connecting it to its neighbors along the chain, carrying the bond-dimension index that gets summed
over. A \textbf{matrix product operator} (MPO) represents an operator, most importantly the Hamiltonian
itself, in the same spirit: each site now carries \emph{two} physical legs instead of one, corresponding to
the two indices $\sigma_i,\sigma_i'$ of the operator, plus the same virtual legs linking neighboring
sites. Applying an MPO to an MPS (computing $H|\Psi\rangle$, say) is then just gluing the two diagrams
together along the physical legs and contracting: multiplying every pair of matrices that share an
index and summing over it. Done naively, this operation multiplies the bond dimensions of the two
objects together, which would defeat the whole point; in practice one performs a singular value
decomposition on the result and keeps only the dominant singular values, truncating back down to a
manageable bond dimension while discarding the least important part of the Hilbert space being
represented. This truncation step is the practical engine behind essentially every MPS algorithm,
including the ground-state search we turn to next. Figure~\ref{fig:s07:tensors} collects the
notation, the ansatz (7.5) redrawn in it, and this contract-then-truncate step in one place.

\section{Finding the ground state: the variational principle and DMRG}

Having an ansatz is only useful if we can actually optimize it. Treat the MPS matrices $\{M_i^{s_i}\}$
as variational parameters and minimize the Rayleigh quotient

\begin{equation}
E[\Psi] = \frac{\langle \Psi | H | \Psi \rangle}{\langle \Psi | \Psi \rangle},
\end{equation}

equivalently, introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ to enforce normalization, minimize $\langle
\Psi|H|\Psi\rangle - \lambda\left(\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle - 1\right)$ with respect to every matrix in
the network. Diagrammatically, differentiating with respect to one particular tensor in the network
(say the matrix at site $k$) is simply ``erasing'' that tensor from the diagram, since every tensor
appears linearly (once in the bra layer, once in the ket layer) in the full contraction. What remains,
after erasing site $k$'s tensor from both the numerator and denominator diagrams, is exactly an
eigenvalue problem for that single tensor: an effective, reduced Hamiltonian acting on site $k$'s
indices, whose ground eigenvector is the locally optimal replacement for $M_k^{s_k}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:s07:dmrg} shows the erasure and the sweep it generates.

\input{figures/s07f05_dmrg}

This suggests, and is, an iterative algorithm: fix every tensor except site $1$'s, solve the local
eigenvalue problem there, update $M_1^{s_1}$ to the resulting eigenvector; move to site $2$, fix
everything else, solve the local eigenproblem there, update; continue sweeping through the chain, site
by site, until the energy stops decreasing. This sweeping procedure is exactly the \textbf{density matrix
renormalization group (DMRG)} algorithm. Its efficiency comes from precisely the feature we built
(7.11) to exploit: at every step we only ever optimize one matrix at a time, a small, tractable
linear-algebra problem, rather than the full exponentially large ground-state search all at once.

\section{Beyond MPS: MERA and PEPS}

Matrix product states are the simplest member of a much larger family called \textbf{tensor networks};
nothing forces the tensors to sit in a 1D chain, and the very same underlying coefficient tensor $C$
from (7.4) can just as well be redrawn as a 2D grid or an arbitrary graph of connections. Richer
connectivity trades optimization simplicity, MPS is the easiest tensor network to optimize, for a
larger representable class of states. Two architectures are worth naming specifically.

The \textbf{multi-scale entanglement renormalization ansatz (MERA)} organizes its tensors hierarchically
across length scales rather than along a single chain, and this extra structure lets it support more
entanglement than an MPS built from a comparable number of parameters. That matters because the area
law (7.9) is strictly a \emph{gapped}-system statement; exactly at a critical point (a continuous quantum
phase transition, typically described by a conformal field theory in 1+1 dimensions), the ground state
entanglement entropy no longer saturates but instead grows logarithmically with the size of the
subsystem,

\begin{equation}
S(\ell) \;=\; \frac{c}{3}\log \ell \;+\; \text{const.},
\end{equation}

with $c$ the CFT's central charge. This is a mild but genuine violation of the strict area law (7.9),
and it is exactly the kind of mild violation MERA's extra hierarchical structure is built to
accommodate, which is why MERA is the tensor network of choice specifically at criticality.

\textbf{Projected entangled pair states (PEPS)} are the direct 2D generalization of the MPS idea: instead of
tensors coupled in a line, they sit on the nodes of a genuine 2D lattice, coupled to all of their
lattice neighbors. This is a natural and promising architecture for 2D quantum many-body problems, but
it comes at a real cost: there is no analogue of DMRG's clean, single-tensor local eigenvalue problem
for PEPS, because contracting a general 2D tensor network is itself computationally hard (unlike the 1D
contraction that made Section 7.10's local eigenproblem tractable in the first place). PEPS
optimization remains an active, harder problem. Figure~\ref{fig:s07:merapeps} draws both
architectures, and makes visible where each one's extra structure comes from and what it costs.

\section{Closing summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item Strongly correlated matter, quantum spin liquids and fractional Chern insulators among them, is
  exactly the regime where the mean-field machinery of sessions 1 through 6 has no order parameter to
  converge on, per (7.1) and Section 7.1.
\item The \textbf{Jordan-Wigner transformation} (7.2) is an exact mapping between fermionic and spin-1/2
  Hamiltonians, ``spin equals fermion times string''; it is the bridge between the fermionic language of
  sessions 1-5 and the spin language of sessions 6 and 8, and it is why we can freely work in the spin
  representation for the rest of the session.
\item The real obstruction to solving many-body Hamiltonians is \textbf{storage}: a system of $L$ spins needs
  $2^L$ coefficients (7.4) to specify its wave function exactly.
\item The \textbf{matrix product state} ansatz (7.5) replaces those $2^L$ coefficients with $O(LM^2)$
  parameters, trading exactness for a controllable approximation set by the bond dimension $M$.
\item The \textbf{entanglement entropy} (7.6), computed explicitly for the singlet in (7.7), quantifies how
  entangled a state is; exactly solvable dimer and tetramer models show that gapped ground states obey
  an \textbf{area law} (7.9), entropy set by the boundary length rather than the subsystem volume.
\item MPS entanglement is itself bounded, $S \le \log M$ (7.10), which is exactly why MPS is so effective in
  1D (where the area law saturates) and why it needs exponentially large $M$ in higher dimensions or
  away from the ground state.
\item Ground states are found variationally, by minimizing (7.11) one tensor at a time; this sweeping
  procedure is the \textbf{DMRG} algorithm. \textbf{MERA} and \textbf{PEPS} extend the tensor-network idea to
  criticality (via the logarithmic entropy correction (7.12)) and to genuine 2D lattices, respectively,
  at the cost of DMRG's simple local optimization.
\item Neural network quantum states were named once, alongside tensor networks, as a second answer to the
  same storage problem, but were not developed this session; session 10 takes them up properly from the
  machine-learning side, and also returns directly to this session's MPS/MERA/PEPS machinery as
  ready-made architectures for machine-learning classifiers.
\end{itemize}
